Chapter 23: Control and Coordination

Nervous Integration, Reflex Arcs, Central & Peripheral Systems, Endocrine Chemistry & Plant Tropisms

1. Principles of Biological Control and Coordination

In multicellular organisms, life cannot proceed through isolated metabolic reactions. Survival demands that trillions of specialized cells, tissues, and organ systems function in seamless harmony. Coordination is the process through which two or more bodily organs interact, harmonize, and complement the functions of one another to maintain dynamic internal homeostasis.

Fundamental Definitions:

  • Stimulus: Any detectable environmental change (physical, chemical, thermal, mechanical, or radiant) capable of exciting a sensory receptor and eliciting an active physiological or behavioral response.
  • Receptor: Specialized sensory cells or structures (photoreceptors, phonoreceptors, gustatoreceptors, olfactoreceptors, thermoreceptors, tangoreceptors) that detect environmental stimuli and convert them into electrical potential changes.
  • Effector: A responsive muscle fibre (which contracts) or gland (which secretes chemicals) that executes the ultimate physiological response dictated by the central nervous system.
  • Coordination Teamwork: Humans possess two master regulatory coordinating systems working in close partnership:
    1. The Nervous System: High-speed, point-to-point communication mediated through electrochemical nerve impulses along neuron networks.
    2. The Endocrine System: Chemical coordination mediated through ductless gland secretions called hormones released directly into the circulatory bloodstream.
Comparative Parameter Nervous Coordination Hormonal / Endocrine Coordination
Nature of Signal Electrochemical impulses along nerve fibres and neurotransmitters across synaptic gaps. Chemical messenger compounds (proteins, peptides, modified amino acids, or steroids).
Pathway of Transmission Specific, anatomically wired neurons and nerves directly innervating tissues. Circulatory bloodstream; reaches all tissues but acts exclusively on target cells bearing specific receptor proteins.
Speed of Response Extremely rapid (fractions of a millisecond to milliseconds). Relatively slower (seconds, minutes, hours, or months).
Duration of Effect Transient, immediate, and short-lived. Sustained, prolonged, and persistent metabolic or developmental effects.

2. The Structural and Functional Unit: The Neuron

The neuron (nerve cell) is the microscopic structural and functional unit of the nervous system. Neurons are the longest cells in the human body (some extending over a meter from the lumbar spinal cord down to the foot) and are specialized for excitability and conductivity.

Figure 23.1: Detailed Structural Organization of a Multipolar Neuron

Anatomical Structure of a Multipolar Neuron:

  • Cyton (Cell Body / Soma): Contains abundant cytoplasm (neuroplasm), a large spherical central nucleus, mitochondria, Golgi bodies, and granular aggregations of rough endoplasmic reticulum with ribosomes known as Nissl's granules (essential for active protein synthesis).
  • Dendrites: Multiple short, highly branched cytoplasmic projections extending from the soma. Their specialized dendritic tips receive environmental stimuli or chemical signals from preceding neurons, initiating local depolarizing receptor potentials.
  • Axon (Nerve Fibre): A single, long, cylindrical cytoplasmic projection extending from the conical region of the cyton called the axon hillock. The axoplasm is devoid of Nissl's granules. The axon rapidly conducts nerve impulses away from the cell body toward target cells.
  • Myelin Sheath & Neurilemma: In myelinated neurons, the axon is insulated by concentric lipid-rich layers of a fatty substance called myelin (secreted by Schwann cells in the PNS and oligodendrocytes in the CNS). The outermost cytoplasmic nucleated layer of the Schwann cell is termed the neurilemma.
  • Nodes of Ranvier: Regular microscopic interruptions or gaps in the myelin sheath along the length of the axon. At these uninsulated nodal points, axolemmal ion channels generate action potentials, allowing the nerve impulse to leap rapidly from node to node in a process termed saltatory conduction.
  • Axon Terminals (Terminal Bulbs / Telodendria): Swollen bulb-like distal endings of the axon branches that store membrane-bound synaptic vesicles containing chemical signaling molecules called neurotransmitters (such as Acetylcholine and Noradrenaline).
Transmission of Nerve Impulses & The Synapse

Communication between successive neurons occurs across specialized micro-junctions called synapses:

  1. Generation of Electrical Impulse: When an environmental stimulus stimulates a sensory receptor, it triggers a localized chemical change in the dendritic membrane, opening sodium ion channels and generating an electrical wave of depolarization (action potential).
  2. Axonal Conduction: The electrical impulse travels rapidly through the cyton and sweeps along the axon toward the terminal bulbs.
  3. Synaptic Transmission: Two consecutive neurons never physically touch. They are separated by a microscopic fluid-filled gap of approximately $20\text{ to }30\text{ nm}$ called the synaptic cleft.
  4. Release of Neurotransmitters: The arrival of the electrical impulse at the axon terminal stimulates voltage-gated calcium channels, causing synaptic vesicles to fuse with the presynaptic membrane and discharge neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft via exocytosis.
  5. Receptor Activation: The released neurotransmitter diffuses across the narrow cleft and binds specifically to complementary receptor proteins on the postsynaptic dendrite of the adjacent neuron. This binding opens ligand-gated ion channels, initiating a fresh electrical impulse in the next neuron.
  6. Unidirectional Flow: Because neurotransmitter vesicles are stored exclusively in presynaptic axon terminals and receptor proteins are localized exclusively on postsynaptic dendritic membranes, nerve impulses can travel only in one direction across a chemical synapse.
Functional Class of Neuron Origin & Receptor Connection Destination & Function
Sensory Neurons (Afferent) Connected to sensory receptors in sense organs (eyes, ears, skin, tongue, nose). Conduct sensory impulses from periphery toward the Central Nervous System (brain and spinal cord).
Motor Neurons (Efferent) Originate in the Central Nervous System (motor cortex or spinal ventral horn). Conduct motor commands away from CNS toward effector organs (skeletal muscles or glands) to elicit responses.
Association / Relay Neurons (Interneurons) Located entirely within the brain and spinal cord gray matter. Form complex interconnecting networks linking sensory and motor neurons, processing and integrating information.

3. Nerves: Anatomical Cables of Communication

A nerve is a macroscopic cable-like structure formed by thousands of individual nerve fibres (axons) bundled together and wrapped in tough protective connective tissue sheaths (epineurium, perineurium, and endoneurium). It functions identically to a multi-core underground electrical cable, where each individual wire conducts independently without interference due to lipid insulation.

Classification of Nerves:

  • 1. Sensory Nerves: Composed exclusively of sensory nerve fibres. They carry impulses exclusively from peripheral receptors to the CNS. Example: Optic nerve (carrying visual impulses from retina to brain), Auditory nerve (from cochlea to brain).
  • 2. Motor Nerves: Composed exclusively of motor nerve fibres. They carry regulatory impulses exclusively from the CNS to peripheral muscles or glands. Example: Oculomotor nerve (directing movement of eyeball muscles).
  • 3. Mixed Nerves: Contain both sensory and motor nerve fibres bundled together within the same sheath, conducting sensory inputs toward the CNS and motor outputs toward effectors simultaneously. Example: All 31 pairs of human spinal nerves.

4. The Central Nervous System: Structural Anatomy of the Brain

The Central Nervous System (CNS) represents the master processing and command center of the organism. It consists of the brain encased within the skull and the spinal cord enclosed within the vertebral column.

Figure 23.2: Midsagittal Section of the Human Brain Illustrating Functional Regions

Protective Enclosures of the Human Brain:

  1. Bony Cranium: The brain is housed securely inside a rigid skeletal vault called the cranium (brain box) formed by fused cranial bones, shielding delicate neural tissue from external physical trauma.
  2. Cranial Meninges (Three Protective Membranes):
    • Dura Mater: The outermost, tough, non-elastic fibrous membrane adhering closely to the inner cranial bones.
    • Arachnoid: The middle, extremely delicate, web-like serous membrane.
    • Pia Mater: The innermost, microscopic, transparent, and richly vascularized membrane that hugs every fold and fissure of the cerebral cortex.
  3. Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF): A clear, alkaline lymphatic fluid secreted by choroid plexuses that circulates in the subarachnoid space between arachnoid and pia mater, inside the cerebral ventricles, and within the central canal of the spinal cord.
    Key Functions of CSF:
    • Acts as a physical hydraulic shock-absorbing cushion against mechanical concussions.
    • Provides mechanical buoyancy, reducing the effective weight of the 1400-gram brain to approximately 50 grams.
    • Serves as an essential medium for the exchange of respiratory gases, nutrients, and metabolic waste removal between blood and neural parenchyma.

Clinical Note (Meningitis): An acute and potentially life-threatening inflammation of the meninges and cerebrospinal fluid caused by microbial infection (bacterial, viral, fungal, or amoebic). Symptoms include intense headache, high fever, severe neck stiffness (nuchal rigidity), photophobia, irritability, confusion, and seizures.

Functional Architecture of Brain Regions

The human brain is anatomically divided into three primary subdivisions: Forebrain (Prosencephalon), Midbrain (Mesencephalon), and Hindbrain (Rhombencephalon).

5. The Spinal Cord and Reflex Physiology

The spinal cord is a cylindrical bundle of nervous tissue extending downward from the medulla oblongata through the protective bony neural canal of the vertebral column (backbone), spanning from the base of the skull to the lower lumbar vertebrae.

Internal Architecture of the Spinal Cord:

  • Reversed Arrangement of Matter: Unlike the cerebrum, the spinal cord exhibits an inner 'H'-shaped (butterfly-shaped) Gray Matter (containing motor neuron somas and interneurons) surrounded by an outer White Matter (consisting of ascending sensory and descending motor myelinated axon tracts).
  • Central Canal: A microscopic canal running through the center of the spinal cord, filled with protective and nourishing cerebrospinal fluid.
  • Meningeal Protection: Wrapped externally by the same three continuous meninges: Dura mater, Arachnoid, and Pia mater.
  • Primary Functions:
    1. Acts as the primary coordinating integration center for all spinal reflex actions below the neck.
    2. Serves as the high-speed two-way communication superhighway conducting sensory impulses up from the body to the brain and motor commands down from the brain to effectors.

Reflex Actions & The Reflex Arc

A Reflex Action is a rapid, automatic, spontaneous, and involuntary motor response elicited by a specific sensory stimulus without conscious intervention or delay by the cerebrum. The primary biological purpose of reflex actions is instant self-preservation in the face of imminent hazard.

The Anatomical Components of a Reflex Arc:

The structural pathway traversed by nerve impulses during a reflex action is known as the reflex arc, composed of five mandatory components arranged in strict anatomical sequence:

  1. Receptor: Sensory receptor in the sense organ (e.g., thermoreceptors or pain nociceptors in the skin of the hand) that perceives the noxious stimulus (e.g., burning heat).
  2. Sensory (Afferent) Neuron: Converts the physical stimulus into an action potential and conducts the impulse along its dendrite and axon via the dorsal root of the spinal nerve toward the spinal cord.
  3. Integration Center (Interneuron / Relay Neuron): Located within the central gray matter of the spinal cord. It synapses with the sensory neuron, rapidly processes the input, and relays the excitation directly onto the motor neuron. Simultaneously, an ascending collateral branch carries information up to the brain so that conscious perception of pain occurs after the protective response has already initiated.
  4. Motor (Efferent) Neuron: Transmits the motor impulse out through the ventral root of the spinal cord toward the periphery.
  5. Effector Organ: The target skeletal muscle (e.g., the biceps muscle of the arm) which contracts vigorously to jerk the hand safely away from the heat source.

THE REFLEX ARC IMPULSE SEQUENCE

$$\text{Stimulus} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Receptor (Sense Organ)} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Sensory Neuron} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Spinal Cord (Interneuron)} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Motor Neuron} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Effector (Muscle)} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Response}$$

6. The Peripheral Nervous System (PNS)

The Peripheral Nervous System (PNS) encompasses all nerve tissue lying outside the brain and spinal cord, linking the central processor to every sensory receptor, muscle, and visceral organ. It is subdivided into the Somatic Nervous System and the Autonomic Nervous System.

1. Somatic Nervous System (SNS):

Monitors external stimuli and regulates voluntary contraction of skeletal muscles. Consists of two categories of peripheral nerves:

  • Cranial Nerves (12 Pairs): Emerge directly from the brainstem and pass through cranial foramina to innervate the head, sensory structures of the face, neck, and upper thorax (e.g., optic, olfactory, auditory, and vagus nerves).
  • Spinal Nerves (31 Pairs): Arise symmetrically from the spinal cord segments along the vertebral column (8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral, 1 coccygeal). Every spinal nerve is a mixed nerve containing both sensory and motor fibres.

2. Autonomic Nervous System (ANS):

Regulates all automatic, involuntary physiological functions of visceral internal organs (heart muscle, smooth muscles of gastrointestinal tract, urinary bladder, bronchioles, and exocrine glands). The ANS consists of two anatomically and functionally complementary divisions that maintain homeostatic balance:

7. The Human Endocrine System & Chemical Coordination

While the nervous system provides localized, split-second electrical communication, the endocrine system governs long-term metabolic homeostasis, physical growth, cellular differentiation, and reproductive maturation through chemical messengers called hormones.

Characteristics of Hormones:

  • The term hormone originates from the Greek hormaein, meaning "to set in motion" or "to spur on."
  • Hormones are chemical substances synthesized and secreted by ductless glands (endocrine glands) directly into the bloodstream without passing through anatomical ducts.
  • They circulate throughout the general systemic circulation but exert biological actions exclusively upon specific target tissues / target cells equipped with complementary receptor proteins.
  • Hormones are biologically potent in extremely minute (nanomolar or micromolar) concentrations. Both hyposecretion (deficiency) and hypersecretion (excess) lead to severe clinical pathologies.
  • Unlike enzymes, hormones are consumed during their physiological action and undergo enzymatic degradation and renal excretion.
  • Hormonal release is tightly regulated by negative feedback control mechanisms (e.g., rising blood glucose stimulates insulin secretion; insulin lowers glucose, which in turn switches off further insulin release).

8. Plant Coordination: Phytohormones and Tropic Movements

Unlike animals, plants lack nervous systems, sense organs, and specialized contractile muscular tissues. Nevertheless, plants exhibit remarkable coordination and respond to environmental stimuli through chemical coordination mediated by plant hormones (phytohormones) and differential growth movements.

Major Classes of Plant Hormones (Phytohormones):

  1. Auxins (e.g., Indole-3-Acetic Acid - IAA): Synthesized at the growing apices of shoots and roots. Promotes cellular elongation, apical dominance, root initiation in cuttings, and mediates phototropic and geotropic bending.
  2. Gibberellins: Synthesized in young leaves and developing seeds. Induces rapid internodal elongation (bolting), breaks seed dormancy, and promotes parthenocarpy (seedless fruit production).
  3. Cytokinins (e.g., Zeatin): Synthesized in regions of rapid cell division (root apices, shoot tips, developing fruits). Promotes active cytokinesis (cell division), delays leaf senescence, and overcomes apical dominance.
  4. Abscisic Acid (ABA - The Stress Hormone): Growth inhibitor. Synthesized during drought, salinity, and cold stress. Triggers immediate stomatal closure to conserve water, promotes leaf abscission (falling of leaves), and maintains seed dormancy.
  5. Ethylene: A gaseous phytohormone synthesized in ripening fruits and aging tissues. Accelerates commercial fruit ripening, breaks dormancy, and stimulates flower fading and leaf shedding.

Classification of Plant Movements

Plant movements are categorized into two distinct classes based on the directionality of the stimulus:

  • 1. Tropic Movements (Tropisms): Directional growth movements where the direction of bending is determined by the direction of the stimulus:
    • Phototropism: Response to unidirectional light. Shoots show positive phototropism (bend toward light due to auxin accumulation on the shaded side causing differential elongation), while roots exhibit negative phototropism.
    • Geotropism (Gravitropism): Response to Earth's gravitational pull. Primary roots show positive geotropism (grow downward into soil), while main stems show negative geotropism (grow upward away from gravity).
    • Hydrotropism: Directional growth of roots toward moisture and water sources (positive hydrotropism).
    • Chemotropism: Directional growth in response to chemical gradients. Classic Example: The growth of the pollen tube down through the style toward the chemical secretions (calcium-boron inducers) of the ovule micropyle.
    • Thigmotropism: Directional growth in response to physical touch/contact. Tendrils of climbing plants (peas, bitter gourd) coil securely around structural supports.
  • 2. Nastic Movements: Non-directional, reversible turgor-driven responses where the direction of movement is independent of the stimulus direction.
    Example: Thigmonasty (Seismonasty) in the touch-me-not plant (Mimosa pudica). Mechanical touching of leaves causes rapid efflux of potassium and water from the swollen bases of petioles (pulvini), causing sudden loss of turgor pressure and immediate drooping of leaflets.

10. Conceptual Questions & Detailed Model Answers

Question 1: Explain the electrochemical mechanism by which a nerve impulse crosses a chemical synapse. Why is synaptic transmission strictly unidirectional?

Model Answer:

  • When an action potential (electrical impulse) sweeps along the axon and reaches the terminal bulb (bouton), it causes voltage-gated calcium channels to open. Calcium ions ($Ca^{2+}$) rush into the terminal axoplasm.
  • The influx of calcium stimulates synaptic vesicles to translocate, dock, and fuse with the presynaptic membrane, releasing neurotransmitter molecules (such as Acetylcholine) into the narrow fluid-filled gap known as the synaptic cleft via exocytosis.
  • The neurotransmitters diffuse across the cleft and bind specifically to complementary receptor proteins located on the postsynaptic dendrite of the adjacent neuron.
  • This ligand-receptor binding opens chemically-gated sodium channels, initiating depolarization and generating a fresh electrical impulse in the second neuron.
  • Why Unidirectional: Chemical synapses are strictly unidirectional because neurotransmitter vesicles are synthesized and stored exclusively in presynaptic terminal bulbs, whereas matching receptor proteins are situated exclusively on postsynaptic membranes. Impulses can never travel in the reverse direction.

Question 2: Trace the complete anatomical pathway of a reflex arc when a person accidentally steps on a sharp nail. Why does the withdrawal response precede conscious sensation of pain?

Model Answer:

  • 1. Stimulus & Receptor: The sharp nail piercing the skin of the foot stimulates cutaneous pain receptors (nociceptors).
  • 2. Sensory Neuron: Generates an electrical impulse and conducts it through the dorsal root into the spinal cord.
  • 3. Relay Neuron (Interneuron): Within the gray matter of the spinal cord, the sensory axon synapses immediately with an association neuron that diverts the motor command.
  • 4. Motor Neuron: Exits via the ventral root of the spinal cord and carries the motor impulse directly to the leg musculature.
  • 5. Effector & Response: The flexor muscles of the thigh and leg contract instantly, jerking the foot upward away from the nail.
  • Why response precedes pain: The reflex arc is completed at the local level of the spinal cord within a few milliseconds across minimal synaptic delays. A collateral ascending axon simultaneously carries the sensory signal up the spinal tract to the sensory cortex of the cerebrum. Because travel distance to the brain is longer and involves multiple cortical synapses, conscious awareness and vocalization of pain occur only after the leg has already been safely withdrawn.

Question 3: Differentiate between the physiological roles of the Cerebrum, Cerebellum, and Medulla Oblongata. What happens if the medulla oblongata suffers mechanical injury?

Model Answer:

  • Cerebrum: Controls conscious thoughts, intelligence, speech, voluntary motor commands, memory recall, logic, and sensations.
  • Cerebellum: Coordinates and balances muscular contractions; fine-tunes motor timing and preserves physical equilibrium.
  • Medulla Oblongata: Governs vital involuntary visceral processes including heartbeat rhythmicity, respiratory ventilation rate, vasomotor tone, swallowing, and peristalsis.
  • Injury Consequence: Physical damage or compression of the medulla oblongata results in immediate clinical death because the autonomous cardiac and respiratory rhythmicity pacemaking centers cease functioning, terminating breathing and circulation.

Question 4: Why is dietary Iodine essential for human health? Name and explain the disease resulting from its deficiency in adults and infants.

Model Answer:

  • Importance of Iodine: Iodine is an indispensable trace mineral required by the thyroid gland for the chemical synthesis of its metabolic hormones, Thyroxine ($T_4$) and Triiodothyronine ($T_3$). Thyroxine governs basal cellular metabolism, mitochondrial respiration, and tissue growth.
  • Deficiency in Adults (Simple Goitre): When dietary intake of iodine is chronically insufficient, the thyroid cannot synthesize adequate thyroxine. In an attempt to produce hormone, the anterior pituitary secretes excess Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone (TSH), stimulating cellular hyperplasia and resulting in dramatic enlargement of the thyroid gland visible as a prominent swelling in the front of the neck (Goitre).
  • Deficiency in Infants (Cretinism): Congenital hypothyroidism or severe gestational iodine deficiency causes Cretinism, characterized by stunted skeletal growth (disproportionate dwarfism), dry wrinkled skin, enlarged tongue, and profound irreversible mental retardation. Iodized salt iodization programs effectively prevent these conditions.

Question 5: How does the Autonomic Nervous System mediate the body's response to an acute threat? Compare the actions of the Sympathetic and Parasympathetic systems.

Model Answer:

  • During an acute emergency (e.g., encountering a charging animal), the Sympathetic Nervous System is instantaneously activated, triggering a systemic "fight-or-flight" response mediated directly by adrenergic nerves and augmented by adrenal secretion of adrenaline:
    • Accelerates heart rate and elevates blood pressure to supply working muscles.
    • Dilates respiratory bronchioles to facilitate oxygen delivery.
    • Dilates pupils to sharpen distance vision and perception of threat.
    • Directs blood flow away from the skin and digestive viscera toward skeletal muscles, halting digestion and salivation.
  • Once the threat subsides, the Parasympathetic Nervous System (cholinergic division) restores homeostatic baseline in a "rest-and-digest" response:
    • Slows the heart rate down to normal resting rhythm.
    • Constricts bronchioles and pupils back to resting size.
    • Restores gastrointestinal peristalsis, bile flow, and digestive secretions.
  • Thus, the two systems are not opposing enemies; they are dynamic, complementary homeostatic regulators.
