Core Mechanical Properties
  • Metallic Lustre (Activity 3.1): Metals in their pure state possess a brilliant, shining reflective surface. Rubbing with sand paper removes oxide tarnish.
  • Hardness (Activity 3.2): Generally hard, but hardness varies widely. Exception: Alkali metals (Li, Na, K) are so soft they are easily cut with a knife.
  • Malleability (Activity 3.3): Property by which metals can be beaten into ultra-thin sheets with a hammer without shattering. Gold (Au) and Silver (Ag) are the most malleable metals known.
  • Ductility (Activity 3.4): Ability to be drawn into thin, flexible wires. Gold is the most ductile metal — 1 gram of gold can be drawn into a wire of about 2 km length!
Sonorous Property • School Bells

Metals produce a deep, ringing musical acoustic tone when struck hard against a rigid surface. Hence, school and temple bells are manufactured from metals.

Activity 3.5: Testing Thermal Conductivity

An aluminium/copper wire is clamped to a stand. A pin is fixed at the free end using molten wax. The wire is heated near the clamp with a burner.

  • Poor Conductors: Lead (Pb) and Mercury (Hg).

Vardaan Learning Institute Topic 02 — Reaction with Oxygen 09
NCERT 3.2.1 What Happens when Metals are Burnt in Air? — Activity 3.9
$$\mathbf{\text{Metal} + \text{Oxygen} \longrightarrow \text{Metal Oxide}}$$
Combustion Observations Across Metals
  • Sodium & Potassium: React violently; catch fire spontaneously at room temperature. Stored under kerosene oil to prevent accidental ignition.
  • Magnesium: Burns with a dazzling white flame to form white powder $MgO$:
    $$2Mg(s) + O_2(g) \longrightarrow 2MgO(s)$$
  • Copper: Does not burn, but hot surface is coated with a black layer of copper(II) oxide:
    $$2Cu(s) + O_2(g) \longrightarrow 2CuO(s)\quad (\text{Black oxide})$$
  • Iron: Iron rods do not burn on heating, but iron filings burn vigorously when sprinkled in the flame of a burner!
  • Silver & Gold: Do not react with oxygen even at very high temperatures.
Protective Oxide Layers

At ordinary temperatures, surfaces of metals such as Mg, Al, Zn, Pb form a thin, impermeable oxide film. This passive layer shields the interior metal from further oxidation/corrosion.

Soluble Oxides: Alkalis

Most metal oxides are water-insoluble. However, $Na_2O$ and $K_2O$ dissolve in water to form alkalis:

$Na_2O(s) + H_2O(l) \rightarrow 2NaOH(aq)$
$K_2O(s) + H_2O(l) \rightarrow 2KOH(aq)$

Board Favorite Amphoteric Oxides & The Industrial Anodising Process
Definition: Amphoteric Oxides

Metal oxides that show both acidic as well as basic behaviour, reacting with both acids and bases to yield salt and water, are called amphoteric oxides. Prime examples: Al2O3 and ZnO.

Dual Reactions of Aluminium Oxide (Al2O3)

1. Acting as a Base (Reaction with Acid):

$$Al_2O_3 + 6HCl \longrightarrow 2AlCl_3 + 3H_2O$$

2. Acting as an Acid (Reaction with Base):

$$Al_2O_3 + 2NaOH \longrightarrow 2NaAlO_2 + H_2O\quad (\text{Sodium aluminate})$$
NCERT "Do You Know?" — Anodising

Anodising is the process of forming a thick, durable protective layer of aluminium oxide on aluminium articles.

  • A clean aluminium article is made the anode.
  • Electrolyte: Dilute sulphuric acid ($H_2SO_4$).
  • Oxygen gas liberated at the anode reacts with aluminium to produce a thick, non-corrosive oxide film.
  • Can be dyed in attractive vibrant colours for cookware and window frames.

What Happens when Metals React with Water?
From Cold Water to Red-Hot Steam

"Metals react differently with cold water, hot water, and steam — producing either hydroxides or oxides!"

Vardaan Learning Institute Topic 03 — Reaction with Water 13
NCERT 3.2.2 Reactions with Cold & Hot Water — Activity 3.10
$$\text{Metal} + \text{Water} \longrightarrow \text{Metal Oxide} + H_2 \uparrow$$ $$\text{Metal Oxide} + \text{Water} \longrightarrow \text{Metal Hydroxide}$$
1. Potassium & Sodium (Cold Water — Violent)

React vigorously and violently with cold water. The reaction is so strongly exothermic that the liberated hydrogen immediately catches fire:

$$2K(s) + 2H_2O(l) \longrightarrow 2KOH(aq) + H_2(g) + \text{Heat}$$ $$2Na(s) + 2H_2O(l) \longrightarrow 2NaOH(aq) + H_2(g) + \text{Heat}$$

2. Calcium (Cold Water — Less Violent • Metal Floats)

Reaction is less violent; heat evolved is insufficient for $H_2$ to catch fire. Calcium floats because bubbles of hydrogen gas stick to its surface:

$$Ca(s) + 2H_2O(l) \longrightarrow Ca(OH)_2(aq) + H_2(g)$$

3. Magnesium (Reacts only with Hot Water)

Magnesium does NOT react with cold water. It reacts with hot water to form magnesium hydroxide and hydrogen. It also floats due to $H_2$ bubbles sticking to it:

$$Mg(s) + 2H_2O(l) \longrightarrow Mg(OH)_2(aq) + H_2(g)$$

Vardaan Learning Institute Topic 03 — Action of Steam 14
NCERT Fig 3.3 Action of Steam on Metals (Al, Fe, Zn) — Activity 3.10
Important Rule: Steam Produces Oxides (Not Hydroxides!)

Metals like Aluminium, Iron, and Zinc do not react with cold or hot water. They react only with steam to form the metal oxide and hydrogen gas:

$$2Al(s) + 3H_2O(g) \longrightarrow Al_2O_3(s) + 3H_2(g)$$ $$3Fe(s) + 4H_2O(g) \longrightarrow Fe_3O_4(s) + 4H_2(g)$$

Deep Note: With steam, iron forms magnetic black iron oxide (Iron(II,III) oxide, $Fe_3O_4$), not $Fe_2O_3$.

Metals with Zero Reaction

Lead (Pb), Copper (Cu), Silver (Ag), and Gold (Au) do not react with water or steam at all.

Vardaan Learning Institute Topic 04 — Acids & Aqua Regia 17
NCERT 3.2.3 Reaction with Acids — The Vital $HNO_3$ Oxidising Exception
$$\mathbf{\text{Metal} + \text{Dilute Acid} \longrightarrow \text{Salt} + H_2 \uparrow}$$
Reactivity with Dilute HCl (Activity 3.11)

Rate of bubble formation and exothermicity follows the order:
    Mg > Al > Zn > Fe

Copper ($Cu$) shows zero bubbles and no temperature rise — does not react with dilute $HCl$.

Why is H2 NOT evolved with Nitric Acid (HNO3)?

Nitric acid ($HNO_3$) is a powerful oxidising agent. It instantly oxidises the $H_2$ gas produced to water ($H_2O$), and itself gets reduced to nitrogen oxides ($N_2O, NO, NO_2$).

The Only Exceptions: Magnesium (Mg) and Manganese (Mn) react with very dilute $HNO_3$ (approx 1%) to liberate $H_2$ gas!

NCERT "Do You Know?" — Aqua Regia

Aqua Regia (Latin for 'Royal Water') is a freshly prepared corrosive, fuming mixture of:

$$\text{Conc. } HCl : \text{Conc. } HNO_3 = \mathbf{3 : 1}$$

It can dissolve noble metals like Gold (Au) and Platinum (Pt), even though neither acid can do so alone!

NCERT 3.2.4 & 3.2.5 Metal Displacement Reactions & Table 3.2: The Activity Series
Activity 3.12: Displacement Rule

A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its salt solution in aqueous or molten form:

$$\text{Metal A} + \text{Salt Sol. of B} \longrightarrow \text{Salt Sol. of A} + \text{Metal B}$$

Example: Iron nail in blue copper sulphate solution ($CuSO_4$):

$$Fe(s) + CuSO_4(aq)\; [\text{Blue}] \longrightarrow FeSO_4(aq)\; [\text{Pale Green}] + Cu(s)\; [\text{Brown deposit}]$$

Hydrogen Reference Benchmark

Metals positioned above Hydrogen displace $H_2$ from dilute acids and water. Metals below Hydrogen (Cu, Hg, Ag, Au) cannot displace hydrogen.

Vardaan Learning Institute Topic 05 — Ionic Bonding 22
NCERT 3.3 Why Elements React — Table 3.3 Electronic Configurations
The Principle of Chemical Bonding

Noble gases (He, Ne, Ar) have completely filled outermost shells and show little chemical activity. The reactivity of elements is explained as their tendency to attain a completely filled valence shell (stable octet).

  • Metals: Have 1, 2, or 3 electrons in valence shell → tend to lose electrons to form stable positive cations ($M^{n+}$).
  • Non-Metals: Have 5, 6, or 7 valence electrons → tend to gain electrons to form stable negative anions ($X^{n-}$).
Electrovalent (Ionic) Bond Definition

The chemical bond formed by the complete transfer of one or more electrons from a metal atom to a non-metal atom is known as an ionic bond or electrovalent bond.

NCERT Fig 3.5 & 3.6 Electron Transfer Mechanism — Formation of NaCl & MgCl2
Formation of Sodium Chloride (NaCl) — Fig 3.5

$$Na\; (2,8,1) \longrightarrow Na^+\; (2,8) + e^-\quad (\text{Sodium cation})$$ $$Cl\; (2,8,7) + e^- \longrightarrow Cl^-\; (2,8,8)\quad (\text{Chloride anion})$$

Crucial Fact: Sodium chloride does NOT exist as discrete single molecules, but as large 3D aggregates of oppositely charged ions held by intense electrostatic force.

Formation of Magnesium Chloride (MgCl2) — Fig 3.6

$$Mg\; (2,8,2) \longrightarrow Mg^{2+}\; (2,8) + 2e^-\quad (\text{Magnesium cation})$$ $$2Cl\; (2,8,7) + 2e^- \longrightarrow 2Cl^-\; (2,8,8)\quad (\text{Two Chloride anions})$$

Magnesium loses 2 valence electrons; one electron goes to each of the two chlorine atoms.

Vardaan Learning Institute Topic 06 — Ionic Properties 26
NCERT 3.3.1 Physical Properties & High MP/BP — Activity 3.13
1. Physical Nature & Brittleness

Ionic compounds are crystalline solids and are somewhat hard because of the strong electrostatic force of attraction between the positive and negative ions. They are brittle and break into pieces when mechanical shear pressure is applied (like-charged ions align and repel).

2. High Melting & Boiling Points (Table 3.4)

Ionic compounds possess exceptionally high melting and boiling points because a considerable amount of thermal energy is required to break the strong inter-ionic electrostatic attractions.

3. Solubility Behaviour

Electrovalent compounds are generally soluble in polar solvents like water ($H_2O$), but completely insoluble in non-polar organic solvents such as kerosene, petrol, and benzene.

Vardaan Learning Institute Topic 06 — Conduction 27
NCERT Fig 3.8 Conduction of Electricity: Solid vs. Molten/Aqueous State
Solid State — NON-CONDUCTOR (Insulator)

Ionic compounds in the solid state do NOT conduct electricity.

Scientific Reason: Conduction requires mobile charge carriers. In a solid crystal lattice, the ions are held in fixed, rigid equilibrium positions by intense electrostatic attraction. The movement of ions is completely impossible.

Molten / Aqueous State — EXCELLENT CONDUCTOR

Ionic compounds conduct electricity readily in the molten state or when dissolved in water.

Scientific Reason: In the molten state, thermal energy overcomes the electrostatic forces of attraction, enabling ions to dissociate and move freely. In water, polar water molecules solvate the ions, freeing them to migrate toward oppositely charged electrodes when a potential difference is applied.

• At Cathode (-): Cations ($Na^+, Mg^{2+}$) migrate to accept electrons (Reduction).
• At Anode (+): Anions ($Cl^-$) migrate to lose electrons (Oxidation).

Vardaan Learning Institute Topic 07 — Occurrence 30
NCERT 3.4 & 3.4.2 Occurrence of Metals, Minerals, Ores & Gangue
Fundamental NCERT Definitions (Section 3.4)
  • Source of Metals: The earth's crust is the major source of metals. Seawater contains soluble salts such as $NaCl, MgCl_2$, etc.
  • Minerals: The elements or compounds which occur naturally in the earth's crust are known as minerals.
  • Ores: At some places, minerals contain a very high percentage of a particular metal and the metal can be profitably extracted from it. These minerals are called ores.
  • Golden Rule: "All ores are minerals, but all minerals are not ores."
Why are the Ores of Many Metals Oxides?

Oxygen is an exceptionally reactive element and is very abundant on the earth. Hence, metals react over geological time to form stable oxide ores.

3.4.2 Enrichment of Ores (Concentration)

Ores mined from the earth are usually contaminated with large amounts of impurities such as soil, sand, rocky debris, etc., called gangue.

The impurities must be removed from the ore prior to chemical extraction.

Underlying Principle: The processes used for removing gangue are based on the differences between the physical or chemical properties of the gangue and the ore.

Vardaan Learning Institute Topic 07 — Low Reactivity 32
NCERT 3.4.3 Extracting Metals Low in the Activity Series — Heating Alone
Core Principle: Thermal Reduction by Heat Alone

Metals low in the activity series are very unreactive. The oxides of these metals can be reduced to free metals by heating alone without requiring any external reducing agent.

Case 1: Mercury from Cinnabar (HgS)

When cinnabar ($HgS$) is heated strongly in air, it is first converted into mercuric oxide ($HgO$), which on further heating decomposes into liquid mercury:

$$2HgS(s) + 3O_2(g) \xrightarrow{\text{Heat}} 2HgO(s) + 2SO_2(g)$$ $$2HgO(s) \xrightarrow{\text{Heat}} 2Hg(l) + O_2(g)$$
Case 2: Copper from Copper Sulphide (Cu2S)

Copper which is found as $Cu_2S$ (copper glance) in nature can be obtained from its ore by simply heating in air:

$$2Cu_2S + 3O_2(g) \xrightarrow{\text{Heat}} 2Cu_2O(s) + 2SO_2(g)$$ $$2Cu_2O + Cu_2S \xrightarrow{\text{Heat}} 6Cu(s) + SO_2(g)$$

Crucial NCERT Reasoning (Section 3.4.4 & Ex Q12d)

Why convert sulphides and carbonates into oxides before reduction?
It is much easier and thermodynamically more feasible to obtain a metal from its oxide, as compared to its sulphides and carbonates. Therefore, prior to reduction, metal sulphides and carbonates are converted into metal oxides.

Vardaan Learning Institute Topic 08 — Reduction 35
NCERT 3.4.4 Reduction of Metal Oxides — Carbon & Reactive Metal Displacement
1. Reduction using Carbon (Coke)

The metal oxides are reduced to the corresponding metals by using suitable reducing agents such as carbon (coke):

$$ZnO(s) + C(s) \longrightarrow Zn(s) + CO(g)$$

NCERT Note: Obtaining metals from their compounds is also a reduction process.

2. Reduction using Displacement Reactions

Highly reactive metals such as sodium, calcium, and aluminium are used as reducing agents because they can displace metals of lower reactivity from their compounds.

Example — Manganese dioxide with aluminium powder:

$$3MnO_2(s) + 4Al(s) \longrightarrow 3Mn(l) + 2Al_2O_3(s) + \text{Heat}$$

Vardaan Learning Institute Topic 08 — Thermit Reaction 36
NCERT Fig 3.11 The Thermit Reaction — Welding Railway Tracks & Machine Parts
The Famous Thermit Reaction (NCERT 3.4.4)

Displacement reactions using aluminium powder are highly exothermic. The quantity of heat evolved is so immense that the produced metal is obtained in the molten state.

$$\mathbf{Fe_2O_3(s) + 2Al(s) \longrightarrow 2Fe(l) + Al_2O_3(s) + \text{Heat}}$$

Engineering Application: The molten iron ($Fe(l)$) flows directly into fissures to weld together cracked railway tracks and heavy fractured machinery on site!

NCERT In-Text Question: Identify Substances Oxidised & Reduced
  • Substance Oxidised: Aluminium ($Al$) — gains oxygen to become $Al_2O_3$.
  • Substance Reduced: Iron(III) oxide ($Fe_2O_3$) — loses oxygen to yield $Fe$.
  • Reducing Agent: $Al$  •  Oxidising Agent: $Fe_2O_3$.

NCERT 3.4.5 Extracting Metals towards the Top of the Activity Series
Why Can't Carbon Reduce Top Reactive Metals?

Metals high up in the reactivity series (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al) cannot be obtained from their compounds by heating with carbon.

Reason: These metals have a much greater chemical affinity for oxygen than carbon does. Carbon cannot wrest oxygen away from oxides like $Na_2O$ or $Al_2O_3$.

Electrolytic Reduction of Molten Chlorides

These metals are obtained by the electrolysis of their molten chlorides:

  • At Cathode (Negatively charged electrode): Sodium metal is deposited (Reduction):
    $$\mathbf{Na^+ + e^- \longrightarrow Na}$$
  • At Anode (Positively charged electrode): Chlorine gas is liberated (Oxidation):
    $$\mathbf{2Cl^- \longrightarrow Cl_2 + 2e^-}$$
  • Similarly, aluminium is obtained by the electrolytic reduction of aluminium oxide.

The Apparatus Setup (Fig 3.12)
  • Anode (+): Block of impure copper.
  • Cathode (-): Thin strip of pure copper.
  • Electrolyte: Solution of acidified copper sulphate ($CuSO_4$).
Process & Fate of Impurities
  • On passing electric current, pure copper from the impure anode dissolves into the electrolyte:
    $Cu\text{ (anode)} \longrightarrow Cu^{2+} + 2e^-$
  • An equivalent amount of pure copper from the electrolyte deposits onto the cathode:
    $Cu^{2+} + 2e^- \longrightarrow Cu\text{ (pure cathode)}$
  • Soluble Impurities: Go directly into the solution.
  • Insoluble Impurities: Settle down at the bottom beneath the anode as Anode Mud (contains valuable trace noble metals like Au, Ag, Pt).

NCERT 3.5 Corrosion of Metals — Three Classic Case Studies
1. Silver Tarnishing (Black Coating)

Silver articles become black after some time when exposed to air. Silver reacts with trace sulphur compounds in the air to form a black coating of Silver Sulphide ($Ag_2S$).

2. Copper Tarnishing (Green Coating)

Copper reacts slowly with moist carbon dioxide in the air and loses its shiny reddish-brown surface, gaining a protective green coating of Basic Copper Carbonate [$CuCO_3 \cdot Cu(OH)_2$].

3. Iron Rusting (Reddish-Brown Flakes)

Iron when exposed to moist air for a long time acquires a flaky reddish-brown coating called Rust (Hydrated iron(III) oxide, $Fe_2O_3 \cdot xH_2O$).

NCERT 3.5.1 Prevention of Corrosion — Galvanisation & Surface Coatings
Standard Barrier Prevention Methods

Rusting of iron can be prevented by isolating the surface from oxygen and moisture via: painting, oiling, greasing, galvanising, chrome plating, anodising, or making alloys.

Galvanisation — Sacrificial Zinc Protection

Galvanisation: A method of protecting steel and iron from rusting by coating them with a thin protective layer of Zinc ($Zn$).

Board Question: Why is the galvanised article protected even if the zinc coating is scratched or broken?

Reason: Zinc is more electrochemically reactive than iron ($Zn > Fe$). Zinc oxidises preferentially in place of iron (sacrificial protection), thus shielding the iron from rusting even when exposed.

NCERT 3.5.1 Alloying: Improving Metal Properties & Ancient Indian Metallurgy
What is an Alloy?

An alloy is a homogeneous mixture of two or more metals, or a metal and a non-metal. Prepared by melting primary metal and dissolving other elements in definite proportions.

• Pure Iron: Soft, stretches easily when hot. Adding ~0.05% Carbon makes it hard and strong.
• Electrical Conductivity & MP: Lower for alloys than pure constituent metals.

Summary Metals & Non-Metals — Complete Chapter Key Takeaways
Core Pillars of Chapter 03
  • Physical • Exceptions: Hg (liquid), Br (liquid non-metal), Ga/Cs (palm melt), Iodine (lustrous), Diamond (hardest), Graphite (conducts).
  • Oxides: Non-metals → Acidic ($SO_2$); Metals → Basic ($MgO$). Dual amphoteric: $Al_2O_3, ZnO$.
  • Reactions with Water: Cold (K, Na, Ca), Hot (Mg), Steam only ($Al, Fe$). Pb, Cu, Ag, Au: No reaction.
  • Acids: $HNO_3$ oxidises $H_2$ to $H_2O$ (except Mg, Mn with dilute $HNO_3$). Aqua Regia = 3:1 conc. $HCl : HNO_3$.
  • Ionic Bonding: Electron transfer from metal to non-metal ($NaCl, MgCl_2$); high MP/BP, brittle, conducts only in molten/aqueous.
Metallurgy & Corrosion Checklist
  • Roasting: Sulphides heated in excess air ($2ZnS + 3O_2 \to 2ZnO + 2SO_2$).
  • Calcination: Carbonates heated in limited air ($ZnCO_3 \to ZnO + CO_2$).
  • Thermit: $Fe_2O_3 + 2Al \to 2Fe(l) + Al_2O_3 + \text{Heat}$ (molten iron joins railway tracks).
  • Electrolytic Reduction: Molten chlorides ($Na, Mg, Ca$); cathode collects metal.
  • Refining: Anode = impure Cu; Cathode = pure Cu strip; Anode mud collects noble residue.
  • Corrosion: Air + Water mandatory; Galvanisation uses sacrificial Zinc.
