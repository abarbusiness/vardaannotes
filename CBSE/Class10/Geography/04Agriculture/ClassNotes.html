Geography • Chapter 4: Agriculture (Class Notes)

Chapter at a Glance
  • Workforce: Nearly two-thirds ($66\%$) of India's population is engaged in agriculture (Primary economic activity).
  • Core Function: Produces almost all foodgrains consumed domestically, raw materials for agro-based industries (textiles, sugar, rubber, tea, jute), and agricultural exports (tea, coffee, spices) earning foreign exchange.
  • 3 Farming Types: (1) Primitive Subsistence (Slash & burn), (2) Intensive Subsistence (Labour & chemical heavy), (3) Commercial Farming & Plantations.
  • 3 Cropping Seasons: Rabi (Winter: Oct–Jun), Kharif (Monsoon: Jun–Oct), Zaid (Short summer: Mar–Jun).

1. Types of Farming: Quick Comparison

Farming Type Tools & Labour Inputs & Irrigation Core Exam Facts & Regions
Primitive Subsistence ('Slash and Burn') Primitive tools (hoe, dao, digging sticks). Family / community labour. Zero chemical inputs. Purely dependent on monsoon rains and natural soil fertility. Low yield. Forest patch burnt → potash ash enriches soil → shifted when depleted.
• Jhumming: North-East India.
• Pamlou: Manipur | Dipa: Bastar & A&N.
Intensive Subsistence Enormous manual human labour. Heavy population pressure on land. High doses of biochemical inputs (fertilizers, pesticides) and intensive canal/tube-well irrigation. High yield per hectare from small fragmented plots. Driven by the 'Right of Inheritance' (successive land division). Found in UP, Bihar, West Bengal.
Commercial Farming Modern machinery (tractors, combine harvesters). High Yielding Variety (HYV) seeds, synthetic fertilizers, chemical insecticides. Grown for market profit and export.
Exam Example: Rice is commercial in Punjab & Haryana, but subsistence in Odisha.
Plantation Agriculture (Commercial) Capital-intensive, modern management, cheap migrant labour. High capital, processing factories within or near estates. Single cash crop on vast estates. Direct interface of agriculture and industry. Requires well-developed transport network.
• Tea (Assam, North Bengal)
• Coffee (Karnataka)
• Rubber (Kerala)

2. Cropping Seasons in India: Fast Matrix

Season Sowing & Harvest Major Crops Critical Exam Key
Rabi (Winter) Sown: Oct – Dec
Harvest: Apr – Jun		 Wheat, Barley, Peas, Gram, Mustard. Winter precipitation from Western Temperate Cyclones ensures success. Heartland: Punjab, Haryana, Western UP.
Kharif (Monsoon) Sown: Jun – Jul (Monsoon onset)
Harvest: Sep – Oct		 Paddy (Rice), Maize, Jowar, Bajra, Tur (Arhar), Moong, Urad, Cotton, Jute, Groundnut, Soyabean. Requires high warmth and plentiful rainfall. Major in Eastern and Southern coastal deltas.
Zaid (Summer) Sown/Harvest: Mar – Jun (Short summer) Watermelon, Muskmelon, Cucumber, vegetables, fodder crops. Grown in riverbeds and irrigated tracts between Rabi and Kharif. (Note: Sugarcane takes almost a full year).
💡 Exam Distinction: Three Paddy Crops

In Assam, West Bengal, and Odisha, 3 paddy crops are grown per year: Aus (Autumn), Aman (Winter – primary crop), and Boro (Summer).

• Rain: > 100 cm (High humidity)		 Alluvial clayey soil that holds standing water. Flooded plains & deltas. 2nd globally (after China).
States: West Bengal, UP, Punjab, AP, Bihar.

Maize (Food + Fodder) Kharif (Rabi in Bihar) • Temp: 21°C – 27°C
• Moderate rainfall		 Old alluvial soil (Bangar). Boosted by HYV seeds & fertilizers. States: Karnataka, MP, UP, Bihar, AP, Telangana.
Pulses (Protein Source) Rabi & Kharif Dry conditions; requires less moisture. Leguminous crops (fix atmospheric nitrogen to restore soil fertility – except Arhar/Tur). Grown in rotation. Largest producer & consumer in the world.
States: Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Maharashtra, UP.
Sugarcane Tropical & Subtropical Cash Crop • Temp: 21°C – 27°C
• Rain: 75 – 100 cm		 Variety of well-drained soils. Needs exhaustive manual labour from sowing to harvest. Raw material for sugar, gur, khandsari, molasses. 2nd globally (after Brazil).
States: Uttar Pradesh (largest), Maharashtra, Karnataka.
Groundnut Kharif Oilseed Warm climate, sensitive to frost. Accounts for ~50% of major oilseeds produced in India. States: Gujarat (largest), Rajasthan, Tamil Nadu.
Tea Plantation Beverage • Warm & moist frost-free climate
• Rain: Frequent showers evenly distributed throughout year		 Deep, fertile, well-drained loamy soil rich in humus & organic matter. Labour-intensive (processed in garden to preserve aroma). Introduced by British. 2nd largest globally after China.
States: Assam, West Bengal (Darjeeling/Jalpaiguri), Tamil Nadu, Kerala.
Coffee Plantation Beverage Cool to warm humid hill slopes. Renowned for quality. Superior Arabica variety brought from Yemen. Originated on Baba Budan Hills. Strictly confined to Nilgiri Hills in Karnataka (dominant), Kerala, Tamil Nadu.
Rubber Equatorial / Industrial Raw Material • Temp: > 25°C
• Rain: > 200 cm (Moist & humid)		 Heavy rainfall tropical hill tracts. Indispensable for tyres, tubes, conveyor belts. States: Kerala (overwhelming), Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, A&N, Garo hills.
Cotton ('King of Fibres') Kharif Fibre • High temperature
• Light rainfall or irrigation
• 210 frost-free days & bright sunshine		 Drier parts of Black cotton soil (Regur) of Deccan plateau. Takes 6 to 8 months to mature. Original home of cotton. 2nd globally (after China).
States: Maharashtra, Gujarat, MP, Karnataka.
Jute ('Golden Fibre') Fibre Cash Crop High temperature during growth. Well-drained fertile soils of floodplains renewed annually by silt floods. Used for gunny bags, ropes, carpets. Facing severe competition from synthetic nylon. States: West Bengal (Ganga delta heartland), Bihar, Assam, Odisha.

  • Oilseed Area: Covers ~12% of total cropped area.

    • Mangoes: Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, UP, West Bengal.
    • Oranges: Nagpur (Maharashtra) & Cherrapunjee (Meghalaya).

  • Sericulture: The commercial rearing of silkworms for silk fibre production (silkworms feed on green leaves of mulberry).

4. Agricultural Reforms & The Bloodless Revolution

Institutional Reforms
Land Reforms (Phase 1)

• Abolition of Zamindari: Intermediaries removed; tillers became owners.
• Consolidation of Holdings (Chakbandi): Scattered fragmented strips merged.
• First Five Year Plan: Land reform declared main focus.

Technological Reforms
Green & White Revolutions (Phase 2)

• Green Revolution: Package technology (HYV seeds + chemical fertilizers + tubewells).
• White Revolution (Operation Flood): Dairy milk cooperatives to boost rural income.

Government Support
Comprehensive Package (1980s–90s)

• Crop Insurance: Against drought, flood, cyclone, fire, disease.
• KCC & PAIS: Kisan Credit Card & Personal Accident Insurance Scheme.
• MSP: Minimum Support Price to eliminate middleman exploitation.
• Media: Weather bulletins & farming shows on Radio & Television.

Historic Movement
Bhoodan – Gramdan Movement

• Leader: Vinoba Bhave (Gandhi's spiritual heir).
• Bhoodan: Shri Ram Chandra Reddy offered 80 acres to 80 landless at Pochampally (1951).
• Gramdan: Zamindars donating entire villages.
• Known as the Bloodless Revolution.

6. Top 5 Most-Asked Board Questions (Ready Answers)

  1. Why did the 'Right of Inheritance' lead to uneconomical landholdings?
    Answer: The right of inheritance led to successive division of land among generations, reducing farm sizes to tiny, fragmented plots that cannot support modern machinery or yield surplus income.

  2. State the temperature, rainfall, and soil requirements for Rice.
    Answer: High temperature (above $25^\circ\text{C}$), high humidity with annual rainfall above $100\text{ cm}$ (or canal/tubewell irrigation), and alluvial clayey moisture-retaining soil.

  3. Why is tea processed within the tea garden itself?
    Answer: Tea leaves are processed inside the plantation to prevent wilting and to preserve their natural freshness, essential oils, and aromatic flavour.

  4. Why is jute losing its market to synthetic fibres?
    Answer: Jute is losing ground to synthetic packing materials like nylon because synthetics are far cheaper, durable, and lighter than natural jute.

  5. What was the Bhoodan – Gramdan movement, and why is it called the 'Bloodless Revolution'?
    Answer: Initiated by Vinoba Bhave, it was a voluntary socio-economic movement where wealthy landlords voluntarily donated land plots (Bhoodan) or entire villages (Gramdan) to landless farmers without any state coercion, violent uprising, or bloodshed.
