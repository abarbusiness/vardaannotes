7.1 Classification

Fundamental Definitions

1. Alcohols: Organic compounds formed when one or more hydrogen atoms of an aliphatic hydrocarbon are replaced by hydroxyl (–OH) group(s). The –OH group is bonded to an sp³ hybridised carbon atom. Example: $\text{CH}_3\text{OH}$ (Methanol).

2. Phenols: Organic compounds formed when a hydrogen atom in an aromatic ring (benzene ring) is directly replaced by a hydroxyl (–OH) group. The –OH group is bonded to an sp² hybridised carbon of the aromatic ring. Example: $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH}$ (Phenol).

3. Ethers: Organic compounds formed by substituting a hydrogen atom of a hydrocarbon with an alkoxy (–OR) or aryloxy (–OAr) group, represented as $\text{R–O–R}'$, $\text{Ar–O–R}$, or $\text{Ar–O–Ar}$. Example: $\text{CH}_3\text{OCH}_3$ (Methoxymethane).

B. Based on the Hybridisation of Carbon Atom Carrying –OH:

Compounds Containing $sp^3\text{ C–OH}$ Bonds
Primary ($1^\circ$) Alcohol

–OH is attached to a primary carbon atom (bonded to zero or only one carbon atom).

General Formula: $\text{R–CH}_2\text{–OH}$

Example: $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH}$ (Ethanol)

Secondary ($2^\circ$) Alcohol

–OH is attached to a secondary carbon atom (bonded to two other carbon atoms).

General Formula: $\text{R}_2\text{CH–OH}$

Example: $\text{CH}_3\text{–CH(OH)–CH}_3$ (Propan-2-ol)

Tertiary ($3^\circ$) Alcohol

–OH is attached to a tertiary carbon atom (bonded to three other carbon atoms).

General Formula: $\text{R}_3\text{C–OH}$

Example: $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–OH}$ (2-Methylpropan-2-ol)

  • Allylic Alcohols: The –OH group is attached to an $sp^3$ hybridised carbon atom next to an aliphatic carbon-carbon double bond ($\text{C}=\text{C}$):
    • $1^\circ$ Allylic: $\text{CH}_2=\text{CH–CH}_2\text{OH}$ (Prop-2-en-1-ol / Allyl alcohol)
    • $2^\circ$ Allylic: $\text{CH}_2=\text{CH–CH(OH)CH}_3$ (But-3-en-2-ol)
    • $3^\circ$ Allylic: $\text{CH}_2=\text{CH–C(CH}_3)_2\text{OH}$ (2-Methylbut-3-en-2-ol)
  • Benzylic Alcohols: The –OH group is attached to an $sp^3$ hybridised carbon atom next to an aromatic ring:
    • $1^\circ$ Benzylic: $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH}_2\text{OH}$ (Benzyl alcohol / Phenylmethanol)
    • $2^\circ$ Benzylic: $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH(OH)CH}_3$ (1-Phenylethanol)
    • $3^\circ$ Benzylic: $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{C(CH}_3)_2\text{OH}$ (2-Phenylpropan-2-ol)
  • Vinylic Alcohols ($sp^2\text{ C–OH}$): The –OH group is attached directly to a carbon-carbon double bond ($\text{C}=\text{C}$). Example: $\text{CH}_2=\text{CH–OH}$ (Ethenol / Vinyl alcohol). Note: Vinylic alcohols are generally unstable and rapidly tautomerise into their corresponding carbonyl isomers (acetaldehyde).

7.1.3 Classification of Ethers

Simple or Symmetrical Ethers

The two alkyl or aryl groups attached to the central oxygen atom are identical ($\text{R–O–R}$ or $\text{Ar–O–Ar}$).

Examples:

  • $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{–O–C}_2\text{H}_5$ (Diethyl ether / Ethoxyethane)
  • $\text{CH}_3\text{–O–CH}_3$ (Dimethyl ether / Methoxymethane)
  • $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{–O–C}_6\text{H}_5$ (Diphenyl ether / Phenoxybenzene)
Mixed or Unsymmetrical Ethers

The two alkyl or aryl groups attached to the central oxygen atom are different ($\text{R–O–R}'$ or $\text{Ar–O–R}$).

Examples:

  • $\text{CH}_3\text{–O–C}_2\text{H}_5$ (Ethyl methyl ether / Methoxyethane)
  • $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{–O–CH}_3$ (Anisole / Methoxybenzene)
  • $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{–O–C}_2\text{H}_5$ (Phenetole / Ethoxybenzene)

7.2 Nomenclature & Structures

(a) Rules for IUPAC Naming of Alcohols

Standard IUPAC Procedure
  1. Identify the longest continuous carbon chain containing the –OH group as the parent alkane.
  2. Replace the suffix ‘e’ of the parent alkane with ‘ol’ (e.g., Propane $\to$ Propan-1-ol).
  3. Number the chain starting from the end that gives the carbon bearing the –OH group the lowest possible locant.
  4. Substituents and prefixes are assigned locants and arranged alphabetically.
  5. For polyhydric alcohols (compounds with two or more –OH groups), retain the terminal ‘e’ of the alkane name and attach the suffixes ‘diol’, ‘triol’, etc., with numerical locants indicating the positions of the –OH groups (e.g., Ethane-1,2-diol).
  6. For cyclic alcohols, use the prefix ‘cyclo’ and number the ring such that the –OH carbon is automatically designated position C-1.

(b) Nomenclature of Phenols

The simplest monohydroxy derivative of benzene is Phenol ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH}$), which is accepted as both its common and standard IUPAC name. Substituted phenols are named using ortho ($o$-), meta ($m$-), and para ($p$-) in common systems, and numerical locants (1, 2, 3...) in IUPAC nomenclature.

Naming Conventions

IUPAC System: Ethers are named as alkoxyalkanes. The larger alkyl/aryl group forms the parent hydrocarbon chain, while the smaller group with the oxygen atom is treated as an alkoxy substituent (e.g., Methoxyethane).

7.3 Structures of Functional Groups

The oxygen atom in alcohols, phenols, and ethers is in an $sp^3$ hybridised state. However, the exact bond lengths and bond angles differ characteristically due to hybridization differences, lone pair repulsions, steric effects, and resonance delocalization.

Comparative Structural Analysis (High-Yield NCERT Insights)
  • Bond Angles Comparison:
    • In Methanol ($\text{CH}_3\text{OH}$), the $\text{C–O–H}$ bond angle is $108.9^\circ$. This is slightly smaller than the ideal tetrahedral angle ($109^\circ 28'$ or $109.5^\circ$) due to the strong unshared electron pair repulsion (lone pair–lone pair repulsion on the oxygen atom) which pushes the bonding pairs closer.
    • In Phenol ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH}$), the $\text{C–O–H}$ bond angle is $109^\circ$.
    • In Ethers ($\text{CH}_3\text{–O–CH}_3$), the $\text{C–O–C}$ bond angle is $111.7^\circ$. This is significantly larger than the tetrahedral angle due to the repulsive steric interaction between the two bulky alkyl (methyl) groups attached to the central oxygen atom.
  • $\text{C–O}$ Bond Lengths Comparison:
    • $\text{C–O}$ in Methanol = $142\text{ pm}$ ($sp^3\text{ C–O}$ single bond).
    • $\text{C–O}$ in Phenol = $136\text{ pm}$ (significantly shorter than in methanol).
    • $\text{C–O}$ in Dimethyl Ether = $141\text{ pm}$.
  • Why is the $\text{C–O}$ bond in Phenol shorter ($136\text{ pm}$) than in Methanol ($142\text{ pm}$)?
    1. Partial Double Bond Character: Conjugation of the unshared electron pairs of oxygen with the $\pi$-electron system of the aromatic ring imparts partial double bond character to the carbon-oxygen bond.
    2. Hybridisation State of Carbon: In phenol, oxygen is attached to an $sp^2$ hybridised carbon atom of the benzene ring, which has more $s$-character ($33.3\%$) and is more electronegative than the $sp^3$ hybridised carbon ($25\% \text{ s-character}$) of methanol, thereby holding the bond electrons closer.

7.4a Preparation of Alcohols

1. From Alkenes

(i) By Acid-Catalysed Hydration (Markovnikov Addition)

Alkenes react with water in the presence of an acid catalyst (dilute $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) to form alcohols. In unsymmetrical alkenes, addition strictly follows Markovnikov's Rule (the $-\text{OH}$ group adds to the carbon atom with fewer hydrogen atoms).

Complete 3-Step Reaction Mechanism (Mandatory Board Topic):

  1. Step 1: Protonation of alkene to form a carbocation (Electrophilic attack by $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$): $$\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{H}^+ \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_3\text{O}^+$$ $$\text{CH}_3\text{–CH}=\text{CH}_2 + \text{H–O}^+\text{H}_2 \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{–}\overset{+}{\text{C}}\text{H–CH}_3\text{ (more stable } 2^\circ \text{ carbocation)} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
  2. Step 2: Nucleophilic attack of water on carbocation: $$\text{CH}_3\text{–}\overset{+}{\text{C}}\text{H–CH}_3 + {:\text{O}}\text{H}_2 \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{–CH}(\overset{+}{\text{O}}\text{H}_2)\text{–CH}_3\text{ (Protonated alcohol)}$$
  3. Step 3: Deprotonation to form alcohol: $$\text{CH}_3\text{–CH}(\overset{+}{\text{O}}\text{H}_2)\text{–CH}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{–CH(OH)–CH}_3 + \text{H}_3\text{O}^+$$
(ii) By Hydroboration–Oxidation (Anti-Markovnikov Product)

Diborane ($(\text{BH}_3)_2$ or $\text{B}_2\text{H}_6$) reacts with alkenes to yield trialkylboranes as addition product. This addition is followed by oxidation with hydrogen peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$) in the presence of aqueous sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$) to yield alcohols.

Net Result: Addition of water across the double bond opposite to Markovnikov's rule (Anti-Markovnikov addition, yielding predominantly primary alcohols in high yield).

💡
Key Distinction: Hydration vs Hydroboration-Oxidation • Acid-Catalysed Hydration $\to$ Markovnikov addition $\to$ Secondary/Tertiary alcohol (with risk of carbocation rearrangement).
• Hydroboration–Oxidation $\to$ Anti-Markovnikov addition $\to$ Primary alcohol (Syn-addition, stereospecific, no carbocation formed $\implies$ NO rearrangement).

2. From Carbonyl Compounds

(i) Reduction of Aldehydes & Ketones

Aldehydes are reduced to primary ($1^\circ$) alcohols, whereas ketones are reduced to secondary ($2^\circ$) alcohols.

Reducing Agents:

  • Catalytic hydrogenation: $\text{H}_2$ in the presence of finely divided $\text{Ni}$, $\text{Pt}$, or $\text{Pd}$.
  • Complex metal hydrides: Sodium borohydride ($\text{NaBH}_4$) in ethanol/water, or Lithium aluminium hydride ($\text{LiAlH}_4$) in dry ether.

$$\text{R–CHO} \xrightarrow{\text{NaBH}_4 / \text{H}^+} \text{R–CH}_2\text{OH} \quad (1^\circ)$$

$$\text{R–CO–R}' \xrightarrow{\text{NaBH}_4 / \text{H}^+} \text{R–CH(OH)–R}' \quad (2^\circ)$$

(ii) Reduction of Carboxylic Acids & Esters

Carboxylic acids are reduced to primary alcohols in excellent yield by $\text{LiAlH}_4$ (a strong reducing agent):

$$\text{RCOOH} \xrightarrow{(i)\ \text{LiAlH}_4,\ (ii)\ \text{H}_2\text{O}} \text{R–CH}_2\text{OH}$$

Commercial Production Route: Since $\text{LiAlH}_4$ is an expensive reagent, commercial reduction of acids is achieved by first converting the acid into an ester with an alcohol, followed by catalytic hydrogenation ($\text{H}_2/\text{cat}$):

$$\text{RCOOH} \xrightarrow{\text{R}'\text{OH},\ \text{H}^+} \text{RCOOR}' \xrightarrow{\text{H}_2\ /\ \text{catalyst}} \text{RCH}_2\text{OH} + \text{R}'\text{OH}$$

3. From Grignard Reagents ($\text{R–Mg–X}$) — Master Synthetic Route

Grignard reagents react with carbonyl compounds (methanal, other aldehydes, and ketones) via nucleophilic addition to form an intermediate magnesium alkoxy complex (adduct), which on acid hydrolysis yields alcohols.

Starting Carbonyl Compound Reaction with Grignard Reagent ($\text{R}'\text{MgX}$) Type of Alcohol Formed
Methanal (Formaldehyde, $\text{HCHO}$) $\text{HCHO} + \text{R}'\text{MgX} \to \text{R}'\text{CH}_2\text{OMgX} \xrightarrow{\text{H}_2\text{O}} \text{R}'\text{CH}_2\text{OH} + \text{Mg(OH)X}$ Primary ($1^\circ$) Alcohol
Other Aldehydes ($\text{RCHO}$, e.g. Ethanal) $\text{RCHO} + \text{R}'\text{MgX} \to \text{R–CH(OMgX)–R}' \xrightarrow{\text{H}_2\text{O}} \text{R–CH(OH)–R}' + \text{Mg(OH)X}$ Secondary ($2^\circ$) Alcohol
Ketones ($\text{RCOR}'$, e.g. Acetone) $\text{RCOR}' + \text{R}''\text{MgX} \to \text{R–C(OMgX)(R}')\text{R}'' \xrightarrow{\text{H}_2\text{O}} \text{R–C(OH)(R}')\text{R}'' + \text{Mg(OH)X}$ Tertiary ($3^\circ$) Alcohol

7.4b Preparation of Phenols

Phenol (also historically called carbolic acid) was first isolated from coal tar in the early 19th century. In the laboratory and industry, phenols are prepared by four standard routes:

1. From Haloarenes (Dow's Process)

Chlorobenzene is fused with aqueous sodium hydroxide at high temperature and pressure ($623\text{ K}$ and $300\text{ atm}$) to produce sodium phenoxide, which upon acidification yields phenol.

2. From Benzenesulphonic Acid

Benzene is sulphonated with oleum ($\text{H}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_7$) to form benzenesulphonic acid. Heating benzenesulphonic acid with molten sodium hydroxide at $573\text{ K}$ converts it to sodium phenoxide, which upon acidification yields phenol.

$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_6 \xrightarrow{\text{Oleum}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{SO}_3\text{H} \xrightarrow{(i)\ \text{NaOH (molten), } 573\text{ K},\ (ii)\ \text{H}^+} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH}$$

3. From Diazonium Salts

An aromatic primary amine (aniline) is treated with nitrous acid ($\text{NaNO}_2 + \text{HCl}$) at low temperatures ($273–278\text{ K}$) to form benzene diazonium chloride. Warming the diazonium salt solution with water or treating with dilute acids hydrolyses it to phenol with evolution of nitrogen gas.

$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NH}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{NaNO}_2 + \text{HCl},\ 273–278\text{ K}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2^+\text{Cl}^- \xrightarrow{\text{Warm with } \text{H}_2\text{O}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH} + \text{N}_2\uparrow + \text{HCl}$$

4. From Cumene — Industrial Method (Most Important)

Cumene (isopropylbenzene) is prepared by Friedel-Crafts alkylation of benzene with propene. It is oxidised in the presence of air to cumene hydroperoxide. This intermediate is converted to phenol and acetone by treating it with dilute acid. Acetone is obtained as an economically valuable by-product in equimolar amounts.

7.4c Physical Properties of Alcohols and Phenols

1. Boiling Points Trends

Explanation of High Boiling Points

The boiling points of alcohols and phenols are much higher than those of other classes of compounds, namely hydrocarbons, ethers, and haloalkanes of comparable molecular masses.

Reason: In alcohols and phenols, the presence of the highly electronegative oxygen atom bonded to hydrogen enables them to form strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds ($\text{–O–H}\cdots\text{O–H–}$). Overcoming these attractive intermolecular forces requires a large input of thermal energy.

Key Boiling Point Trends in Alcohols:

  • Effect of Carbon Chain Length: Boiling points increase with increasing number of carbon atoms (increase in molecular mass) because of the corresponding increase in van der Waals surface dispersion forces.
  • Effect of Branching: Among isomeric alcohols, the boiling point decreases with an increase in branching of the carbon chain. Branching causes the molecule to assume a more spherical, compact shape, thereby reducing its surface area and weakening van der Waals forces.

2. Solubility in Water

Solubility Principles

Alcohols and phenols are soluble in water because they can form hydrogen bonds with water molecules ($\text{R–O–H}\cdots\text{O–H}_2$).

  • Lower members (methanol, ethanol, propanol) are completely miscible with water in all proportions.
  • Trend: Solubility decreases with increase in the size of the alkyl / aryl (hydrophobic) group, which resists interaction with water molecules. Higher alcohols are insoluble, oily liquids or solids.

7.4d Chemical Reactions of Alcohols

Alcohols are versatile compounds in organic chemistry. They behave as nucleophiles when the $\text{O–H}$ bond breaks, and as electrophiles when the $\text{C–O}$ bond breaks (after protonation of oxygen).

(A) Reactions Involving Cleavage of the O–H Bond

1. Acidity of Alcohols & Reaction with Active Metals

Alcohols react with active metals like Sodium ($\text{Na}$), Potassium ($\text{K}$), and Aluminium ($\text{Al}$) to liberate hydrogen gas and form metal alkoxides. This reaction confirms the acidic nature of alcohols.

$$2\text{R–OH} + 2\text{Na} \to 2\text{R–O}^-\text{Na}^+\text{ (Sodium alkoxide)} + \text{H}_2\uparrow$$ $$6(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–OH} + 2\text{Al} \to 2[(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–O}]_3\text{Al (Aluminium tert-butoxide)} + 3\text{H}_2\uparrow$$

Acidic Strength Order of Alcohols:

$$\mathbf{\text{Primary }(1^\circ) > \text{Secondary }(2^\circ) > \text{Tertiary }(3^\circ)}$$

Reason: Alkyl groups are electron-releasing ($+I$ inductive effect). In tertiary alcohols, three alkyl groups donate electron density to the oxygen atom, increasing the electron density on oxygen. This decreases the polarity of the $\text{O–H}$ bond, making it harder for the proton ($\text{H}^+$) to leave. Hence, tertiary alcohols are the weakest acids.

Alcohols vs. Water Acidity: Alcohols are weaker acids than water (except methanol, which is slightly more acidic than water). The alkoxide ion ($\text{RO}^-$) is a stronger conjugate base than hydroxide ion ($\text{OH}^-$):

$$\text{R–O}^- + \text{H–OH} \to \text{R–OH} + \text{OH}^-$$
2. Esterification Reactions

Alcohols and phenols react with carboxylic acids, acid anhydrides, and acid chlorides to form esters:

Role of Pyridine: In reaction with acid chlorides, a base like pyridine is added to neutralise the $\text{HCl}$ gas formed as by-product, which shifts the equilibrium to the right side.

★ Acetylation of Salicylic Acid $\to$ Synthesis of Aspirin:

Acetylation of salicylic acid with acetic anhydride in the presence of an acid catalyst produces Aspirin (Acetylsalicylic acid), widely used as an analgesic, antipyretic, and anti-inflammatory drug.

(B) Reactions Involving Cleavage of the C–O Bond

1. Reaction with Hydrogen Halides & Lucas Test

Alcohols react with hydrogen halides ($\text{HX}$) to form alkyl halides:

$$\text{R–OH} + \text{HX} \xrightarrow{\text{anhyd. }\text{ZnCl}_2} \text{R–X} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

Reactivity Order of Halogen Acids: $\text{HI} > \text{HBr} > \text{HCl}$

Reactivity Order of Alcohols: $\text{Tertiary }(3^\circ) > \text{Secondary }(2^\circ) > \text{Primary }(1^\circ)$

The Lucas Test (Distinction between $1^\circ, 2^\circ, 3^\circ$ Alcohols):

Lucas Reagent: A mixture of concentrated $\text{HCl}$ and anhydrous $\text{ZnCl}_2$.

  • $3^\circ$ Alcohols: React immediately at room temperature to form insoluble alkyl chloride $\to$ Turbidity appears immediately.
  • $2^\circ$ Alcohols: React slowly at room temperature $\to$ Turbidity appears within 5 minutes.
  • $1^\circ$ Alcohols: Do not react at room temperature $\to$ No turbidity at room temperature; solution remains clear (turbidity appears only upon heating).
2. Dehydration of Alcohols to Alkenes

Alcohols undergo dehydration (elimination of a water molecule) to form alkenes when heated with protic acids such as concentrated $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ or $\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$:

Ease of Dehydration: $\mathbf{\text{Tertiary }(3^\circ) > \text{Secondary }(2^\circ) > \text{Primary }(1^\circ)}$

3-Step Mechanism of Acid-Catalysed Dehydration of Ethanol (NCERT Core):

  1. Step 1 (Fast): Formation of protonated alcohol (ethyl oxonium ion): $$\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + \text{H}^+ \rightleftharpoons \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{–}\overset{+}{\text{O}}\text{H}_2$$
  2. Step 2 (Slow, Rate Determining Step): Formation of carbocation: $$\text{CH}_3\text{–CH}_2\text{–}\overset{+}{\text{O}}\text{H}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{slow}} \text{CH}_3\text{–}\overset{+}{\text{C}}\text{H}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
  3. Step 3 (Fast): Elimination of proton to form ethene: $$\text{CH}_3\text{–}\overset{+}{\text{C}}\text{H}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{fast}} \text{CH}_2=\text{CH}_2 + \text{H}^+$$
3. Oxidation & Dehydrogenation of Alcohols

Oxidation of alcohols involves the cleavage of $\text{O–H}$ and $\text{C–H}$ bonds with the formation of a $\text{C}=\text{O}$ double bond. It is also known as a dehydrogenation reaction.

7.4e Acidity & Reactions of Phenols

1. Why Phenols are Much More Acidic than Alcohols

Phenol Acidity Analysis

Phenol ($\text{p}K_a \approx 10.0$) is approximately $10^6$ times (one million times) more acidic than ethanol ($\text{p}K_a \approx 15.9$).

Reason 1: Hybridisation of Carbon Atom: In phenol, the $-\text{OH}$ group is attached to an $sp^2$ hybridised carbon atom of the benzene ring, which is more electronegative than the $sp^3$ hybridised carbon of alcohols. This draws electron density away from oxygen, increasing the polarity of the $\text{O–H}$ bond and facilitating $\text{H}^+$ release.

Reason 2: Resonance Stabilisation of Phenoxide Ion: In an alkoxide ion ($\text{RO}^-$), the negative charge is strictly localised on the single oxygen atom. In contrast, in the phenoxide ion ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{O}^-$), the negative charge is delocalised across the conjugated aromatic ring over the ortho and para positions across 5 resonance structures.

2. Effect of Substituents on Acidity of Phenols

Substituent Influence on pKa
  • Electron-Withdrawing Groups (EWG: $-\text{NO}_2, -\text{CN}, -\text{CHO}, -\text{COOH}, -\text{X}$): Increase acidity ($\text{p}K_a$ decreases). They stabilise the phenoxide ion by effectively dispersing the negative charge via $-I$ and $-M$ (mesomeric) effects. This effect is especially pronounced when the EWG is present at ortho and para positions because the negative charge is concentrated at these carbons.
  • Electron-Donating Groups (EDG: $-\text{CH}_3, -\text{OCH}_3, -\text{NH}_2$): Decrease acidity ($\text{p}K_a$ increases). Their $+I$ and $+M$ effects donate electron density into the ring, intensifying the negative charge on the phenoxide ion and destabilising it.

3. Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (EAS) of Phenols

The $-\text{OH}$ group attached to the benzene ring is strongly activating and strictly ortho/para directing. The lone pair on oxygen participates in resonance, increasing electron density substantially at the ortho and para positions.

(i) Nitration of Phenol

A. With Dilute $\text{HNO}_3$ at $298\text{ K}$ (Room Temperature): Gives a mixture of ortho-nitrophenol and para-nitrophenol.

Separation: The mixture is easily separated by steam distillation:

  • $o$-Nitrophenol is steam volatile: Due to intramolecular hydrogen bonding (chelation between adjacent $-\text{OH}$ and $-\text{NO}_2$ groups within the same molecule), it cannot form intermolecular bonds with neighbouring molecules $\implies$ lower boiling point and steam volatile.
  • $p$-Nitrophenol is non-steam volatile: Due to intermolecular hydrogen bonding between different molecules, it is associated $\implies$ much higher boiling point, less volatile, and remains in the flask.

B. With Concentrated $\text{HNO}_3$: Phenol is converted directly to 2,4,6-Trinitrophenol (Picric Acid):

$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 3\text{HNO}_3\text{ (conc.)} \to \text{Picric Acid (2,4,6-Trinitrophenol)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
(ii) Halogenation of Phenol

A. In non-polar solvent ($\text{Br}_2$ in $\text{CS}_2$ or $\text{CHCl}_3$ at $273\text{ K}$): Monobromination occurs yielding $p$-bromophenol (Major) and $o$-bromophenol (Minor). Note: No Lewis acid catalyst ($\text{FeBr}_3$) is required because the $-\text{OH}$ group is already exceptionally activating.

B. In aqueous solution (Bromine Water Test): When phenol is treated with bromine water, it undergoes polybromination instantaneously to form a white precipitate of 2,4,6-Tribromophenol. This serves as a qualitative test for phenol.

$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 3\text{Br}_2\text{ (aq)} \to \text{2,4,6-Tribromophenol}\downarrow\text{ (White ppt)} + 3\text{HBr}$$
★ (iii) Kolbe's Reaction (Kolbe–Schmitt Synthesis)

Phenoxide ion generated by treating phenol with $\text{NaOH}$ is even more reactive than phenol towards electrophilic aromatic substitution. It undergoes electrophilic attack with carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$, a weak electrophile) at $400\text{ K}$ under $4–7\text{ atm}$ pressure to yield 2-hydroxybenzoic acid (Salicylic acid) as the major product.

★ (iv) Reimer–Tiemann Reaction

On treating phenol with chloroform ($\text{CHCl}_3$) in the presence of aqueous sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$) at $340\text{ K}$, an aldehyde group ($-\text{CHO}$) is introduced at the ortho position of benzene ring, yielding Salicylaldehyde (2-hydroxybenzaldehyde).

Electrophilic Species: The attacking electrophile is Dichlorocarbene ($:\text{CCl}_2$), formed in situ by $\alpha$-elimination from chloroform: $$\text{CHCl}_3 + \text{OH}^- \rightleftharpoons {^-\text{C}}\text{Cl}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \to {:\text{CCl}}_2\text{ (Dichlorocarbene)} + \text{Cl}^-$$

Exam Note: If carbon tetrachloride ($\text{CCl}_4$) is used instead of $\text{CHCl}_3$, the major product obtained is Salicylic acid!

(v) Reaction with Zinc Dust

Phenol is reduced to benzene when heated with zinc dust:

$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH} + \text{Zn} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{C}_6\text{H}_6\text{ (Benzene)} + \text{ZnO}$$
(vi) Oxidation of Phenol

Oxidation of phenol with chromic acid ($\text{Na}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7 / \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) produces a conjugated diketone called benzo-1,4-quinone:

$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH} \xrightarrow{\text{Na}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7\ /\ \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4} \text{1,4-Benzoquinone}$$

Air Oxidation: In the presence of air, phenols slowly oxidise to dark-coloured mixtures containing quinones.

7.5 Commercially Important Alcohols

Methanol ($\text{CH}_3\text{OH}$) — Wood Spirit

Industrial Synthesis: Produced by catalytic hydrogenation of carbon monoxide at high pressure and temperature over a $\text{ZnO–Cr}_2\text{O}_3$ catalyst:

$$\text{CO} + 2\text{H}_2 \xrightarrow[\text{200–300 atm, 573–673 K}]{\text{ZnO–Cr}_2\text{O}_3} \text{CH}_3\text{OH}$$

Properties & Uses: Colourless liquid (bp $337\text{ K}$). Used as a solvent in paints, varnishes, and for making formaldehyde.

Extreme Toxicity: Ingestion of even small quantities can cause blindness due to destruction of the optic nerve. Large quantities cause death. In the body, methanol is oxidised to methanal ($\text{HCHO}$) and then to methanoic acid ($\text{HCOOH}$).

Antidote: Intravenous infusion of dilute ethanol, which competes with methanol for the alcohol dehydrogenase enzyme.

Ethanol ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$) — Grain Alcohol

Commercial Production via Fermentation: Fermentation of sugars (molasses, sugarcane juice, starch) by enzymes present in yeast:

$$\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{22}\text{O}_{11} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{Invertase}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6\text{ (Glucose)} + \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6\text{ (Fructose)}$$ $$\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \xrightarrow{\text{Zymase}} 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2\text{CO}_2\uparrow$$

Fermentation Limit: Once alcohol concentration reaches $14\%$, the enzyme zymase is poisoned and fermentation ceases.

Important Definitions:

  • Rectified Spirit: $95.5\%$ ethanol $+ 4.5\%$ water (constant boiling azeotropic mixture).
  • Absolute Alcohol: $100\%$ pure ethanol.
  • Denatured Alcohol (Methylated Spirit): Commercial alcohol made unfit for drinking by adding poisonous methanol, $\text{CuSO}_4$ (to give blue colour), and pyridine (foul smell).

7.6a Preparation of Ethers

1. By Dehydration of Alcohols

Alcohols undergo dehydration in the presence of protic acids ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4, \text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$). The formation of the reaction product (alkene or ether) is strictly controlled by the temperature:

Limitations of Alcohol Dehydration for Ethers
  • The method is suitable only for the preparation of symmetrical ethers with primary alkyl groups.
  • The alkyl group should be unhindered and temperature must be maintained strictly at $413\text{ K}$.
  • With secondary and tertiary alcohols, elimination predominates over substitution to give alkenes as major products.
  • Cannot be used to prepare unsymmetrical ethers because a mixture of three different ethers is obtained.

★ 2. Williamson Ether Synthesis — The Master Method

Williamson synthesis is the most versatile laboratory method for the preparation of both symmetrical and unsymmetrical ethers. It involves an $S_N2$ reaction of a primary alkyl halide with a sodium alkoxide or phenoxide:

$$\text{R–X} + \text{R}'\text{–O}^-\text{Na}^+ \xrightarrow{S_N2} \text{R–O–R}' + \text{NaX}$$
★ The Golden Rule & The Tertiary Halide Trap

1. Golden Rule: The alkyl halide ($\text{R–X}$) must ALWAYS be primary ($1^\circ$). The alkoxide ($\text{R}'\text{O}^-\text{Na}^+$) may be primary, secondary, tertiary, or aryl.

2. Synthesis of tert-Butyl Ethyl Ether:

  • Correct Choice: Reaction of sodium tert-butoxide with ethyl bromide ($1^\circ$ halide): $$(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–O}^-\text{Na}^+ + \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{Br} \xrightarrow{S_N2} (\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–O–CH}_2\text{CH}_3\text{ (Ether)} + \text{NaBr}$$
  • Incorrect Choice (The Exam Trap): Reaction of sodium ethoxide with tert-butyl bromide ($3^\circ$ halide): $$\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{O}^-\text{Na}^+ + (\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–Br} \xrightarrow{E2} \text{CH}_3\text{–C(CH}_3)=\text{CH}_2\text{ (2-Methylpropene)} + \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH} + \text{NaBr}$$ Reason: Alkoxides are not only nucleophiles but also strong bases. Tertiary alkyl halides preferentially undergo elimination ($E2$) rather than substitution due to severe steric hindrance, giving alkenes exclusively.

7.6b Physical Properties of Ethers

Core Properties
  • Dipole Moment: The $\text{C–O–C}$ bond angle is non-linear ($111.7^\circ$). The individual polar $\text{C–O}$ bond moments do not cancel each other out $\implies$ Ethers possess a net dipole moment ($\mu \ne 0$) and are weakly polar.
  • Boiling Points: The boiling points of ethers are comparable to those of alkanes of similar molecular mass, but are much lower than isomeric alcohols.
    Reason: Ethers do not have an $\text{O–H}$ bond and therefore cannot form intermolecular hydrogen bonds among themselves.
    Example: Ethoxyethane ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OC}_2\text{H}_5$, MW $74$, bp $307.6\text{ K}$) vs. Butan-1-ol ($\text{C}_4\text{H}_9\text{OH}$, MW $74$, bp $390\text{ K}$) vs. Pentane (MW $72$, bp $309\text{ K}$).
  • Solubility in Water: Ethers have solubility in water comparable to corresponding alcohols of the same molecular mass (e.g., ethoxyethane $7.5\text{ g}/100\text{ mL}$ water, butan-1-ol $9.0\text{ g}/100\text{ mL}$ water).
    Reason: The oxygen atom of ethers can accept hydrogen bonds from water molecules ($\text{R–O–R}\cdots\text{H–O–H}$).

7.6c Chemical Reactions of Ethers

Ethers are the least reactive among oxygenated functional groups because the alkoxy group ($-\text{OR}$) is a poor leaving group. Their primary chemical reaction is the cleavage of the $\text{C–O}$ bond by strong halogen acids.

1. Cleavage of $\text{C–O}$ Bond by Hydrogen Halides ($\text{HX}$)

Ethers react with concentrated $\text{HI}$ or $\text{HBr}$ at high temperatures ($373\text{ K}$). Reactivity order: $\mathbf{\text{HI} > \text{HBr} > \text{HCl}}$.

Rules for Products & Mechanisms

Case 1: Dialkyl Ethers with Primary / Secondary Alkyl Groups ($S_N2$ Mechanism):

Halide ion ($\text{I}^-$) attacks the less hindered (smaller) alkyl group of the protonated ether via $S_N2$ pathway:

$$\text{CH}_3\text{–O–CH}_2\text{CH}_3 + \text{HI} \xrightarrow{373\text{ K}} \text{CH}_3\text{I (Methyl iodide)} + \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH (Ethanol)}$$

If excess $\text{HI}$ is present, the alcohol reacts further to form a second alkyl halide:

$$\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH} + \text{HI} \to \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{I} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

Case 2: Ethers Containing a Tertiary ($3^\circ$) Alkyl Group ($S_N1$ Mechanism):

If one of the alkyl groups is tertiary, the reaction follows an $S_N1$ pathway because the departure of methanol leaves a highly stable $3^\circ$ carbocation, which rapidly combines with $\text{I}^-$ to form a tertiary alkyl iodide:

$$(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–O–CH}_3 + \text{HI} \to (\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–I (tert-Butyl iodide)} + \text{CH}_3\text{OH (Methanol)}$$

Case 3: Alkyl Aryl Ethers (e.g. Anisole / Methoxybenzene):

Cleavage ALWAYS yields Phenol and Alkyl Halide:

$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{–O–CH}_3 + \text{HI} \xrightarrow{373\text{ K}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH (Phenol)} + \text{CH}_3\text{I (Methyl iodide)}$$

Why does cleavage never yield Iodobenzene and Methanol? In alkyl aryl ethers, the $\text{C(aryl)–O}$ bond has partial double bond character due to resonance with the benzene ring and the carbon is $sp^2$ hybridised. This bond is much stronger than the $sp^3\ \text{C(alkyl)–O}$ bond. Hence, the nucleophile $\text{I}^-$ attacks the $sp^3$ methyl carbon via $S_N2$.

2. Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution of Alkyl Aryl Ethers (Anisole)

The alkoxy group ($-\text{OCH}_3$) in anisole is ortho/para directing and activates the benzene ring towards electrophilic aromatic substitution to almost the same extent as $-\text{OH}$.

✏️ Practice Questions & Detailed Solutions

Q1
Arrange the following sets of compounds in order of their increasing boiling points and give concise reasons: (a) Pentan-1-ol, butan-1-ol, butan-2-ol, ethanol, propan-1-ol, methanol. (b) Pentan-1-ol, n-butane, pentanal, ethoxyethane.
(a) Increasing Boiling Point Order:
$$\text{Methanol } (337.5\text{ K}) < \text{Ethanol } (351\text{ K}) < \text{Propan-1-ol } (370\text{ K}) < \text{Butan-2-ol } (373\text{ K}) < \text{Butan-1-ol } (391\text{ K}) < \text{Pentan-1-ol } (411\text{ K})$$ Reasoning:
1. Boiling point increases with increasing molecular mass due to increasing van der Waals dispersion forces (methanol < ethanol < propanol < butanol < pentanol).
2. Among isomeric alcohols, branching decreases boiling point by making the molecule more compact/spherical, reducing its contact surface area (butan-2-ol < butan-1-ol).

(b) Increasing Boiling Point Order:
$$\text{n-Butane } (272.5\text{ K}) < \text{Ethoxyethane } (307.6\text{ K}) < \text{Pentanal } (348\text{ K}) < \text{Pentan-1-ol } (411\text{ K})$$ Reasoning:
• n-Butane has only weak van der Waals forces.
• Ethoxyethane has weak dipole-dipole attractions but lacks intermolecular H-bonds.
• Pentanal has stronger dipole-dipole interactions due to polar $\text{C}=\text{O}$.
• Pentan-1-ol exhibits strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding ($\text{O–H}\cdots\text{O–H}$), giving it the highest boiling point.
Q2
Explain why the C–O bond length in phenol (136 pm) is significantly shorter than in methanol (142 pm).
The $\text{C–O}$ bond length in phenol is shorter due to two primary factors:
1. Partial Double Bond Character: Conjugation of the unshared pair of electrons on oxygen with the $\pi$-electrons of the aromatic ring results in resonance, giving the $\text{C–O}$ bond partial double bond character.
2. Hybridisation Difference: In phenol, the oxygen atom is bonded to an $sp^2$ hybridised carbon atom ($33.3\% \text{ s-character}$), which is more electronegative and has a smaller covalent radius than the $sp^3$ hybridised carbon ($25\% \text{ s-character}$) in methanol.
Q3
Give the chemical equations and mechanism for the acid-catalysed dehydration of ethanol to ethene at 443 K. Why is ethoxyethane formed at 413 K?
Reaction at 443 K: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} \xrightarrow{\text{conc. H}_2\text{SO}_4, 443\text{ K}} \text{CH}_2=\text{CH}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$

Stepwise Mechanism:
• Step 1 (Fast): Protonation of alcohol: $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH} + \text{H}^+ \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{–}\overset{+}{\text{O}}\text{H}_2$
• Step 2 (Slow, RDS): Carbocation formation: $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{–}\overset{+}{\text{O}}\text{H}_2 \to \text{CH}_3\text{–}\overset{+}{\text{C}}\text{H}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$
• Step 3 (Fast): Elimination of $\beta$-proton: $\text{CH}_3\text{–}\overset{+}{\text{C}}\text{H}_2 \to \text{CH}_2=\text{CH}_2 + \text{H}^+$

At 413 K (Lower Temperature): Intermolecular bimolecular nucleophilic substitution ($S_N2$) occurs where an unprotonated ethanol molecule attacks the protonated ethanol molecule, yielding ethoxyethane ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OC}_2\text{H}_5$). At $443\text{ K}$, higher thermal energy favours unimolecular elimination ($E1$) to give ethene.
Q4
Explain why ortho-nitrophenol is more steam-volatile than para-nitrophenol. How does this facilitate their separation?
$o$-Nitrophenol: The $-\text{NO}_2$ and $-\text{OH}$ groups are in adjacent positions, forming an intramolecular hydrogen bond (within the same molecule). This prevents the molecule from associating with neighbouring molecules, resulting in lower boiling point and high volatility in steam.

$p$-Nitrophenol: The groups are far apart and form strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds between different molecules, causing molecular association, higher boiling point, and non-volatility in steam.

Separation: Passing steam through the mixture distils out $o$-nitrophenol as a vapour, while $p$-nitrophenol remains behind in the distillation flask.
Q5
Write the structural formula and IUPAC name of the major product when: (a) Propanone reacts with CH₃MgBr followed by hydrolysis. (b) Phenol is treated with CHCl₃ and aqueous NaOH. (c) Anisole is heated with concentrated HI.
(a) Propanone + $\text{CH}_3\text{MgBr} \xrightarrow{\text{H}_2\text{O}}$:
Product: $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–OH}$ (2-Methylpropan-2-ol / tert-Butyl alcohol, $3^\circ$).

(b) Phenol + $\text{CHCl}_3 + \text{aq. NaOH}$ (Reimer–Tiemann Reaction):
Product: $o\text{-HO–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–CHO}$ (2-Hydroxybenzaldehyde / Salicylaldehyde).

(c) Anisole + concentrated $\text{HI}$ at 373 K:
Product: $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH}$ (Phenol) and $\text{CH}_3\text{I}$ (Iodomethane / Methyl iodide).
Q6
Why does the reaction of tert-butyl bromide with sodium methoxide yield 2-methylpropene instead of tert-butyl methyl ether? Write the correct reaction to prepare tert-butyl methyl ether.
Sodium methoxide ($\text{CH}_3\text{ONa}$) is a strong nucleophile and also a strong base. In tert-butyl bromide ($3^\circ$ alkyl halide), steric hindrance around the tertiary carbon prevents backside nucleophilic attack ($S_N2$). Consequently, elimination ($E2$) predominates, yielding 2-methylpropene:
$$(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–Br} + \text{CH}_3\text{ONa} \to (\text{CH}_3)_2\text{C}=\text{CH}_2 + \text{CH}_3\text{OH} + \text{NaBr}$$
Correct Route to prepare tert-Butyl Methyl Ether: Use a $1^\circ$ alkyl halide (methyl bromide) with a $3^\circ$ alkoxide (sodium tert-butoxide):
$$\text{CH}_3\text{Br} + (\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–O}^-\text{Na}^+ \xrightarrow{S_N2} (\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–O–CH}_3 + \text{NaBr}$$
