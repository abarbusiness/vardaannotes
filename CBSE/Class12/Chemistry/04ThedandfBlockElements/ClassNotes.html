Chapter 4: The d- and f-Block Elements (Master Student Revision Notes)

1. Introduction & IUPAC Definition

CONCEPTd-Block Elements: Groups 3 to 12 in which inner penultimate $(n-1)d$ subshells are progressively filled across $3d, 4d, 5d,$ and $6d$ series. f-Block Elements: Antepenultimate $(n-2)f$ subshells are filled ($4f$ Lanthanoids, $5f$ Actinoids).

Position of d-Block (Groups 3-12) and f-Block Elements in the Periodic Table
EXCEPTION 1: IUPAC DEFINITION & GROUP 12 NON-TYPICAL ELEMENTS
  • IUPAC RULETransition Metal Definition: An element having an incompletely (partially) filled d-subshell ($d^{1-9}$) in its ground state or in any of its common oxidation states.
  • EXCEPTIONGroup 12 Elements ($Zn, Cd, Hg, Cn$): Zinc ($Zn$), Cadmium ($Cd$), Mercury ($Hg$), and Copernicium ($Cn$) have completely filled $d^{10}$ subshells in BOTH ground state ($d^{10}s^2$) and common $+2$ ionic state ($d^{10}$). Thus, IUPAC classifies them as non-typical transition elements.

3. Physical Properties & Periodic Trends Exceptions

3.1 Melting Points & Enthalpies of Atomisation

Transition metals have high melting points and atomisation enthalpies due to strong interatomic metallic bonding plus covalent-like d-d overlaps.

NCERT Fig 4.1 & 4.2: Melting Points & Enthalpies of Atomisation Trends
EXCEPTION 3: MELTING POINT & ATOMISATION DIPS (Mn, Tc, Zn, Hg)
  • DIP ANOMALYAnomalous Dip at Mn ($3d^5 4s^2$) & Tc ($4d^5 5s^2$): Although melting points peak at $d^5$ in the middle ($Cr, Mo, W$), $Mn$ and $Tc$ show anomalous lower melting points because stable $d^5$ binds electrons tightly, weakening interatomic metallic bonding.
  • LIQUID METALMercury ($Hg$): The ONLY liquid metal at room temperature due to extremely weak metallic bonding ($5d^{10} 6s^2$).
  • LOWEST ATOMISATIONZinc ($Zn, 126 \text{ kJ/mol}$): Fully filled $3d^{10} 4s^2$ has no unpaired d-electrons available for interatomic bonding.

3.2 Atomic Radii Trends & Lanthanoid Contraction Exception

NCERT Fig 4.3: Atomic Radii Trends across 3d, 4d, and 5d Series
The Tug-of-War: Atomic Radius along a 3d Period 1. Sc to Cr (Decreases): Nuclear charge ($Z_{eff}$) increases faster than weak d-electron shielding.
2. Mn to Ni (Nearly Constant): Increasing d-electron screening balances increasing nuclear charge.
3. Cu to Zn (Slight Increase Exception): Paired $d^{10}$ electron-electron repulsions overcome nuclear attraction.
Shielding Effectiveness: s > p > d > f (f-orbitals shield poorest)
EXCEPTION 4: LANTHANOID CONTRACTION & IDENTICAL 4d/5d RADII

Normally, moving down a group increases atomic size ($3d < 4d$). However, $5d$ elements have almost IDENTICAL radii to corresponding $4d$ elements!

  • Cause: 14 inner $4f$ electrons filled before $5d$ series exert extremely poor shielding $\rightarrow Z_{eff}$ increases sharply by $+14 \rightarrow$ outer shells pulled inward.
  • Identical Pairs to Memorize:
    • $Zr (4d) = 160 \text{ pm}$ vs $Hf (5d) = 159 \text{ pm}$
    • $Nb (4d) = 146 \text{ pm}$ vs $Ta (5d) = 146 \text{ pm}$
    • $Mo (4d) = 139 \text{ pm}$ vs $W (5d) = 139 \text{ pm}$

3.3 Density & Ionisation Enthalpy Exceptions

Density & Ionisation Anomalies
  • Density Drop at Zn: Density increases steadily from $Sc$ to $Cu$, but drops at $Zn$ ($7.1 \text{ g/cm}^3$) due to its larger atomic radius.
  • Highest Density Metals: Osmium ($Os, 22.59 \text{ g/cm}^3$) and Iridium ($Ir, 22.56 \text{ g/cm}^3$).
  • Second IE ($\Delta_i H_2^\circ$) High Exception: Unusually high for $Cr$ ($3d^5 \rightarrow 3d^4$) and $Cu$ ($3d^{10} \rightarrow 3d^9$).
  • Third IE ($\Delta_i H_3^\circ$) Exceptions: High for $Mn^{2+}$ ($3d^5 \rightarrow 3d^4$) and $Zn^{2+}$ ($3d^{10} \rightarrow 3d^9$). Low for $Fe^{2+}$ ($3d^6 \rightarrow 3d^5$ stable).

EXCEPTION 5: OXIDATION STATE ANOMALIES & GROUP STABILITY TRENDS
  • NO VARIABLE STATEScandium & Zinc: $Sc$ exhibits ONLY $+3$ ($Sc^{3+}: 3d^0$). $Zn$ exhibits ONLY $+2$ ($Zn^{2+}: 3d^{10}$).
  • MAXIMUM STATESManganese: Shows highest number of states ($+2$ to $+7$ in $MnO_4^-$).
  • PARADOX TRENDd-Block vs p-Block Group Trend Exception:

EXCEPTION 8: LMCT COLOR IN KMnO4 & FERROMAGNETISM
  • COLOR EXCEPTIONLMCT Charge Transfer in $KMnO_4$: $MnO_4^-$ has $Mn(VII)$ with $3d^0$ configuration (no unpaired d-electrons). Its intense dark purple color is NOT due to d-d transition, but due to Ligand-to-Metal Charge Transfer (LMCT) ($p$-orbital of $O^{2-} \rightarrow d$-orbital of $Mn^{7+}$).

  • FERROMAGNETISMFerromagnetism: Extreme paramagnetism shown by $Fe, Co, Ni$.

7. The f-Block Elements: Lanthanoids & Actinoids (Comprehensive Theory)

The f-block elements (also known as Inner Transition Elements) consist of two series of 14 elements each, situated at the bottom of the periodic table, where electrons are progressively filled into the inner antepenultimate $(n-2)f$ subshell:

  • Lanthanoids ($4f$ series): 14 elements following Lanthanum ($\text{Ce}_{58}$ to $\text{Lu}_{71}$), where $4f$ subshell is filled.
  • Actinoids ($5f$ series): 14 elements following Actinium ($\text{Th}_{90}$ to $\text{Lr}_{103}$), where $5f$ subshell is filled.

7.1 The Lanthanoids ($4f$ Series) Theory

General Electronic Configuration: $\mathbf{[Xe] 4f^{1-14} 5d^{0-1} 6s^2}$

Lanthanum ($\text{La}, Z=57$) has configuration $[Xe] 5d^1 6s^2$. After Lanthanum, the filling of the $4f$ orbital begins at Cerium ($\text{Ce}, Z=58$) and finishes at Lutetium ($\text{Lu}, Z=71$).

7.2 Atomic/Ionic Radii & Lanthanoid Contraction

NCERT Fig 4.6: Lanthanoid Contraction Curve ($Ln^{3+}$ Radii: $106 \text{ pm} \rightarrow 86 \text{ pm}$)
Definition, Mechanism & Consequences of Lanthanoid Contraction Lanthanoid Contraction Definition: The steady, cumulative decrease in atomic radii ($187\text{ pm} \rightarrow 175\text{ pm}$) and ionic radii ($Ln^{3+}: 106\text{ pm} \rightarrow 86\text{ pm}$) across the series from Lanthanum to Lutetium.

Underlying Cause/Mechanism: As atomic number increases, nuclear charge increases by $+1$ unit at each element. The added electron enters the inner $4f$ subshell. Because $4f$ orbitals have multi-lobed, diffuse shapes and are deeply buried inside the atom, the mutual shielding effect of $4f$ electrons on each other is extremely poor. Consequently, the outermost electrons experience a steadily increasing effective nuclear charge ($Z_{eff}$), pulling the outer shells inward.

Three Crucial Consequences:
  1. Identical Radii of $4d$ and $5d$ Transition Elements: The cumulative size decrease across 14 lanthanoids perfectly balances the size increase expected from adding a new principal energy shell ($4d \rightarrow 5d$). As a result, pairs of elements in the same group of $4d$ and $5d$ series have nearly identical atomic/ionic radii:
    - $\text{Zr } (160\text{ pm}) \approx \text{Hf } (159\text{ pm})$ in Group 4.
    - $\text{Nb } (146\text{ pm}) \approx \text{Ta } (146\text{ pm})$ in Group 5.
    - $\text{Mo } (139\text{ pm}) \approx \text{W } (139\text{ pm})$ in Group 6.
    Effect: $5d$ series elements have nearly double the density of $4d$ series elements!
  2. Separation Difficulty: Because ionic radii and chemical properties are almost identical across lanthanoids, separating them in pure form from natural ores is extremely difficult and requires complex ion-exchange chromatography.
  3. Decrease in Hydroxide Basicity: As $Ln^{3+}$ size decreases from $\text{La}^{3+} (106\text{ pm})$ to $\text{Lu}^{3+} (86\text{ pm})$, charge density increases, enhancing the covalent character of the $Ln-OH$ bond (Fajans' Rule). Thus, $\text{La(OH)}_3$ is the MOST basic hydroxide, while $\text{Lu(OH)}_3$ is the LEAST basic hydroxide.

7.3 Oxidation States & Redox Roles Theory

The $+3$ oxidation state is the most stable and predominant state for all lanthanoids in aqueous solutions and solid compounds.

ANOMALOUS OXIDATION STATES & REDOX AGENTS
  • $+4$ Oxidation States (Oxidising Agents):
    • Cerium ($\text{Ce}^{4+}$): Config $[Xe]4f^0$ (empty $4f$ noble gas configuration). Stable in solid oxides ($\text{CeO}_2$), but in aqueous solution it acts as a strong analytical oxidising agent ($E^\circ(\text{Ce}^{4+}/\text{Ce}^{3+}) = +1.74\text{ V}$) because it readily gains an electron to revert to the favoured $+3$ state.
    • Terbium ($\text{Tb}^{4+}$): Config $[Xe]4f^7$ (half-filled). Strong oxidising agent.
    • Praseodymium ($\text{Pr}^{4+}$) & Neodymium ($\text{Nd}^{4+}$): Exist only in solid fluorides ($\text{MF}_4$) and oxides ($\text{MO}_2$).
  • $+2$ Oxidation States (Reducing Agents):
    • Europium ($\text{Eu}^{2+}$): Config $[Xe]4f^7$ (half-filled). Acts as a strong reducing agent ($\text{Eu}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Eu}^{3+} + e^-$).
    • Ytterbium ($\text{Yb}^{2+}$): Config $[Xe]4f^{14}$ (fully-filled). Acts as a strong reductant.
    • Samarium ($\text{Sm}^{2+}$) & Thulium ($\text{Tm}^{2+}$): Also exhibit $+2$ state and act as reductants ($\text{Sm}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Sm}^{3+}$).

7.4 Physical Properties, Colour & Magnetic Behavior

  • Physical Appearance: Soft, silvery-white metals. Hardness increases with atomic number ($\text{Sm}$ is as hard as steel, melting point $1623\text{ K}$). High melting points ($1000\text{ K}-1200\text{ K}$). Good heat and electrical conductors.
  • Origin of Colour ($f-f$ Transitions): Many trivalent lanthanoid ions ($Ln^{3+}$) are colored in solid state and aqueous solutions due to $f-f$ electronic transitions (absorption of light in visible region).
    - Colour Symmetry Rule: Ions containing $4f^n$ electrons have the SAME color as ions containing $4f^{14-n}$ electrons:
    â€¢ $\text{La}^{3+} (4f^0)$ and $\text{Lu}^{3+} (4f^{14})$ $\rightarrow$ Colorless
    â€¢ $\text{Ce}^{3+} (4f^1)$ and $\text{Yb}^{3+} (4f^{13})$ $\rightarrow$ Colorless
    â€¢ $\text{Pr}^{3+} (4f^2)$ and $\text{Tm}^{3+} (4f^{12})$ $\rightarrow$ Green
    â€¢ $\text{Nd}^{3+} (4f^3)$ and $\text{Er}^{3+} (4f^{11})$ $\rightarrow$ Pink / Pinkish-Purple
    â€¢ $\text{Sm}^{3+} (4f^5)$ and $\text{Dy}^{3+} (4f^9)$ $\rightarrow$ Yellow
  • Paramagnetism & Magnetic Moments: All $Ln^{3+}$ ions except $4f^0$ ($\text{La}^{3+}, \text{Ce}^{4+}$) and $4f^{14}$ ($\text{Yb}^{2+}, \text{Lu}^{3+}$) are paramagnetic. Unlike $3d$ transition metal ions where orbital contribution is quenched, in lanthanoids the orbital angular momentum ($L$) contribution is active, so magnetic moments are calculated using total angular momentum $J = L+S$: $$\mu = g \sqrt{J(J+1)} \text{ Bohr Magnetons (B.M.)}$$

7.5 Chemical Reactivity & Alloys

Earlier members of the series are chemically reactive like Calcium, but with increasing atomic number they behave more like Aluminum.

$5f, 6d, 7s$ Energy Overlap: In early actinoids, the $5f, 6d,$ and $7s$ energy levels are extremely close in energy. Consequently, electrons from all three subshells can participate in chemical bonding, leading to variable oxidation states.

7.7 Actinoid Contraction vs Lanthanoid Contraction Theory

Like lanthanoids, actinoids show a gradual decrease in atomic and ionic radii ($An^{3+}$ and $An^{4+}$) across the series, termed Actinoid Contraction.

CRUCIAL COMPARISON: ACTINOID VS LANTHANOID CONTRACTION Key Distinction: Actinoid contraction is GREATER from element to element than Lanthanoid contraction.

Theoretical Cause: In actinoids, electrons are added to $5f$ subshell, whereas in lanthanoids they enter $4f$. Because $5f$ orbitals extend further out in space beyond the core than $4f$ orbitals, the mutual shielding effect of $5f$ electrons is even poorer than that of $4f$ electrons. Hence, the effective nuclear charge ($Z_{eff}$) increases more sharply across actinoids, producing a larger size contraction per element.

7.8 Oxidation States & Oxo-Cation Formation

Unlike lanthanoids where $+3$ is overwhelmingly predominant, actinoids exhibit a wide range of oxidation states ($+3, +4, +5, +6, +7$):

  • Thorium ($\text{Th}$): $+4$ (predominant)
  • Protactinium ($\text{Pa}$): $+4, +5$
  • Uranium ($\text{U}$): $+3, +4, +5, \mathbf{+6}$ (e.g., $\text{UF}_6, \text{UO}_2^{2+}$)
  • Neptunium ($\text{Np}$) & Plutonium ($\text{Pu}$): $+3, +4, +5, +6, \mathbf{+7}$ (highest states in $\text{NpO}_5^{3-}, \text{PuO}_5^{3-}$)
  • Americium ($\text{Am}$): $+2, +3, +4, +5, +6$
  • Curium ($\text{Cm}$) to Lawrencium ($\text{Lr}$): $+3$ state becomes progressively the most stable.

Oxo-Cations: Actinoids in higher oxidation states ($+5, +6$) do not exist as simple monoatomic cations, but form stable oxo-cations in aqueous solutions: $\text{UO}_2^{2+}$ (Uranyl), $\mathbf{\text{UO}_2^+}$, $\mathbf{\text{NpO}_2^+}$, $\mathbf{\text{PuO}_2^{2+}}$.

7.9 General Characteristics of Actinoids

  • High Reactivity: Actinoids are far more reactive than lanthanoids. Finely divided actinoid metals ignite spontaneously in air (pyrophoric). They react with boiling water to form oxides and hydroxides.
  • Action of Acids & Alkalies: They are rapidly attacked by Hydrochloric acid ($\text{HCl}$).
    â€¢ NITRIC ACID PASSIVITYPassivity with $\text{HNO}_3$: Concentrated Nitric Acid renders actinoids passive due to the formation of an insoluble, protective oxide film on the metal surface.
    â€¢ Alkalies have no action on actinoid metals.
  • Universal Radioactivity: ALL 14 actinoids are strictly radioactive. Earlier members ($\text{Th}, \text{U}$) have long half-lives ($10^9$ years), while trans-uranium elements ($Z > 92$, e.g., $\text{Lr}$) are synthetic with half-lives ranging from days to 3 minutes!
  • High Complexation Tendency: Actinoids have a much higher tendency to form complexes than lanthanoids because of smaller ionic radii and higher charge density of $An^{4+}$ and oxo-cations. Order of complexing power: $$\mathbf{An^{4+} > UO_2^{2+} > An^{3+} > UO_2^+}$$

7.10 Comprehensive Theoretical Comparison: Lanthanoids vs Actinoids

Property Lanthanoids ($4f$ Series) Actinoids ($5f$ Series)
1. Inner Subshell Filling Filling of $4f$ subshell ($\text{Ce}_{58} \rightarrow \text{Lu}_{71}$). Filling of $5f$ subshell ($\text{Th}_{90} \rightarrow \text{Lr}_{103}$).
