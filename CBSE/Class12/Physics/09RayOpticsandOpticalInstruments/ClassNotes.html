Chapter 9: Ray Optics and Optical Instruments

Exam Revision Capsule: A concise, highly condensed, and fully comprehensive master review sheet containing every core concept, derivation crux, key formula, ray diagram, and optical instrument needed for CBSE Board & Competitive Examinations.

1. Nature of Light & Reflection by Spherical Mirrors

  • Visible Spectrum: Light wavelength $\lambda \approx 400\text{ nm}$ to $750\text{ nm}$ ($4000\text{ Å} - 7500\text{ Å}$). Speed in vacuum $\mathbf{c \approx 3 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}}$.
  • Rectilinear Propagation: Because macroscopic apertures $\gg \lambda$, wave diffraction is negligible; light travels along straight rays.
  • Laws of Reflection:
    1. Angle of incidence equals angle of reflection: $\mathbf{\angle i = \angle r'}$.
    2. Incident ray, reflected ray, and normal lie in the same plane at the point of incidence.
Figure 9.1: The incident ray, reflected ray, and normal lie in the same plane with \(\angle i = \angle r'\).
Cartesian Sign Convention (Must-Follow)
  1. Pole ($P$) of mirror (or optical center $O$ of lens) is the origin $(0,0)$.
  2. All distances measured in the direction of incident light (towards right) are positive (+ve).
  3. All distances measured opposite to incident light (towards left) are negative (-ve) $\implies$ For real object, $\mathbf{u < 0}$ always.
  4. Heights measured upward ($+y$) are positive (+ve); downward ($-y$) are negative (-ve).
Figure 9.2: The Cartesian Sign Convention for spherical mirrors and lenses.

Principal Focus ($F$) & Focal Length ($f$)

  • Concave Mirror: Parallel rays converge to a real focus in front of the mirror $\implies \mathbf{f < 0}$.
  • Convex Mirror: Parallel rays appear to diverge from a virtual focus behind the mirror $\implies \mathbf{f > 0}$.
Figure 9.3: Focus of (a) concave mirror (real), (b) convex mirror (virtual), and (c) focal plane.
Derivation Crux: Focal Length & Radius of Curvature From paraxial triangle geometry in Figure 9.4: $\tan \theta \approx \frac{MD}{CD} \approx \frac{MD}{R}$ and $\tan 2\theta \approx \frac{MD}{FD} \approx \frac{MD}{f}$.
For small angles, $2\theta \approx 2\left(\frac{MD}{R}\right) = \frac{MD}{f} \implies \mathbf{f = \frac{R}{2}}$.
Figure 9.4: Geometry of reflection of an incident ray for deriving \(f = R/2\).
Derivation Crux: Mirror Equation & Magnification From similar triangles $\triangle A'B'F \sim \triangle MPF$ and $\triangle A'B'P \sim \triangle ABP$ (Figure 9.5):
$$\frac{B'F}{FP} = \frac{B'P}{BP} \implies \frac{-v - (-f)}{-f} = \frac{-v}{-u} \implies \frac{v - f}{f} = \frac{v}{u}$$ Dividing throughout by $v$ yields the Mirror Formula: $$\mathbf{\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}}$$ Linear Magnification: $\mathbf{m = \frac{h'}{h} = -\frac{v}{u} = \frac{f}{f - u} = \frac{f - v}{f}}$
Sign Guide: $m < 0 \implies$ Real & Inverted image; $m > 0 \implies$ Virtual & Erect image.
Figure 9.5: Ray diagram for image formation by a concave mirror (Mirror Equation Derivation).
Figure 9.6: Virtual image formation: (a) concave mirror with \(u < f\) (magnified), (b) convex mirror (diminished).

Quick Summary: Image Formation by Mirrors

Mirror Type Object Position Image Position Nature & Size Magnification ($m$)

Exam Traps: Mirror Key Points
  • Covering half of mirror: Complete image is still formed (every ray obeys laws of reflection). Only the intensity/brightness is reduced to half.
  • Longitudinal Object (Phone): Since $m = -v/u$ varies continuously across the phone, longitudinal magnification $m_L = \frac{dv}{du} = -m^2$. The nearer end is magnified more, causing a distorted trapezoidal image.
Figure 9.7: Non-uniform longitudinal magnification of an object lying along the principal axis.

2. Refraction of Light at Plane Interfaces

  • Snell's Law: $\mathbf{\frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = n_{21} = \frac{n_2}{n_1} = \frac{v_1}{v_2} = \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2}}$. Frequency $\nu$ remains strictly unchanged during refraction!
  • Optical vs Mass Density: Turpentine oil has lower mass density than water ($870\text{ kg m}^{-3} < 1000\text{ kg m}^{-3}$) but is optically denser ($n \approx 1.47 > 1.33$).
Figure 9.8: Refraction and reflection at a plane interface between two optical media.
Lateral Shift in a Glass Slab For a rectangular slab of thickness $t$, emergent ray is strictly parallel to the incident ray ($i_1 = r_2'$).
Perpendicular lateral shift: $\mathbf{x = \frac{t \sin(i - r)}{\cos r}}$.
Shift $x$ increases with: slab thickness $t$, angle of incidence $i$, refractive index $n$, and decreasing wavelength (violet shifts more than red).
Figure 9.9: Lateral displacement of a ray passing through a parallel-sided glass slab.
Apparent Depth & Normal Shift When viewing an object in a denser medium ($n$) from a rarer medium (air) near normal incidence:
$$\mathbf{\text{Apparent Depth } (h') = \frac{\text{Real Depth } (h)}{n}} \implies \mathbf{\text{Normal Shift } (d) = h - h' = t\left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)}$$ For multiple liquid layers: $\mathbf{d_{total} = \sum t_i \left(1 - \frac{1}{n_i}\right)}$.
Inverse Case (Object in air viewed from water): Apparent height is greater: $\mathbf{h' = n \cdot h}$.
Figure 9.10: Apparent depth and normal upward shift under normal and oblique viewing.

3. Total Internal Reflection (TIR)

Mandatory Conditions for TIR
  1. Light must travel from an optically denser medium to an optically rarer medium.
  2. Angle of incidence must exceed the critical angle: $\mathbf{i > i_c}$.
Critical Angle Formula: $\mathbf{\sin i_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1}} \implies \mathbf{n = \frac{1}{\sin i_c}}$ (when rarer medium is air).
Standard Critical Angles: Water ($48.75^\circ$), Crown glass ($41.14^\circ$), Flint glass ($37.31^\circ$), Diamond ($24.41^\circ$).
Figure 9.11: Successive rays from denser medium showing critical angle and total internal reflection.
Figure 9.12: Demonstration of total internal reflection using a laser beam in water.

High-Yield Applications of TIR

  • Mirage: Hot air near ground is optically rarer than upper cooler air. Downward rays bend away from normal and undergo TIR ($i > i_c$), producing an inverted virtual image resembling a water pool.
  • Diamond Sparkle: Very small critical angle ($i_c \approx 24.4^\circ$) and high refractive index ($n \approx 2.42$) allow multiple internal reflections before light emerges through facets.
  • Totally Reflecting Prisms ($45^\circ-90^\circ-45^\circ$): Crown glass ($i_c \approx 41.1^\circ < 45^\circ$) reflects light with 100% efficiency without silvering degradation:
    • Bends rays through $90^\circ$ (periscopes) or $180^\circ$ (Porro prism in binoculars).
    • Inverts images without altering size (erecting prisms).
Figure 9.13: Prisms designed to bend rays by \(90^\circ\), \(180^\circ\), or invert images using TIR.
Optical Fibres & Light Pipes Consists of a central Core ($n_1 \approx 1.68$) coated with outer Cladding ($n_2 \approx 1.44$).
Rays launched inside within the acceptance angle undergo repeated TIR with negligible loss over kilometers. Used in endoscopy, communications, and sensing.
Figure 9.14: Light undergoing successive total internal reflections along the core of an optical fibre.

4. Refraction at Spherical Surfaces and Thin Lenses

Derivation Crux: Refraction at a Single Spherical Surface Using paraxial Snell's law $n_1 i \approx n_2 r$ with exterior angles $i = \alpha + \gamma$ and $r = \gamma - \beta$ (Figure 9.15):
$$n_1\left(\frac{MN}{OM} + \frac{MN}{MC}\right) = n_2\left(\frac{MN}{MC} - \frac{MN}{MI}\right) \implies \frac{n_1}{OM} + \frac{n_2}{MI} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{MC}$$ Applying sign convention ($OM = -u, MI = +v, MC = +R$): $$\mathbf{\frac{n_2}{v} - \frac{n_1}{u} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R}}$$
Figure 9.15: Refraction at a convex spherical surface separating medium \(n_1\) from medium \(n_2\).
Derivation Crux: Lens Maker's Formula & Thin Lens Equation Adding interface equations for first surface $ABC$ and second surface $ADC$ (Figure 9.16):
$$\frac{n_2}{v_1} - \frac{n_1}{u} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R_1} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{n_1}{v} - \frac{n_2}{v_1} = \frac{n_1 - n_2}{R_2} = -\frac{n_2 - n_1}{R_2}$$ Summing eliminates the intermediate image distance $v_1$: $$\frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \left(\frac{n_2}{n_1} - 1\right)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right)$$ When $u = \infty, v = f$, which gives the Lens Maker's Formula: $$\mathbf{\frac{1}{f} = (n_{21} - 1)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right)} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{\frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}}$$ Linear Magnification for Lens: $\mathbf{m = \frac{h'}{h} = +\frac{v}{u} = \frac{f}{f + u} = \frac{f - v}{f}}$
Figure 9.16: Refraction through a double convex lens showing first and second surface refractions.

Rules for Ray Tracing Through Lenses

  1. Ray parallel to principal axis passes through $F'$ (convex) or appears to diverge from $F$ (concave).
  2. Ray through optical center ($O$) passes undeviated.
  3. Ray through primary focus $F$ emerges parallel to principal axis.
Figure 9.17: Rules for tracing rays through (a) convex lens and (b) concave lens.
Power of a Lens & Combination in Contact Power ($P$): Converging/diverging ability of a lens: $\mathbf{P = \frac{1}{f\text{ (in meters)}}}$ in Dioptre ($\text{D}$).
Converging (convex) lens: $P > 0$; Diverging (concave) lens: $P < 0$.

Coaxial Thin Lenses in Contact: $$\mathbf{\frac{1}{F} = \frac{1}{f_1} + \frac{1}{f_2} + \dots}, \quad \mathbf{P = P_1 + P_2 + \dots}, \quad \mathbf{m = m_1 \times m_2 \times \dots}$$
Figure 9.18: Lens power defined by deviation \(\delta\) for a ray incident at unit height \(h = 1\text{ m}\).
Figure 9.19: Image formation by two thin coaxial lenses in contact.
High-Yield Competitive Rules: Lens Alterations
  • Lens in a liquid medium: $\frac{f_m}{f_a} = \frac{(n_g - 1)}{(n_g/n_m - 1)}$. In water ($n_m = 4/3$), $\mathbf{f_{water} = 4 f_{air}}$. If $n_m = n_g$, $f \to \infty$ (lens becomes invisible plate). If $n_m > n_g$, lens nature reverses!
  • Transverse vertical cut: Cutting equiconvex lens ($f$) vertically into two halves produces plano-convex lenses with $\mathbf{f' = 2f, P' = P/2}$.
  • Longitudinal horizontal cut: Cutting along principal axis keeps radii unchanged $\implies \mathbf{f' = f}$; aperture halved $\implies$ intensity halved.

5. Refraction through a Prism & Dispersion

The Prism Formula & Minimum Deviation From quadrilateral and triangle geometry of triangular prism (Figure 9.21):
$$\mathbf{r_1 + r_2 = A} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{\delta = i + e - A \iff A + \delta = i + e}$$ At Minimum Deviation ($D_m$): Ray passes symmetrically, $i = e$ and $r_1 = r_2 = r = A/2$. Refracted ray is parallel to the base.
$$\mathbf{n = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{A + D_m}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{A}{2}\right)}}$$ For thin prism ($A < 10^\circ$): $\mathbf{D_m = (n - 1)A}$.
Figure 9.21: Ray path, angles of incidence, refraction, emergence, and deviation in a triangular prism.
Figure 9.22: Plot of angle of deviation \(\delta\) versus angle of incidence \(i\) showing unique minimum deviation \(D_m\).

Dispersion of Light

  • Cause: Cauchy's formula $n(\lambda) = A + B/\lambda^2$. Since $\lambda_r > \lambda_v \implies n_r < n_v \implies \delta_r < \delta_v$. Red light deviates the least; violet light deviates the most.
  • Angular Dispersion: $\mathbf{\theta = \delta_v - \delta_r = (n_v - n_r)A}$.
  • Dispersive Power: $\mathbf{\omega = \frac{\delta_v - \delta_r}{\delta_y} = \frac{n_v - n_r}{n_y - 1}}$.
  • Rainbow:
    • Primary Rainbow: 2 refractions + 1 internal reflection. Red on outer ring ($42^\circ$), violet on inner ($40^\circ$).
    • Secondary Rainbow: 2 refractions + 2 internal reflections. Fainter, reversed color order (violet outer $53^\circ$, red inner $50^\circ$).

6. Scattering of Light

Rayleigh's Law of Scattering For scattering particles smaller than wavelength ($a \ll \lambda$): $$\mathbf{I \propto \frac{1}{\lambda^4}}$$ Scattering intensity is inversely proportional to the 4th power of wavelength.
  • Blue Color of Clear Sky: Blue light has shorter wavelength ($\sim 1.8\times$ shorter than red) and scatters $\sim (1.8)^4 \approx 10\text{–}15$ times more strongly.
  • Reddish Sunrise and Sunset: Sunlight traverses maximum atmospheric path; blue is scattered out, leaving longer wavelengths (red/orange) to reach the eye directly.
  • Danger Signals are Red: Red light scatters least and penetrates smoke/fog over maximum distances.
  • White Clouds: Droplets have size $a \gg \lambda \implies$ Rayleigh's law fails; all wavelengths are scattered equally (Mie scattering), appearing white.

7. Optical Instruments: Formulas & Ray Diagrams

7.1 The Simple Microscope (Magnifier)

A single convex lens of short focal length $f$. Object placed between $O$ and $F$ forms an erect, magnified, virtual image.

Simple Microscope Formulas $$\text{Angular Magnification } m = \frac{\beta}{\alpha}$$
  • Case 1: Image at Near Point ($v = -D = -25\text{ cm}$ • Maximum Strain): $$\mathbf{m = 1 + \frac{D}{f}}$$
  • Case 2: Normal Adjustment (Image at Infinity • Relaxed Eye): $$\mathbf{m = \frac{D}{f}}$$
Figure 9.23: Simple microscope: (a) image at near point \(D\), (b) unaided eye, and (c) normal adjustment (image at infinity).

7.2 The Compound Microscope

Uses two convex lenses: Objective ($O$) with very short $f_o$ and small aperture; Eyepiece ($E$) with short $f_e$ ($f_e > f_o$) and larger aperture.

Compound Microscope Formulas Total Magnification: $\mathbf{m = m_o \times m_e \approx -\left(\frac{L}{f_o}\right) m_e}$
  • Case 1: Image at Near Point ($D = 25\text{ cm}$): $$\mathbf{m = -\left(\frac{L}{f_o}\right)\left(1 + \frac{D}{f_e}\right) \approx -\left(\frac{v_o}{u_o}\right)\left(1 + \frac{D}{f_e}\right)}, \quad \mathbf{L_{tube} = v_o + u_e}$$
  • Case 2: Normal Adjustment (Image at Infinity • Relaxed Eye): $$\mathbf{m = -\left(\frac{L}{f_o}\right)\left(\frac{D}{f_e}\right) \approx -\left(\frac{v_o}{u_o}\right)\left(\frac{D}{f_e}\right)}, \quad \mathbf{L_{tube} = v_o + f_e}$$
Figure 9.24: Complete ray diagram of image formation in a compound microscope.

7.3 Astronomical Telescope (Refracting Type)

Observes distant objects. Objective ($O$) has large focal length $f_o$ and very large aperture (to maximize light gathering & resolution). Eyepiece ($E$) has small focal length $f_e$.

Astronomical Refracting Telescope Formulas $$\text{Magnifying Power } m = \frac{\beta}{\alpha}$$
  • Case 1: Normal Adjustment (Image at Infinity • Relaxed Eye): $$\mathbf{m = \frac{f_o}{f_e}}, \quad \text{Tube Length } \mathbf{L = f_o + f_e}$$
  • Case 2: Image at Near Point ($D = 25\text{ cm}$): $$\mathbf{m = \frac{f_o}{f_e}\left(1 + \frac{f_e}{D}\right)}, \quad \text{Tube Length } \mathbf{L = f_o + u_e}$$

7.4 Reflecting Telescopes (Cassegrain Telescope)

Uses a large concave paraboloidal primary mirror and a convex secondary mirror to focus light through a central hole in the primary.

Figure 9.26: Schematic diagram of a Cassegrain reflecting telescope using a primary concave mirror and secondary convex mirror.
4 Major Advantages of Reflecting over Refracting Telescopes (CBSE Favorite)
  1. Zero Chromatic Aberration: Reflection does not depend on wavelength; mirrors reflect all colors identically.
  2. Zero Spherical Aberration: A paraboloidal primary mirror focuses all parallel rays to a single geometric point.
  3. Mechanical Support: Heavy mirrors can be supported across their entire back surface, preventing gravitational sagging.
  4. Cost & Practicality: Only one front optical surface needs grinding and polishing, enabling giant apertures (e.g., Keck $10\text{ m}$).
