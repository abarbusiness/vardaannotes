Chapter 4: Early Humans and Beginning of Civilisation

NCERT Solutions
Q1. Do you think life became easier or more challenging after humans started farming? Give two reasons for your answer.
Answer

Life became both easier and more challenging in different ways after humans started farming. However, the challenges of a sedentary farming lifestyle were significant.

Reasons it became more challenging:

  • Dependence on Weather: Farmers were highly dependent on favorable weather conditions like timely rains. Droughts or floods could destroy entire crops, leading to severe food shortages, whereas hunter-gatherers could migrate to find new food sources.
  • Labor Intensive: Agriculture required immense physical labor—clearing land, ploughing, sowing, weeding, and harvesting. It involved much more sustained hard work compared to hunting and foraging.
Q2. The environment offers human societies both opportunities as well as challenges. Explain with reference to early farming communities and river-valley civilisations.
Answer

The environment played a dual role in the development of early communities:

  • Opportunities: River valleys (like the Nile, Indus, Tigris, and Euphrates) provided highly fertile alluvial soil and an abundant water supply. This made large-scale agriculture possible, yielding surplus food. Rivers also served as natural transport routes for trade and communication.
  • Challenges: The environment also posed significant risks. Rivers were prone to devastating annual floods that could wipe out crops and settlements. In response, these civilisations had to build complex irrigation systems, check dams, and embankments. Furthermore, some regions faced extreme heat or shifting river courses (like the drying up of the Sarasvati), forcing populations to adapt or migrate.
Q3. Why do historians divide early human history into different ages such as Stone Age, Bronze Age, and Iron Age? What does this classification tell us about human progress?
Answer

Historians divide early human history into the Stone Age, Bronze Age, and Iron Age primarily based on the types of materials used for making tools and weapons during those periods.

What this tells us about human progress:

  • It highlights a clear trajectory of technological advancement. Moving from crude chipped stones to refined metals shows an increasing understanding of natural resources (metallurgy) and complex manufacturing techniques.
  • It reflects economic and social evolution. Better tools (like bronze and iron) led to increased agricultural efficiency, which created food surpluses. This surplus allowed society to diversify into different professions (artisans, traders, rulers), leading to the rise of urban cities and complex civilisations.
Q4. Imagine you are a Neolithic farmer. Describe one day of your life. What challenges would you face that a hunter-gatherer would not?
Answer

Example description: I wake up at dawn in my sun-dried brick house. My day starts with tending to our domesticated goats and sheep, ensuring they are fed. Then, I head to the fields with my stone hoe and sickle to clear weeds and harvest the wheat. The sun is hot, and the work is exhausting. In the evening, my family uses earthenware pots to store the harvested grains and cook our meal. We sleep closely together in our settlement to protect our crops and animals from wild predators.

Challenges unique to a farmer:

  • I am tied to one piece of land and cannot simply move away if the soil becomes poor or if a drought hits.
  • I have to constantly defend my stored grains and livestock from thieves and wild animals.
  • My daily routine involves repetitive, back-breaking labor compared to the varied, mobile lifestyle of a hunter-gatherer.
Q5. Imagine that the Harappan script gets deciphered tomorrow. What new types of information do you think historians might learn?
Answer

If the Harappan script (Sindhu lipi) is deciphered, historians could unlock a wealth of unknown information:

  • Political Structure: We might finally learn the names of their rulers, dynasties, or administrative officials, and understand how such a vast, standardized civilisation was governed.
  • Religious Beliefs: The inscriptions on the seals could reveal the names of the gods they worshipped, their myths, and the meaning behind rituals like the Great Bath.
  • Trade and Language: It would provide details about their exact trade agreements with Mesopotamia, their accounting systems, and the phonetic sounds of the language they actually spoke.

Q7. "Bronze Age civilisations developed independently but shared common features." Examine this statement with reference to the civilisations given in the chapter.
Answer

The major Bronze Age civilisations—Harappan, Mesopotamian, Egyptian, and Chinese—emerged in geographically distinct river valleys. Despite their independent development, they shared several striking common features:

  • River Valley Origins: All of them flourished along major rivers (Indus/Sarasvati, Tigris/Euphrates, Nile, and Huang He/Yangtze) which provided fertile land for agriculture.
  • Urbanisation: They all transitioned from villages to large, well-planned city-states or urban centres.
  • Metallurgy & Craft: They all mastered the use of bronze/copper to create superior tools, weapons, and artistic vessels.
  • Invention of Writing: Each civilisation developed a distinct writing system (Cuneiform in Mesopotamia, Hieroglyphics in Egypt, Logographics in China, and the Harappan script) to record trade, laws, and history.
  • Social Stratification: They all developed complex social hierarchies featuring rulers, priests, artisans, and farmers.
Q8. Although rivers provided many benefits, they also created challenges for early societies. Discuss both the advantages and disadvantages of settling near rivers.
Answer

Advantages:

  • Fertile Soil: Annual flooding deposited nutrient-rich silt (like the 'kemet' in Egypt), making the land highly productive for agriculture.
  • Water Supply: Rivers provided a constant source of fresh water for drinking, domestic use, and large-scale irrigation for crops.
  • Transport & Trade: Rivers acted as natural highways, facilitating the easy movement of people and heavy goods, which boosted trade and cultural exchange.

Disadvantages (Challenges):

  • Unpredictable Floods: Violent and unpredictable flooding could destroy entire settlements, wash away crops, and drown livestock and people.
  • Changing River Courses: Rivers occasionally changed their paths or dried up (such as the Sarasvati river), forcing entire populations to abandon their cities and migrate.
  • Waterborne Diseases: Stagnant water left behind by floods could become a breeding ground for mosquitoes, leading to diseases.
Q9. With the help of your teacher, find out more about the Code of Hammurabi. Why was it important? Do you think it was fair to all sections of society? Give reasons for your answer.
Answer

The Code of Hammurabi was a comprehensive set of laws compiled by the Babylonian King Hammurabi around 1754 BCE. It is one of the oldest deciphered writings of significant length in the world.

Why it was important: It was the first time laws were written down and publicly displayed for all to see. It established the principle that government should provide justice, heavily influencing the concept of the rule of law in future civilisations.

Was it fair? By modern standards, it was not fair to all sections of society. It operated on the principle of "an eye for an eye," but the punishments varied drastically depending on social class. For example, if a noble injured another noble, the punishment was severe. But if a noble injured a commoner or a slave, they usually only had to pay a minor fine. It heavily favored the wealthy and ruling classes over the lower classes.

Q10. If you had to choose one major innovation from early civilisations that changed the world permanently, what would it be and why?
Answer

(Students can choose various answers, but a strong choice is writing.)

The most transformative innovation was the invention of writing (such as cuneiform in Mesopotamia).

Why: Before writing, human knowledge relied entirely on oral traditions, which were prone to being forgotten or altered. Writing allowed humanity to record laws, scientific discoveries, literature, and history permanently. It enabled communication across vast distances and across generations, acting as the foundation upon which all modern human progress and education is built.

Q11. Compare the social hierarchy and daily life of people in the Egyptian civilisation with those in Mesopotamia or China. What similarities and differences do you notice?
Answer

Similarities:

  • Hierarchy: All three civilisations were highly stratified. A supreme ruler (Pharaoh in Egypt, King/Priest in Mesopotamia/China) sat at the top, followed by nobles/priests, artisans/merchants, and finally a large base of farmers and slaves.
  • Agriculture-Based Daily Life: The vast majority of people in all these civilisations were farmers whose daily lives revolved around the agricultural seasons and river flooding.

Differences:

  • Status of Women: In Egypt, women generally enjoyed more rights than their counterparts in Mesopotamia or China. Egyptian women could own property, run businesses, and even rule as Queens (like Cleopatra), whereas societies in China and Mesopotamia were more strictly patriarchal.
  • Ruler's Divine Status: In Egypt, the Pharaoh was worshipped directly as a living god. In China, the Zhou rulers were seen as "appointees of heaven" but could be overthrown if they failed the people. In Mesopotamia, kings were often seen as intermediaries between the gods and the people rather than gods themselves.

Q13. Activity: Choose one early civilisation (Mesopotamia, Egypt, or China) and prepare a mini-scrapbook or a presentation showing their innovations in tools, writing, art, and architecture. Include pictures, brief descriptions, and explain their significance.
Answer

(This is a creative project for students. Below is an example structure for Egypt.)

  • Civilisation: Egypt
  • Writing: Hieroglyphics and Papyrus. Significance: Created one of the earliest forms of paper and a complex writing system used for religion and administration.
  • Architecture: The Pyramids and Sphinx. Significance: Demonstrated incredible engineering skills and deep religious beliefs regarding the afterlife.
  • Tools: Bronze tools and the shaduf (water-lifting device). Significance: Allowed for the construction of massive stone monuments and expanded irrigation for farming.
  • Art: Tomb paintings and gold jewelry. Significance: Preserved the daily life, fashion, and beliefs of the Egyptian people for eternity.
