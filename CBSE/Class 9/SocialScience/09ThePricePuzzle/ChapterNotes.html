Chapter 9: The Price Puzzle

What Drives the Market

The Big Questions
  1. What are the factors that influence the demand for and supply of goods and services in a market?
  2. How are prices of goods and services determined through demand and supply interactions?
  3. What is market equilibrium, and does it exist in the real world?
  4. How and why does the government intervene in the market?

1. Understanding Demand

What happens if the mangoes your parents bought last week are now half the price? Why are vegetables expensive in the morning but cheaper in the evening? Why does a flight seat cost ₹3,000 one day and ₹9,000 the next? These real-world situations are all driven by the interaction among buyers and sellers. Prices are determined by two powerful forces constantly at work: Demand and Supply.

What is Demand?

As the mango season approaches, prices are high, so people buy smaller quantities. When prices fall, people buy more. Demand is the quantity of a product that people are willing and able to buy at a particular price, depending on their needs, preferences, season, trend, and income. Demand is not just the desire to buy something; it is willingness complemented by the purchasing power to buy it.

Core Concept The Law of Demand

The Law of Demand highlights the inverse relationship between the price of a product and its quantity demanded, keeping other factors constant:

  • When Price (P) ↑ → Quantity Demanded (Q) ↓.
  • When Price (P) ↓ → Quantity Demanded (Q) ↑.

Individual vs. Market Demand

  • Individual Demand: The quantity of a good an individual consumer wants to buy at different prices.
    Example: At ₹150/kg, Srivalli buys 1 kg of mangoes. When the price drops to ₹100/kg, she buys 2 kg. At ₹50/kg, she buys 3 kg. Her individual demand schedule graphically forms a downward-sloping Individual Demand Curve.
  • Market Demand: What happens when others want to buy mangoes too? Market demand is the total quantity demanded by all potential buyers at different prices. It is the sum of all individual demands (e.g., Srivalli + Alex + Israt). The Market Demand Curve is also downward-sloping but is generally flatter (more responsive) because a price drop increases demand across many consumers simultaneously.

2. Other Determinants of Demand

While the price of the good itself is a major factor, several other determinants influence how much people want to buy, even if the price of the original good remains unchanged:

A. Price of Related Goods

  • Substitute goods: Goods that can replace each other (e.g., tea and coffee). If the price of coffee rises, people may switch to tea. Thus, the price of a substitute increases → the demand for the other good increases.
  • Complementary goods: Goods used together to provide utility (e.g., cars and petrol, printers and cartridges). If petrol prices rise sharply, people may buy fewer cars. Thus, the price of a complement increases → the demand for the related good decreases.

B. Income of the Consumer

When household income rises, consumers can afford to buy more goods or choose higher-quality products. A rise in income generally increases the quantity demanded for most goods.

C. Taste and Preference of the Buyer

Consumer preferences, influenced by trends, culture, and demographics, dictate demand. For example, a country with a high population of children will have increased demand for toys and sports shoes, while a population with more elderly people will demand more orthopaedic shoes or medicines.

D. Seasonality

Demand fluctuates based on weather, festivals, and cultural habits (e.g., high demand for sweaters in winter, sweets during Diwali, or books at the start of an academic session).

E. Future Price Expectations

If consumers expect the price of a good to fall in the near future (like a festival sale), they will postpone their purchase, decreasing present demand. Conversely, if they expect prices to rise soon, they will buy immediately, increasing present demand.

Why does Demand Fall as Quantity Increases? Diminishing Marginal Utility

The first mango you eat tastes delicious. The second is good, but by the third or fourth, you barely want any more. This is the principle of diminishing marginal utility. As the utility (usefulness/satisfaction) derived from a successive quantity of a product falls, the willingness to pay for it also decreases. This is a key reason why demand falls at higher quantities.

3. Understanding Supply

From the seller's perspective, we look at supply.

What is Supply?

Supply is the quantity of a product that sellers are willing and able to offer at a particular price in the market.

Core Concept The Law of Supply

The Law of Supply states that there is a direct relationship between the price of a product and its quantity supplied, assuming other factors remain constant:

  • When Price (P) ↑ → Quantity Supplied (Q) ↑ (higher prices incentivize producers by increasing profitability).
  • When Price (P) ↓ → Quantity Supplied (Q) ↓.
  • Individual Supply: The quantity a particular seller offers. For example, when mangoes are ₹50/kg, Seller A supplies 1 kg. At ₹100/kg, he supplies 2 kg, and at ₹150/kg, he supplies 3 kg. This forms an upward-sloping individual supply curve.
  • Market Supply: The sum of all individual supplies (e.g., Seller A + Seller B + Seller C). By combining the quantity supplied by all sellers, we derive the market supply curve, which is also upward-sloping.

4. Other Determinants of Supply

Factors other than the price of the good that affect its supply include:

A. Price of Related Goods (Alternatives for Producer)

Suppliers allocate resources based on profitability. If a farmer can grow wheat or chickpeas, and the price of chickpeas rises significantly in the market while wheat stays low, the farmer will shift resources to grow more chickpeas. The supply of wheat will drop.

B. Number of Sellers in the Market

If new firms enter the market, competition increases, and the overall market supply of the product increases. If sellers leave, supply decreases.

C. Technology

Improvements in technology reduce the cost of production and increase efficiency. For example, adopting cold storage facilities or drip irrigation allows producers to supply more goods at the same cost. Better tech → increased supply.

D. Future Expectations (by Supplier)

If producers expect a boom in demand soon, they will produce more. Alternatively, if a potato wholesaler expects prices to rise during a peak season, they might hold back (hoard) current supply to sell it later at a higher price, thus reducing current supply.

5. Market Equilibrium

Prices in a free market are determined by the interaction between demand and supply. A market involves a constant negotiation between what buyers are willing to pay and what sellers are willing to accept.

  • If Price is too high → Quantity Supplied > Quantity Demanded. This creates a Surplus (Excess Supply). Sellers will have to lower prices to clear their stock.
  • If Price is too low → Quantity Demanded > Quantity Supplied. This creates a Shortage (Excess Demand). Buyers will bid up prices to get the scarce goods.
The Clearing Point Market Equilibrium

Market Equilibrium is the specific point where the Quantity Supplied exactly equals the Quantity Demanded. At this equilibrium price, there is neither a shortage nor a surplus. The market is 'cleared'.

For example, if at ₹100, buyers demand 12 kg of mangoes and sellers supply exactly 12 kg, then ₹100 is the equilibrium price. At ₹40, demand might be 38 kg but supply only 6 kg (severe shortage). At ₹150, demand might be 8 kg but supply 43 kg (severe surplus). Thus, prices tend to naturally adjust toward the stable equilibrium.

Does Market Equilibrium Exist in the Real World?

In economic theory, equilibrium is a neat intersection point. However, in the real world, markets are highly dynamic. Factors like new technology, changes in wages, weather events, political crises, or pandemics constantly shift both the demand and supply curves.

Therefore, 'equilibrium' is never fully stable. The market is always in a continuous process of adjusting to a new equilibrium. For example, during the COVID-19 pandemic, demand for face masks skyrocketed suddenly, causing prices to rise. Over time, suppliers ramped up production (supply increased), and as the pandemic eased (demand fell), prices eventually reduced and adjusted to a new reality.

Real World Example Dynamic Pricing in Hotels

Hotels do not charge the same tariff (price) for rooms all the time. Prices change dynamically based on demand and varying conditions. For example, a hotel room in Goa might cost ₹1,500 on an off-season weekday, ₹8,000 on a tourist season weekend, and ₹25,000 on New Year's Eve (peak demand). If a group tour cancels, the hotel may slash prices by 40% overnight to fill empty rooms. This perfectly illustrates prices changing rapidly with shifts in demand and supply.

6. Role of Government in the Economy

While markets are excellent at allocating goods based on willingness and ability to pay, they do not always work fairly. If essential goods like life-saving medicines become prohibitively expensive, they become inaccessible to the poor. Thus, the government must intervene to ensure fairness, equity, and the welfare of vulnerable groups.

A. Regulation of Unfair Practices

The government intervenes to protect consumers and workers from exploitation:

  • Price Ceiling: An imposed limit that sets the maximum amount a seller can charge for a product. The government uses this for essential goods to prevent overcharging. Example: During COVID-19, sanitizer demand surged, leading to hoarding and black-marketing. The government intervened by declaring sanitizers essential commodities and capping the maximum retail price at ₹100 for a 200 ml bottle.
  • Price Floor: An imposed limit on how low a price can be charged for a good or service. To be effective, a price floor must be set above the market equilibrium price. A common example is the minimum wage set by the government to ensure workers earn enough for their hard work.
  • Controlling Monopolies: A monopoly is a market structure with a single seller controlling the entire supply of a unique product, facing no close substitutes. This allows them to charge exorbitant prices and supply poor quality goods. The government regulates such monopolies to keep prices and quantities in check, protecting consumer welfare.

B. Provision of Public Goods

Public goods are services provided by the government for the benefit of all citizens, such as national defence, roads, bridges, public parks, streetlighting, and sanitation/drainage systems.

Why doesn't the private sector provide them? Because public goods do not generate direct profit. Also, they suffer from the "free-rider problem". Suppose your neighborhood needs a park costing ₹5,000 per family. Many families might refuse to pay, thinking, "If others pay, the park will be built anyway, and I can use it without paying." Because of this thinking, not enough money is collected, and the park is never built. Thus, the government must use tax money to provide these essential services to ensure social welfare and equal access.

C. Limitations of Government Intervention

While necessary, excessive government regulation can be inefficient and have adverse side effects on the economy:

  • Price distortions and reduced producer incentives: When the government fixes prices below market levels, producers lose motivation. For instance, if the government sets a maximum price (ceiling) for wheat at ₹20/kg while market forces set it at ₹30/kg, farmers receive less than they would in a free market. This causes farmers to lose motivation, leading to reduced production and severe shortages.
  • Compliance burdens (Hampering Ease of Doing Business): Government intervention often requires extensive regulations, licenses, permits, and compliance procedures. For instance, a small restaurant may need multiple permissions related to food safety, fire safety, and pollution control. The immense time and cost involved discourage small entrepreneurs from starting or expanding businesses.
  • Discourages innovation: Heavy regulation and price controls reduce the incentive to invest in new ideas or better technology. If farmers cannot earn adequate returns due to price caps, they won't invest in better seeds or irrigation, reducing long-term productivity and output.

Conclusion

Understanding how economic systems work provides the tools to think critically, use resources wisely, and make informed decisions. Markets are not machines with fixed equilibria; they are dynamic systems that constantly adapt, evolve, and respond to changing conditions. The next time you see a price change, you can decode reality by asking: Is supply changing? Is demand shifting? Is government intervention helping or hurting?

Before we move on...
  • Demand is the quantity consumers are willing and able to buy. The Law of Demand shows an inverse relationship: as price falls, quantity demanded rises. It is influenced by income, prices of substitutes/complements, tastes, seasonality, and expectations.
  • Supply is the quantity sellers are willing and able to offer. The Law of Supply shows a direct relationship: as price rises, quantity supplied increases. It depends on prices of related goods, number of sellers, technology, and input costs.
  • Market equilibrium occurs when quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. Fundamental markets constantly adjust toward new equilibria as conditions change dynamically.
  • Government intervenes when markets fail, produce unfair outcomes (unaffordable essentials), under-provide public goods, or enable monopolies. However, excessive regulations can also have adverse effects like reduced producer incentives and compliance burdens.
