Chapter 5: State and Society up to 1000 CE

NCERT Solutions
Q1. How did political organisation change from the Vedic period to the age of large empires such as the Mauryas and the Guptas? Explain the administrative system of the early Indian states.
Answer

Change in Political Organisation:

  • Vedic Period: Society was kin-based. The basic political unit was the jana (clan) led by a raja (chieftain). Identity was tied to kinship, not territory.
  • Janapadas to Mahajanapadas: Over time, kin-based identity shifted to territorial identity. People settled on specific lands forming janapadas, which later consolidated into larger, powerful states called mahajanapadas (e.g., Magadha).
  • Empires (Mauryas & Guptas): These states expanded their control over vast regions, establishing massive empires with highly structured, centralised administrative systems.

Administrative System:

Empires were governed through a multi-layered system:

  • Central: The King, advised by a council of ministers (mantri-parishad) handling treasury, taxation, and defence.
  • Provincial/District: The empire was divided into provinces (bhuktis/mandalams) and further into districts (vishayas/nadus), headed by appointed governors or district officers.
  • Local: Villages remained the smallest administrative unit, largely self-reliant, functioning through assemblies and committees (like variyams) that handled local irrigation, temples, and justice.
Q2. Describe the role of the king, important officers, and the methods used to govern large territories.
Answer

Role of the King:

The king (samrat, maharaja) was the supreme authority responsible for protecting subjects from external threats and internal disorder, ensuring justice, and upholding dharma (ethical conduct). The Arthashastra advised kings to be energetic and actively involved in governance.

Important Officers & Methods:

To govern large territories, the king relied on Kautilya’s Saptanga theory (seven constituents of the state) and delegated power:

  • Mantri-parishad: A council of ministers containing the chief legal advisor, commander-in-chief, and treasurer.
  • Provincial Governors (Kumaramatyas): Administered regional divisions on behalf of the king.
  • Sandhivigrahika: Introduced during the Gupta period, this was the minister of peace and war.
  • Delegation & Decentralisation: Kings divided territories into provinces, districts, and villages, delegating power to local administrators while keeping the central military and treasury under royal control.
Q3. After studying this chapter, what do you think were the most important features of the state and society in India before 1000 CE?
Answer

The most important features included:

  • Evolution of Statehood: The transition from nomadic, kin-based clans (janas) to massive, highly organised territorial empires (like the Mauryas and Cholas).
  • Ethical Governance: The strong link between politics and ethics, where the king was bound by dharma, samatva, and rita to ensure justice and the welfare of the people.
  • Social Mobility and Fluidity: While the four varnas existed, early society showed significant flexibility, with occupations not strictly tied to birth, and guilds facilitating economic and social mobility.
  • Village Autonomy: Regardless of which empire was in power, the village unit remained highly autonomous, managing its own local affairs through democratic assemblies (e.g., the Kudavolai system).
  • Cultural & Religious Continuity: A continuous tradition of learning (Gurukulas), rich literature (Vedas, Sangam texts), and the evolution of religious life towards the Bhakti tradition.
Q4. What do early texts such as the Rig Veda, Arthashastra, and the Mahabharata reveal about political and social life?
Answer
  • Rig Veda: Reveals that early political life was based on clans (janas) led by chiefs (rajas) and controlled by assemblies (sabha, samiti). It shows a flexible social structure where people in the same family could hold different occupations, and highlights the respected position of women sages.
  • Arthashastra: Authored by Kautilya, it provides a highly detailed manual on statecraft, administration, and economics. It reveals the complex machinery of empire management, the Saptanga theory (seven pillars of the state), and the necessity of an active, vigilant king.
  • Mahabharata (especially Shanti Parva): Emphasizes the ethical responsibilities of a ruler. It reveals that the ultimate duty of a king was to uphold dharma, protect the weak, ensure justice impartially, and uphold the principle of samatva (sameness of all beings).
Q5. What can we learn from early Indian society about varna and the role of women?
Answer

Varna:

Early texts show that the varna system was initially a functional division based on duties and values (knowledge, warfare, trade, labour), rather than a rigid hierarchy fixed by birth. There was significant social and occupational mobility, though this gradually solidified into more rigid jatis (castes) based on birth and endogamy over the centuries.

Role of Women:

In early Vedic society, women held a very high and respected position. They had access to education (evidenced by women sages like Apala and Lopamudra composing hymns), participated in philosophical debates, and attended political assemblies like the sabha. Though their status fluctuated in later periods, women continued to play vital roles, occasionally ruling as regents (Prabhavati Gupta), managing property, and actively participating in economic and religious life.

Q6. Explain how assemblies like sabha and samiti limited the power of the raja. Which modern institutions perform similar functions today?
Answer

In the Vedic period, the raja was not an absolute dictator. His power was checked by democratic assemblies:

  • Sabha: A smaller, elite body that functioned primarily as a judicial and advisory council.
  • Samiti: A larger, popular assembly representing the broader population that discussed major policy decisions and political affairs. In some cases, these assemblies even had the power to elect or expel a king.

Modern Equivalents:

Today, these functions are performed by the Parliament (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha) at the national level, and State Legislative Assemblies at the state level. In villages, the Gram Sabha closely mirrors the ancient samiti, ensuring grassroots participation in governance.

Q7. What do the terms varna and jati refer to in early Indian society? How were they different, and what factors may have contributed to the formation of various jatis?
Answer

Difference:

  • Varna: Refers to the broad, four-fold theoretical division of society (Brahmanas, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, Shudras) originally based on occupation, function, and values. The number was fixed at four.
  • Jati: Refers to distinct, localized social and occupational groups within the broader varnas. Unlike varnas, the number of jatis was practically limitless and constantly growing.

Factors contributing to Jati formation:

As the economy expanded, new specialised occupations emerged (e.g., silk weavers, salt makers), forming new groups. Over time, these groups began practicing endogamy (marrying only within their specific occupational or territorial group) and formed deep kinship ties, solidifying into distinct jatis.

Q8. Why do you think education in early India emphasised both knowledge and moral values? How might this have benefited society?
Answer

Education in the gurukula system was holistic. While it taught practical subjects (grammar, astronomy, archery), it heavily emphasized moral values like truth, patience, humility, and self-control.

Why and How it Benefited Society:

The goal of education was not just to make a student employable, but to prepare them for life according to dharma. By instilling strong moral values alongside practical skills, the education system produced individuals who were not only capable but also highly ethical. This ensured social harmony, reduced crime and corruption, and guaranteed that those who gained power (like kings or ministers) ruled with compassion and justice rather than tyranny.

Q9. Look at the major trade routes of early India (Fig 5.12). How do you think these routes helped people in the exchange of goods, skills, beliefs, and cultural practices.
Answer

Major trade routes like the Dakshinapatha (South route) and Uttarapatha (North route), along with maritime ports, acted as the vital arteries of ancient India.

  • Goods & Skills: They allowed for the transport of regional specialties (spices from the south, silk and horses from the north/west). Artisans moving along these routes shared new manufacturing techniques and tools.
  • Beliefs & Culture: Trade routes were used not just by merchants, but by monks, pilgrims, and scholars. This facilitated the spread of religions like Buddhism and Jainism across the subcontinent and eventually to Central and Southeast Asia. Art styles, language, and literature also spread along these paths, creating a synthesised pan-Indian culture.
Q10. What might have been the advantages and challenges of ruling a large empire in the absence of modern communication systems?
Answer

Advantages:

  • A large empire meant vast resources, immense tax revenue, and control over major trade routes, leading to great wealth.
  • It brought peace over a large area (pax), reducing constant warfare between small, rival kingdoms, which allowed agriculture and trade to flourish.

Challenges:

  • Communication Lag: Without modern tech, messages took weeks or months by horse or foot. If a rebellion broke out or an invasion occurred at the border, the central king wouldn't know until it was too late.
  • Dependence on Governors: Kings had to delegate heavy power to provincial governors. If a governor became too ambitious, they could easily break away and declare independence, leading to the empire's fragmentation.
Q11. Many ideas about governance come from texts composed by scholars and advisors of the king. What might be some limitations of relying only on such sources?
Answer

While texts like the Arthashastra are invaluable, relying only on them has limitations:

  • Bias: Because they were written by scholars who were often patronised by the king, the texts tend to glorify the king and paint an overly positive picture of the administration.
  • Theory vs. Reality: These texts describe how things ought to be (the ideal state), which might be very different from how things actually were on the ground for common people.
  • Lack of Commoner Perspective: These texts rarely capture the daily struggles, opinions, or exact living conditions of the ordinary farmers, labourers, and lower castes.
Q12. Read the source and answer the questions: The Nashik cave inscription (2nd century CE) of Ushavadata records...
a. What does this source tell us about the economic role of guilds?
b. Why were guilds trusted with money deposits?
c. Identify the donor and the donees from the given source.
Answer

a. Economic role of guilds: It shows that guilds (shrenis) acted as modern-day banks or financial institutions. They accepted huge monetary deposits and paid out a fixed monthly interest on those deposits.

b. Trust in guilds: Guilds were highly organised, wealthy, and stable institutions backed by wealthy merchants and artisans. Their rules were strictly enforced, making them secure places to invest large sums of money perpetually without fear of the funds being lost.

c. Donor and Donees:

  • Donor: Ushavadata (son of Dinika, son-in-law of King Nahapana).
  • Donees (Recipients of the benefit): The members of the Sangha (Buddhist monks) of any sect dwelling in the cave, who received cloth money and outside life money from the interest generated.
Q13. Activity: Mark and locate on the map of India the following important centres: Pataliputra, Nashik, Ujjayini, Vikramshila, Kanchipuram, Mathura, Rajagriha.
Answer

(This is a map-pointing activity. Students should refer to political/historical maps.)

  • Pataliputra: Modern-day Patna, Bihar (on the banks of the Ganges).
  • Nashik: In northern Maharashtra.
  • Ujjayini: Modern Ujjain, Madhya Pradesh.
  • Vikramshila: In Bhagalpur district, Bihar.
  • Kanchipuram: In northern Tamil Nadu, near Chennai.
  • Mathura: In western Uttar Pradesh, near Agra.
  • Rajagriha: Modern Rajgir, Bihar (south of Patna).
