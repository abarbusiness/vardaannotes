Democracy

Let's Recall What is Democracy?

Democracy is a form of government in which the source of power and authority lies with the citizens, who elect their representatives through free and fair elections. It is founded on the principles of freedom, equality, justice, rights, and duties.

India is the world's largest democracy. With a population of over 140 crore and a voter base of over 96.8 crore (in 2024), Bharat represents the largest participatory democracy in the world.

A constitution plays a crucial role in safeguarding democratic rights. The Constitution of India was adopted on 26 November 1949 and came into force on 26 January 1950. It guarantees and protects Fundamental Rights and ensures no citizen is discriminated against.

1. Tracing Democratic Traditions from Early Times

The democratic ethos in India did not emerge suddenly; it evolved through a long historical process.

In the Vedic period, assemblies like the Sabha, Samiti, and Vidhata involved collective decision-making. From the earliest times, republican states (ganas or sanghas) showed that kings were not independent rulers but worked in consultation with assemblies.

  • Rig Veda: A verse from the Aikyamatya Suktam emphasises collective thinking, shared discussion, and unity of purpose.
  • Bauddha Sanghas: A monastic community established by Gautama Buddha that encouraged debate and discussion, allowing members to choose their leader and make decisions through voting.

Foreign invasions and British colonial rule disrupted these structures, but the long struggle for independence revived and strengthened democratic ideas among the people.

Before independence, the Constituent Assembly was formed in 1946 to draft a constitution. It took 2 years, 11 months, and 18 days to draft the world's longest written Constitution under the chairmanship of Dr. B.R. Ambedkar.

2. Principles of Democracy

A thriving democracy rests on certain non-negotiable principles upheld in India through the Constitution:

A. Popular Sovereignty

The ultimate source of power lies with the people. The state derives its authority from them. A government is elected through free and fair elections via a secret ballot. Every citizen aged 18 and above has the right to vote, known as Universal Adult Franchise.

B. Rule of Law

It ensures equality before the law (every individual is treated equally by law irrespective of status) and equal protection of the law. No one is above the law. Disputes are resolved through procedures established by law. No citizen can be punished without due process.

C. Fundamental Rights

These rights are enforceable in courts and are indivisible and inalienable. There are six Fundamental Rights:

  1. Right to Equality (Articles 14–18)
  2. Right to Freedom (Articles 19–22)
  3. Right Against Exploitation (Articles 23–24)
  4. Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25–28)
  5. Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29–30)
  6. Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32)

The Right to Education (Article 21A) was added in 2009, ensuring free and compulsory education for children aged 6 to 14.

D. Separation of Powers

Power is separated among three organs to maintain checks and balances:

  • Legislature: Makes laws.
  • Executive: Implements laws.
  • Judiciary: Interprets laws, protects democratic values, and can declare unconstitutional laws void. Initiates tools like Public Interest Litigation (PIL) to ensure access to justice.

E. Accountability and Transparency

Democratic governments are accountable to citizens through elections and public debates. Transparency is ensured by tools like the Right to Information (RTI) Act, 2005.

Parliamentary procedures such as Question Hour (the first hour of a session where members ask questions) hold the government accountable.

F. Multi-Party System

Several political parties contest elections, representing diverse socio-cultural and regional interests. The party (or coalition) securing more than 50% of seats forms the government; others form the opposition. They function under the Representation of the People Act, 1951.

G. Safeguarding Rights of Vulnerable Groups

The government must ensure equitable resource distribution. Article 46 states the State shall promote the educational and economic interests of weaker sections, particularly Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.

Role of Media in Democracy

The media keeps people informed, raises public issues, and safeguards people's voices. It is often referred to as the "fourth pillar of democracy".

4. Types of Democracy

1. Direct Democracy: Citizens directly participate in most decision-making processes. (e.g., Switzerland)

2. Representative Democracy (Indirect Democracy): People elect their representatives to govern. The government is accountable to the people. (e.g., India)

  • A. Parliamentary Democracy: Members of the Executive are part of the legislature and accountable to it. People elect the legislature. (e.g., India, Canada)
  • B. Presidential Democracy: The Executive is independent of the legislature. The President is elected directly by the people and is accountable to them. (e.g., USA)
Key Concepts of Power
  • Federalism: A system of government in which power and responsibilities are shared between the central and state governments (e.g., India).
  • Sovereignty: Derived from Latin superanus ('supreme'). It refers to the supreme, final, and independent legal authority of a State over its territory and citizens.

5. Democracy in Practice: India's Living Democracy

India is the world's largest democracy, representing an unparalleled exercise in public participation. Its democracy is considered "living" due to its scale, diversity, and citizens' active engagement.

  • Huge representation: On average, a Member of Parliament represents about 25 lakh people (2019 data).
  • Many languages, one election: Elections are conducted in 22 scheduled languages, along with others. Election symbols help citizens who cannot read vote independently.
  • Over one million polling stations: Booths are set up even in remote areas (mountains, forests, islands)—sometimes for a single voter!
  • Thousands of political parties: Over 2,800 registered parties reflect India's vast social and regional diversity.

Democracy at the Grassroots

India operates through a three-tier system: Union, State, and Local governments (Panchayats and Municipalities). The Constitution also provides for Autonomous District Councils (ADCs) in specific north-eastern regions and the PESA Act (1996) for tribal areas to protect local customs.

Case Studies Grassroots Participation:
  • Jethipura Gram Panchayat (Gujarat): Awarded in 2019 for its exemplary performance, organising Special Gram Sabhas to tackle education, sanitation, and welfare.
  • South Manubankul Gram Panchayat (Tripura): Recognised as a "women-friendly panchayat" focusing on women's welfare, self-help groups, health, and livelihoods.

Women and the Right to Vote

In countries like Britain (1928) and the USA (1920), women had to protest for decades to secure voting rights. In contrast, when India's Constitution came into force in 1950, Universal Adult Franchise was immediately extended to all citizens—men and women alike—without prolonged legal battles.

To further empower women, Article 243(d) guarantees not less than one-third reservation for women in Panchayats and Municipalities. As of 2023, many states have increased this to 50% reservation for women in local bodies.

The Right to Information (RTI) Act, 2005:

Enacted through democratic processes, consultations, and pressure from civil society and journalists. It empowers citizens to seek information from government departments, strengthening accountability and legislation (laws made by the government to maintain order).

6. Challenges to Democratic Practices in India

Democracy requires constant care and active participation. India faces several challenges:

  • Illiteracy, misinformation, and inequality.
  • The spread of fake news, especially through social media, leading to confusion and conflict.
  • Poverty, regionalism, and gender inequality creating barriers to equal participation.
The Emergency (1975–77):

A major challenge was recorded when a National Emergency was imposed in June 1975 due to internal disturbance. Fundamental Rights were suspended, press censored, and leaders arrested. Mass movements led by Jayaprakash Narayan (Lok Nayak) mobilised students and citizens. When the Emergency was lifted in 1977, the defeat of the ruling government demonstrated the immense resilience and strength of Indian democracy.

Democracy and You

Being well-informed is a vital responsibility. Programmes like the National Service Scheme (NSS) and National Cadet Corps (NCC) instill civic responsibility. This includes respecting others' rights, using social media responsibly, and abiding by laws.
