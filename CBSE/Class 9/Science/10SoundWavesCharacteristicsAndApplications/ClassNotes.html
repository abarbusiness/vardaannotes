Class Notes: Sound Waves

Class 9 Science Chapter 10 - Exhaustive Exam Master Notes

1. Production of Sound

  • Core Principle: Sound is a form of energy produced by the vibration of objects (rapid to-and-fro motion about a mean position).
  • Proof of Vibration (Activity 10.2): A vibrating tuning fork prong touching a water surface creates immediate ripples and splashes, proving physical prongs vibrate.
  • Human Voice Box (Larynx): Sound in humans is produced by two vocal cords stretched across the larynx. Air forced from lungs through the narrow slit causes vocal cords to vibrate.
Figure 10.3: Vocal Cords in Humans (Open during breathing vs Closed/vibrating during sound creation)
Exam Definition

Vibration (Oscillation): The periodic to-and-fro motion of an object about its position of rest.

2. Propagation of Sound & Medium Requirement

  • Mechanism: Vibrating objects push nearby medium particles, creating alternating regions of high and low pressure.
  • Compression (C): Region of high pressure and high density created when the source moves forward.
  • Rarefaction (R): Region of low pressure and low density created when the source moves backward.
  • CRITICAL EXAM FACT: Medium particles do not travel from source to ear; only the disturbance (wave energy) travels. Each particle oscillates locally about its mean position.
  • Medium Requirement: Sound is a mechanical wave and requires a material medium (solid, liquid, gas). It cannot travel in a vacuum.
Figures 10.7 & 10.9: Vacuum Bell Jar Experiment (sound needs medium) & Piston Air Density Propagation Model
High-Yield Exam Reasoning Questions
  • Q: Why can two astronauts on the moon not hear each other directly?
    Ans: Because there is a vacuum on the Moon (no air medium). Sound is a mechanical wave requiring a material medium to propagate.
  • Q: Explain the Bell Jar Experiment.
    Ans: As air is pumped out from a bell jar containing a ringing electric bell, the sound becomes fainter and stops completely in a near vacuum, proving sound requires a medium.

3. Longitudinal Waves vs Transverse Waves

Parameter Longitudinal Waves (e.g. Sound in Air) Transverse Waves (e.g. Light, String Waves)
Particle Displacement Particles oscillate parallel ($\leftrightarrow$) to direction of wave propagation. Particles oscillate perpendicular ($\updownarrow$) to direction of wave propagation.
Wave Components Consists of Compressions (C) and Rarefactions (R). Consists of Crests (peaks) and Troughs (valleys).
Medium Requirement Must require a material medium (Mechanical wave). Can propagate without medium in vacuum (Non-mechanical EM wave).
Figures 10.12 & 10.13: Particle oscillation in Longitudinal Waves (parallel) vs Transverse Waves (perpendicular)
Exam Fact: Seismic Waves

Earthquakes produce both longitudinal P-waves (detected first by seismographs because they travel faster) and transverse S-waves through terrestrial rock layers.

Graph Terms to Remember

5. 5 Key Characteristics of Sound Waves

5.1 Wavelength ($\lambda$)

Distance between two consecutive Compressions (C) or Rarefactions (R) (or consecutive Crests/Troughs). SI Unit: Metre (m).

Figure 10.18: (a) Long Wavelength (Low Frequency) vs (b) Short Wavelength (High Frequency)

5.2 Frequency ($\nu$)

Number of complete density oscillations (or compressions passing a point) per unit time. SI Unit: Hertz (Hz) ($1\text{ Hz} = 1\text{ s}^{-1}$).

5.3 Time Period ($T$)

Time taken for one complete density oscillation. SI Unit: Second (s).

$$ \nu = \frac{1}{T} \quad \iff \quad T = \frac{1}{\nu} $$

5.4 Amplitude ($A$)

Magnitude of maximum disturbance/change in density from mean value. Determines Loudness (Higher amplitude = Louder sound).

Figure 10.20: (a) Low Amplitude (Soft Sound) vs (b) High Amplitude (Loud Sound)

5.5 Wave Speed Formula

$$ v = \lambda \times \nu \quad \implies \quad \text{Speed} = \text{Wavelength} \times \text{Frequency} $$

5.6 Intensity vs. Loudness (Crucial Exam Distinctions)

Parameter Intensity of Sound Loudness of Sound
Definition Amount of sound energy passing through unit area per second. Subjective ear perception response to sound.
Nature Objective measurable quantity ($I \propto A^2$). Subjective; depends on both energy and ear sensitivity.
Unit $\text{Watts per square metre (W/m}^2\text{)}$. Decibels (dB).

5.7 Pitch, Loudness & Timbre Comparison

Characteristic Depends On Exam Note / Examples
Pitch Frequency ($\nu$) High frequency = high pitch (shrill voice, e.g. whistling, bird chirping, female voice). Low frequency = low pitch (deep voice, e.g. lion roar, drum beat, male voice).
Loudness Amplitude ($A$) Loudness $\propto \text{Amplitude}^2$. Striking a table harder imparts more kinetic energy $\to$ higher amplitude $\to$ louder sound.
Timbre (Quality) Waveform shape (Overtones) Distinguishes two instruments playing same pitch and loudness. Single frequency = Tone; mixture of frequencies = Note. An Octave is a frequency doubling ($200\text{ Hz} \to 400\text{ Hz}$).

6. Speed of Sound in Media & Factors Affecting Speed

  • State of Matter: $\text{Speed in Solids} > \text{Liquids} > \text{Gases}$. (Steel $\approx 5000\text{ m/s}$, Water $\approx 1500\text{ m/s}$, Air $\approx 344\text{ m/s}$).
  • Temperature Effect: Speed of sound increases as temperature increases ($331\text{ m/s}$ at $0^\circ\text{C}$ vs $344\text{ m/s}$ at $22^\circ\text{C}$).
  • Humidity Effect: Sound travels faster in humid air than in dry air.
  • Sonic Boom: Object traveling at supersonic speed ($> \text{speed of sound}$) creates shock waves in air causing an explosive sound (sonic boom).
Exam Reasoning Question

Q: Why is lightning flash seen before thunder is heard during a storm?
Ans: Because light travels at an extraordinarily high speed ($c = 3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$), whereas sound in air travels much slower ($\approx 340\text{ m/s}$). Hence, light reaches us almost instantaneously, while sound takes a noticeable time delay.

7. Reflection of Sound, Echo & Reverberation

  • Laws of Reflection: Angle of incidence equals angle of reflection ($\angle i = \angle r$); Incident ray, reflected ray, and normal lie in same plane.
  • Echo: Repetition of sound caused by reflection from a distant hard obstacle.
    Condition for Echo: Persistence of hearing $= 0.1\text{ s}$.
    Minimum distance to reflecting surface $d_{\text{min}} = \frac{v \times t}{2} = \frac{344 \times 0.1}{2} = \mathbf{17.2\text{ m}}$ (at $22^\circ\text{C}$).
  • Reverberation: Persistence of sound due to repeated multiple reflections in a hall (time gap $< 0.05\text{ s}$).
    How to reduce: Use sound-absorbing materials like rough plaster, curtains, compressed fibreboard, and upholstered seats.
  • Multiple Reflection Applications: Megaphones (conical directional tube), Stethoscopes (sound reflects through tube), Curved ceiling/soundboard in auditoriums.

8. Audible Range & Ultrasound Applications

  • Human Audible Range: $20\text{ Hz}$ to $20,000\text{ Hz}$ ($20\text{ kHz}$).
  • Infrasonic ($< 20\text{ Hz}$): Earthquakes, rhinoceroses ($5\text{ Hz}$), elephants, whales.
  • Ultrasonic ($> 20\text{ kHz}$): Bats, dolphins, porpoises.
5 Must-Learn Applications of Ultrasound
  1. Industrial Cleaning: Detaches grease and dirt from spiral tubes & odd-shaped electronic parts.
  2. Flaw Detection: Detects internal cracks in metal blocks used in building construction.
  3. Echocardiography: Maps heart wall motion and valve functions.
  4. Medical Ultrasonography: Scans internal organs (liver, kidney, uterus/fetus).
  5. Lithotripsy: Breaks kidney stones into microscopic grains.

10. Structure of the Human Ear

Figure 10.24: Schematic diagram of Human Ear Anatomy
Ear Section Key Components Function in Hearing
Outer Ear Pinna, Auditory Canal, Tympanic Membrane (Eardrum) Pinna collects sound; eardrum vibrates inward on compression & outward on rarefaction.
Middle Ear Hammer (Malleus), Anvil (Incus), Stirrup (Stapes) Three tiny lever bones amplify eardrum vibrations several times.
Inner Ear Cochlea, Auditory Nerve Cochlea converts amplified pressure variations into electrical signals sent to brain.

11. Master Exam Formula & Numerical Solving Guide

Quick Numerical Formula Sheet
  • Wave Speed: $v = \lambda \times \nu = \frac{\lambda}{T}$  |  Time Period: $T = \frac{1}{\nu}$
  • Echo & SONAR Distance: $d = \frac{v \times t}{2}$  |  Total Round-Trip Distance: $2d = v \times t$
  • Frequency: $\nu = \frac{\text{Number of oscillations}}{\text{Time taken}}$
