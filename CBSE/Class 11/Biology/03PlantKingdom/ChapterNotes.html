PLANT KINGDOM

Chapter Overview

The Plant Kingdom (Kingdom Plantae) includes all multicellular, eukaryotic, photosynthetic organisms that have cellulosic cell walls and store starch as reserve food. Plants are broadly classified into: Algae → Bryophytes → Pteridophytes → Gymnosperms → Angiosperms, representing an evolutionary progression from aquatic to fully terrestrial life with increasing complexity.

Reference Books: NCERT Class 11 Biology (kebo103.pdf) | B.R. Vashist – A Textbook of Botany for BSc | Raven, Evert & Eichhorn – Biology of Plants

1. Algae

Algae are chlorophyll-bearing, thalloid, autotrophic organisms. They are largely aquatic (both fresh water and marine), but can also be found in moist terrestrial habitats. The study of algae is called Phycology (or Algology). Algae lack roots, stems, and leaves — they are simply a thallus (undifferentiated body).

Key Characteristics of Algae
  • Body form (thallus): Unicellular (e.g., Chlamydomonas), colonial (e.g., Volvox), filamentous (e.g., Spirogyra, Ulothrix), or parenchymatous (e.g., Sargassum)
  • Habitat: Freshwater, marine, moist soil, tree bark, thermal springs, snowfields (cryophytes)
  • Nutrition: Autotrophic (photosynthetic); some endophytic or epizoic
  • Cell wall: Made of cellulose; some have pectin (e.g., diatoms have siliceous cell wall — frustules)
  • Reserve food: Starch (Chlorophyceae), Mannitol / Laminarin (Phaeophyceae), Floridean starch (Rhodophyceae)
  • Reproduction: Vegetative, Asexual (zoospores), and Sexual (isogamy, anisogamy, oogamy)

⚡ Memory Trick — Algae Pigments

"Green Algae are GREEN because they have both Chl-a and Chl-b (same as land plants)."

"Brown Algae are BROWN because of FUCOXANTHIN — it masks the green colour."

"Red Algae are RED because of r-PHYCOERYTHRIN — they live in deeper water where red light still penetrates."

Remember: Rhodophyceae → Red → r-Phycoerythrin. Red algae can harvest blue/green light that penetrates deep water.

1.2 Reproduction in Algae

Types of Reproduction
  • Vegetative: Fragmentation (e.g., Spirogyra)
  • Asexual: By zoospores (flagellated, motile spores) e.g., Chlamydomonas; or aplanospores (non-motile)
  • Sexual reproduction — Three types:
    • Isogamy: Fusion of morphologically identical (equal-sized) flagellated gametes. e.g., Ulothrix, Spirogyra
    • Anisogamy: Fusion of morphologically dissimilar (unequal-sized) flagellated gametes. e.g., Eudorina
    • Oogamy: Fusion of a large, non-motile egg (oogonium) with a small, motile sperm (antheridium). e.g., Volvox, Fucus and all higher plants

Fig. 3.6 — Flowchart of sexual reproduction in algae: Isogamy (Ulothrix), Anisogamy (Eudorina), and Oogamy (Volvox)

1.3 Economic Importance of Algae

  • Food for aquatic animals: Primary producers in aquatic ecosystems (phytoplankton)
  • Food for humans: Porphyra, Laminaria, Sargassum — eaten in Asia; Chlorella — protein-rich food supplement
  • Agar-agar: Obtained from Gelidium and Gracilaria (Red algae) — used as culture medium in microbiology, food industry (jellies, ice cream)
  • Algin: From Brown algae — used as industrial thickener in ice cream, paints, cosmetics
  • Carrageenan: From Red algae — used as emulsifier in chocolate milk, salad dressings
  • Diatomite (Kieselguhr): Siliceous frustules of diatoms — used in filtration, insulation, abrasive
  • O₂ production: ~50% of total O₂ on earth produced by algae (blue-green algae + green algae)
  • N₂ fixation: Nostoc, Anabaena (Cyanobacteria/BGA) fix atmospheric N₂ — used as biofertilizer in paddy fields

2. Bryophytes

Bryophytes are called the "Amphibians of the Plant Kingdom" because they can live on land but require water for sexual reproduction. They are the first true land plants, representing a major evolutionary step from aquatic algae to terrestrial vascular plants.

General Characteristics
  • Habitat: Moist, shady places; damp rocks, tree trunks, forest floors
  • Body: Thalloid (liverworts) or differentiated into stem-like and leaf-like structures (mosses) — but NOT true roots, stems, or leaves
  • Root-like structures: Rhizoids (unicellular in liverworts, multicellular in mosses) — for anchorage and absorption
  • No vascular tissue (no xylem, no phloem) — transport by osmosis and diffusion
  • Life cycle: Dominant phase = Gametophyte (n). Sporophyte (2n) is dependent on gametophyte
  • Alternation of generations: Heteromorphic (gametophyte and sporophyte look different)
  • Water needed for fertilisation (flagellated antherozoids swim to archegonium)
  • Spore dispersal: Spores are the main unit of dispersal; germinate to form protonema

Fig. 3.2 — Bryophyte life cycles: Marchantia (liverwort) and Funaria (moss) showing gametophyte and sporophyte stages

2.1 Classification of Bryophytes

A. Liverworts (Class Hepaticae)

  • Body: Flat, ribbon-like, dorsiventral thallus (e.g., Marchantia) OR leafy (e.g., Porella)
  • Rhizoids: Unicellular, smooth-walled or tuberculate
  • Asexual reproduction: By Gemmae — small, lens-shaped vegetative propagules in gemma cups on dorsal surface of thallus
  • Sexual reproduction: Antheridiophores (male) and archegoniophores (female) — umbrella-like structures arising from thallus in Marchantia
  • Sporophyte: Consists of foot, seta (stalk), and capsule
  • Examples: Marchantia, Riccia, Porella, Pellia

B. Mosses (Class Musci)

  • Body: More differentiated — stem-like main axis with spirally arranged leaf-like appendages
  • Protonema stage: Spores germinate to form a filamentous protonema (resembles algae), then develop into leafy gametophore
  • Rhizoids: Multicellular, branched
  • Asexual reproduction: By fragmentation, secondary protonema from rhizoids
  • Sporophyte: More elaborated — foot, seta, and capsule with columella (sterile central column). Capsule has peristome teeth for hygroscopic spore dispersal
  • Examples: Funaria, Polytrichum, Sphagnum
Special Notes — Sphagnum (Peat Moss)
  • Sphagnum is economically very important — it forms peat (coal precursor)
  • Has large hyaline cells (dead cells) that absorb water like a sponge
  • Used as packing material for transporting living plants
  • Acidic pH — used as antiseptic dressing material
  • Peat is used as fuel in Scotland and Ireland

2.2 Alternation of Generations in Bryophytes

Fig. 3.7 — Life cycle of Bryophyte (Marchantia/Moss): alternation of generations showing dominant haploid gametophyte and dependent diploid sporophyte

Ecology & Economic Importance of Bryophytes
  • Pioneer plants: First to colonise bare rocks — help in soil formation (ecological succession)
  • Soil erosion prevention: Mosses form mat-like cover that holds soil
  • Water holding: Sphagnum holds 20× its dry weight in water — important in water conservation
  • Peat formation: Sphagnum → peat → coal (over millions of years)
  • Indicators: Very sensitive to air pollution — used as bio-indicators of SO₂ pollution
  • Medicine: Marchantia — used in treatment of liver complaints (hence "Hepaticae"); Sphagnum — natural antiseptic (used in WWI as wound dressing)

3. Pteridophytes

Pteridophytes are the first true vascular land plants. They have true roots, stems, and leaves with a well-developed vascular system (xylem and phloem). They are also called "Vascular Cryptogams" (no seeds) or "Botanical Snakes". The study of pteridophytes is called Pteridology.

General Characteristics
  • Habitat: Moist, shady terrestrial places; some aquatic (e.g., Azolla, Marsilea)
  • Body: Sporophyte is the dominant phase — differentiated into true roots, stem, and leaves
  • Vascular tissue: Present (xylem + phloem), but xylem lacks vessels (only tracheids)
  • Leaves: Two types: Microphylls (small, single-veined — in Selaginella, Equisetum) and Macrophylls/Megaphylls (large, complex-veined — in ferns)
  • Sporophylls: Leaves bearing sporangia; may be aggregated into strobili/cones
  • Spore type: Homosporous (one type of spore, e.g., Dryopteris) or Heterosporous (two types — microspores & megaspores, e.g., Selaginella, Salvinia)
  • Gametophyte: Very small, inconspicuous — called prothallus (free-living, photosynthetic)
  • Fertilisation: Still requires water (motile antherozoids)
  • NO seeds formed — spores are dispersal units
  • Significance of heterospory: First step towards seed habit — retained megaspore = seed precursor

3.2 Heterospory and Seed Habit

Highly Important — NEET

Heterospory = production of two morphologically distinct types of spores

  • Microspore (smaller) → Male gametophyte (microgametophyte)
  • Megaspore (larger) → Female gametophyte (megagametophyte)

Examples of heterosporous pteridophytes: Selaginella, Salvinia, Azolla, Marsilea, Isoetes

Significance: In some pteridophytes, the megaspore germinates while still retained inside the megasporangium — this is the precursor to seed habit. This is the crucial evolutionary link to Gymnosperms.

3.3 Stele Types in Pteridophytes (from B.R. Vashist)

The central vascular cylinder in pteridophyte stems is called the stele. Types include:

  • Protostele: Simplest — solid core of xylem surrounded by phloem (e.g., Psilotum, Lycopodium)
  • Siphonostele: Hollow stele with pith in centre (e.g., Osmunda, Adiantum)
  • Dictyostele: Siphonostele with leaf gaps — appears as dissected stele (e.g., Dryopteris)
  • Polystele / Atactostele: Multiple vascular bundles scattered (e.g., Selaginella)

3.4 Economic Importance of Pteridophytes

  • Food: Rhizomes of Marsilea eaten in India; fiddleheads of some ferns eaten
  • Ornamental: Adiantum (Maidenhair fern), Asplenium, Nephrolepis
  • Biofertilizer: Azolla (water fern) harbours Anabaena azollae (nitrogen-fixing cyanobacterium) — used as biofertilizer in rice paddies
  • Medicine: Equisetum — diuretic, used in kidney stones; Lycopodium powder — used in pharmacy as dusting powder
  • Coal formation: Ancient pteridophytes (Carboniferous period) → coal deposits

4. Gymnosperms

The word Gymnosperm comes from Greek: gymnos = naked + sperma = seed. Hence gymnosperms are plants with naked seeds (seeds not enclosed in fruits — no ovary/fruit wall). They are the first seed-bearing plants (spermatophytes).

General Characteristics
  • Habit: Mostly evergreen trees or shrubs; some arborescent (tree-like)
  • Dominant phase: Sporophyte (2n) — the large tree we see
  • Gametophyte: Greatly reduced — retained within sporophyte (endosporic); dependent on sporophyte
  • Roots: Well-developed tap root system; some have coralloid roots (Cycas) — symbiotic with Anabaena/Nostoc (cyanobacteria) for N₂ fixation
  • Leaves: Often needle-like or scale-like — xerophytic adaptations (reduced transpiration)
  • Heterosporous: Produce microspores (in microsporangia on microsporophylls) and megaspores (in megasporangia/ovules on megasporophylls)
  • Ovule: Exposed/naked — NOT enclosed in ovary. The integument is the outer covering
  • No double fertilization (unlike angiosperms)
  • Pollination: Mostly anemophilous (wind pollinated)
  • Pollen tube: Carries non-motile male gametes to egg (exception: Cycas and Ginkgo have motile sperms — multiflagellate)
  • Xylem: Has only tracheids (no vessels, except Gnetum)
  • Phloem: No companion cells (only sieve cells)

4.2 Cycas — In Detail

Cycas — Special Features (Frequently asked in NEET & School Exams)
  • Habit: Palm-like, dioecious (male and female plants separate)
  • Roots: (i) Normal tap roots (ii) Coralloid roots — specialised negatively geotropic (apogeotropic) root pockets harbouring symbiotic cyanobacteria (Anabaena, Nostoc) → N₂ fixation
  • Stem: Columnar, unbranched, covered with persistent leaf bases
  • Leaves: Two types: Scale leaves (brown, protective) and foliage leaves (large, pinnate — feather-like)
  • Male cone: Compact strobilus of microsporophylls with abaxial microsporangia
  • Female cone: No true female cone — loose collection of megasporophylls (unlike all other gymnosperms)
  • Ovule: Largest in plant kingdom; integuments form outer fleshy layer (orange/red) and inner stony layer
  • Sperms: Largest and most complex motile sperms in plant kingdom (multiflagellate)
  • Endosperm: Present (formed before fertilization = primary endosperm = haploid female gametophyte tissue)

4.3 Pinus — In Detail

Pinus — Special Features
  • Habit: Tall, evergreen, monoecious (male and female cones on same plant)
  • Leaves: Two types: (i) Scale leaves — small, brown, on long shoots; (ii) Foliage needles — green, borne in clusters (fascicles) on dwarf shoots
  • Leaf number in fascicle: Varies by species — 2 needles (P. sylvestris), 3 needles (P. radiata), 5 needles (P. strobus)
  • Resin canals: Present in stem, leaves, and roots — source of turpentine and rosin
  • Male cone (Microsporangiate strobilus): Numerous microsporophylls, each with 2 microsporangia; pollen grains are winged (saccate) — two air bladders for wind dispersal
  • Female cone (Megasporangiate strobilus): Large woody cone; each scale has 2 ovules on upper surface
  • Pollination to fertilisation: Takes about 13 months (long gap)
  • Prothallial cells: Remnant of male gametophyte
  • Seed: Winged — dispersed by wind
Economic Importance of Gymnosperms
  • Timber: Pinus, Cedrus (deodar), Abies — important sources of wood
  • Paper & Resin: Pinus — turpentine (from resin) used in paints, varnish, polish; rosin for violins
  • Edible seeds: Cycas, Pinus (pine nuts / chironji), Ginkgo
  • Medicine: Ephedra → ephedrine (bronchodilator for asthma); Ginkgo biloba → memory enhancer, blood circulation
  • Ornamental: Thuja, Juniperus, Cryptomeria
  • Food: Sago starch from Cycas revoluta (trunk pith)
  • N₂ fixation: Coralloid roots of Cycas

5. Angiosperms

Angiosperms are the most evolved, dominant, and most diverse group of plants on Earth. The word means "enclosed seed" (Greek: angio = vessel/container + sperma = seed). The key feature is that ovules are enclosed within an ovary, which matures into a fruit after fertilisation. They are also called Flowering Plants.

Distinguishing Features of Angiosperms
  • Flowers: Reproductive organs — attract pollinators; enclosed ovary → fruit
  • Fruits: Seeds enclosed within fruit (pericarp) — aids dispersal, protects seeds
  • Double fertilisation: Unique to angiosperms — one sperm fuses with egg (syngamy → zygote), another fuses with polar nuclei (triple fusion → triploid primary endosperm nucleus)
  • Endosperm: Formed after fertilisation — triploid (3n) — provides nutrition to embryo
  • Male gametophyte: 3-celled pollen grain (tube cell nucleus + 2 male gametes) — smallest male gametophyte in plant kingdom
  • Female gametophyte: 7-celled, 8-nucleate embryo sac (megagametophyte) — also highly reduced
  • Xylem: Has both tracheids AND vessels (more efficient water transport)
  • Phloem: Has sieve tube cells AND companion cells
  • Leaves: Broad, flat, with complex venation; large surface area for photosynthesis
  • Range: From tiny Wolffia (1-2 mm, smallest flowering plant) to tall Eucalyptus (100m)

5.2 Life Cycle of Angiosperms (Alternation of Generations)

Dominant Sporophyte Phase
  • Sporophyte (2n): The plant body — roots, stem, leaves, flowers, fruits
  • Microsporangia in anthers → Meiosis → Microspores → Pollen grains (male gametophyte, 3-celled)
  • Megasporangia (ovules) → Meiosis → Megaspores → Embryo sac (female gametophyte, 7-celled, 8-nucleate)
  • Pollination → Pollen tube grows into ovule
  • Double fertilisation:
    • Sperm 1 + Egg (n) → Zygote (2n) → Embryo
    • Sperm 2 + 2 Polar nuclei (n+n) → Primary Endosperm Nucleus (3n) → Endosperm
  • Ovule → Seed; Ovary → Fruit

6. Plant Life Cycles and Alternation of Generations

All plants show alternation of generations — a regular alternation between a haploid gametophyte phase (n) and a diploid sporophyte phase (2n). The relative dominance of each phase varies across plant groups.

6.1 Types of Life Cycles

Life Cycle Type Dominant Phase Found In
Haplontic Gametophyte (n); Sporophyte reduced to zygote only Most algae (Volvox, Spirogyra, Chlamydomonas)
Diplontic Sporophyte (2n); Gametophyte reduced to few cells Most Phaeophyceae (Fucus), All Gymnosperms, All Angiosperms
Haplo-diplontic
(Intermediate)		 Both phases multicellular and free-living (though one may be larger) Bryophytes (gametophyte dominant), Pteridophytes (sporophyte dominant); Some algae (Ectocarpus, Ulva)

6.2 Summary — Dominant Phases Across Plant Kingdom

  • Algae: Gametophyte (n) dominant [Haplontic cycle]
  • Bryophytes: Gametophyte (n) dominant [Haplo-diplontic cycle] — sporophyte dependent
  • Pteridophytes: Sporophyte (2n) dominant [Haplo-diplontic cycle] — gametophyte free-living (prothallus)
  • Gymnosperms: Sporophyte (2n) dominant [Diplontic cycle] — gametophyte fully retained inside sporophyte
  • Angiosperms: Sporophyte (2n) dominant [Diplontic cycle] — most reduced gametophytes

8. School Examination Practice Questions

📝 1 MARK

Q1. What is the common name of Phaeophyceae?

Answer: Brown Algae. The brown colour is due to the pigment Fucoxanthin.

📝 2 MARKS

Q2. Why are bryophytes called "Amphibians of the Plant Kingdom"?

Answer: Bryophytes are called amphibians of the plant kingdom because:

  • They can live on land (moist, shady terrestrial habitats)
  • But they require water for sexual reproduction — the flagellated antherozoids (sperms) need a film of water to swim to the archegonium (egg)

This dual existence — land living but water-dependent for reproduction — mirrors the amphibians among animals.

Q4. What is heterospory? Why is it significant? Give two examples of heterosporous pteridophytes.

Heterospory: The production of two morphologically distinct types of spores — smaller microspores (giving rise to male gametophyte) and larger megaspores (giving rise to female gametophyte).

Significance:

  • It is the first step in the evolution of seed habit
  • Retention of megaspore within the megasporangium = enclosed female gametophyte = precursor to seed
  • Ensures better nourishment for the developing embryo

Examples: Selaginella and Salvinia

📝 5 MARKS

Q5. Write the salient features of Gymnosperms. Give two examples each of: (a) Coniferales, (b) Cycadales.

Salient features of Gymnosperms:

  • Naked seeds — ovules NOT enclosed in ovary; no fruits formed
  • Heterosporous — produce microspores and megaspores
  • Wind pollinated; pollen tube carries non-motile gametes (exception: Cycas has motile sperms)
  • Dominant sporophyte; reduced, endosporic gametophyte
  • No double fertilisation; endosperm haploid (formed before fertilisation)
  • Xylem has tracheids (no vessels, except Gnetum)
  • Phloem lacks companion cells (only sieve cells)

(a) Coniferales: Pinus (Chir Pine), Cedrus (Deodar)

(b) Cycadales: Cycas (Sago Palm), Zamia

9. Additional NEET Important Points & Tricky Facts

🔑 NEET KEY FACTS

Algae Facts

• Chlorella and Spirulina — used as food/protein supplement in space research
• Dunaliella — produces β-carotene (anti-oxidant, Vitamin A precursor)
• Diatoms — chief producers of O₂; their diatomite used in dynamite (Nobel's discovery)
• Laminaria — source of iodine and algin
• Sargassum — forms Sargasso Sea (Atlantic Ocean); pelagic (floating) brown alga

Bryophyte Facts

• In mosses, the protonema is the juvenile stage that resembles filamentous algae
• Sporophyte of bryophytes obtains nutrition from gametophyte through a foot (haustorial absorption)
• In Riccia — simplest sporophyte (only capsule, no seta)
• In Funaria — sporophyte has all 3 parts: foot + seta + capsule
• Peristome teeth in moss capsule show hygroscopic movement for spore dispersal

Pteridophyte Facts

• Fern gametophyte = Prothallus — heart-shaped, 1-cell thick, independent, photosynthetic
• Sori in ferns = clusters of sporangia on undersurface of leaves (fronds)
• Indusium = protective covering over sorus
• Equisetum has jointed stem and whorled microphylls — stem has silica deposits
• Psilotum — most primitive vascular plant; no true roots/leaves (only rhizoids)

Gymnosperm Facts

• Cycas — the only gymnosperm without true female cone (loose megasporophylls)
• Welwitschia — produces only 2 leaves throughout its life (500-1000 years); desert plant
• Ginkgo biloba — "Living fossil"; fan-shaped (bilobed) leaves; dioecious; used for memory enhancement
• Sequoia giganteum (Giant Sequoia) — tallest known gymnosperm
• Pollen grains of Pinus are winged (bisaccate) — 2 air bladders for buoyancy in wind

Angiosperm Facts

• Wolffia — smallest angiosperm (1-2 mm); aquatic
• Eucalyptus — tallest angiosperm (100 m)
• Embryo sac = 7-celled, 8-nucleate (3 antipodal + 2 polar nuclei + 1 egg + 2 synergids)
• Largest seed: Lodoicea maldivica (Double coconut / Coco de Mer)
• Largest inflorescence: Amorphophallus titanum; Largest flower: Rafflesia (parasitic)
• Trapa (Water chestnut) — submerged plant but flowers above water

Plant Kingdom Evolution: "All Big Plants Generally Advance"
→ Algae → Bryophytes → Pteridophytes → Gymnosperms → Angiosperms

For Algae Classes (3 colours): "Green algae have Green Chl-a+b | Brown algae have Brown Fucoxanthin | Red algae have Red Phycoerythrin"

Bryophytes features: NAWL
→ No vascular tissue | Amphibians of plant kingdom | Water needed for fertilisation | Land plants (first true)

Gymnosperms — 4 features: NHAW
→ Naked seeds | Heterosporous | Anemophilous (wind pollinated) | Woody (all trees/shrubs)

Angiosperms ONLY feature: "Angiosperms DOUBLE everything" — Double fertilisation, Double endosperm origin (3n)

🎯 NEET 2019

Q. Identify the CORRECT match for the ploidy of different stages in the life cycle of a plant:
(a) Endosperm of Angiosperm — 3n
(b) Endosperm of Gymnosperm — 3n
(c) Prothallus of Pteridophyte — 2n
(d) Spores of Bryophyte — 2n

Answer: (a) Endosperm of Angiosperm = 3n ✓

  • Gymnosperm endosperm = n (haploid — formed before fertilisation from female gametophyte)
  • Pteridophyte prothallus = n (haploid gametophyte)
  • Bryophyte spores = n (produced by meiosis from sporophyte)
  • Angiosperm endosperm = 3n ✓ (sperm n + 2 polar nuclei n+n = 3n)
