BIOMOLECULES

Chapter Overview

Biomolecules are the chemical compounds found in living organisms. This chapter covers the chemical analysis of cells, types of biomolecules (carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids), their structure, and the detailed study of enzymes. This is one of the most high-yield chapters for NEET UG.

Reference: NCERT Class 11 Biology (kebo109.pdf) | Lehninger — Principles of Biochemistry | B.R. Vashist — Biochemistry

1. Chemical Analysis of Cells

To determine what chemicals are present in living tissue, a chemical analysis procedure is used:

Analytical Method
  1. Take living tissue → grind in trichloroacetic acid (TCA) → get a slurry
  2. Filter → Filtrate = Acid-soluble fraction (small molecules: amino acids, glucose, water, ions, nucleotides)
  3. Retentate = Acid-insoluble fraction (macromolecules: proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides, lipids)

The biomolecules in the acid-insoluble fraction are called Biomacromolecules — they have molecular weights in thousands to millions of Daltons.

2. Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones or compounds that produce them on hydrolysis. General formula: (CH₂O)ₙ. They are the most abundant biomolecules on Earth.

2.1 Classification of Carbohydrates

A. Monosaccharides (Simple Sugars)

Monosaccharides
  • Cannot be hydrolysed further into simpler sugars
  • Classified by: number of carbons (triose C₃, tetrose C₄, pentose C₅, hexose C₆) and functional group (aldose = aldehyde; ketose = ketone)
  • Key examples:
    • Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) — Aldohexose; most important energy source; occurs as D and L forms
    • Fructose (C₆H₁₂O₆) — Ketohexose; isomer of glucose; found in fruits, honey
    • Galactose (C₆H₁₂O₆) — Aldohexose; component of lactose
    • Ribose (C₅H₁₀O₅) — Aldopentose; component of RNA
    • Deoxyribose (C₅H₁₀O₄) — Aldopentose; component of DNA (one oxygen less than ribose)
  • Reducing sugars: Have free aldehyde/ketone group → reduce Benedict's or Fehling's solution (e.g., glucose, fructose, galactose, maltose, lactose)
  • Non-reducing sugars: No free aldehyde/ketone (e.g., Sucrose — glycosidic bond between C1 of glucose and C2 of fructose blocks both anomeric carbons)

B. Oligosaccharides

  • 2–10 monosaccharide units joined by glycosidic bonds (condensation/dehydration)
  • Disaccharides (2 units):
    • Sucrose = Glucose + Fructose (α1→β2 glycosidic bond) — table sugar; non-reducing
    • Maltose = Glucose + Glucose (α1→4 bond) — malt sugar; reducing
    • Lactose = Galactose + Glucose (β1→4 bond) — milk sugar; reducing
    • Cellobiose = Glucose + Glucose (β1→4 bond) — unit of cellulose
    • Trehalose = Glucose + Glucose — insect blood sugar
  • Trisaccharide: Raffinose (Galactose + Glucose + Fructose) — in sugar beet

3. Proteins

Proteins are the most abundant organic molecules in living cells (~50% of dry weight). They are polymers of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. They are the most diverse class of biomolecules in structure and function.

3.1 Amino Acids — Building Blocks of Proteins

Structure of Amino Acids
  • Each amino acid has: Central carbon (α-C) bonded to:
    • Amino group (—NH₂)
    • Carboxyl group (—COOH)
    • Hydrogen atom (—H)
    • R group (side chain) — variable; determines the type and properties of amino acid
  • There are 20 standard amino acids that make up proteins
  • Essential amino acids (cannot be synthesised in the body; must be taken in diet): PVTTIMHLL — F → Phenylalanine, Valine, Threonine, Tryptophan, Isoleucine, Methionine, Histidine, Leucine, Lysine + sometimes Arginine
  • Non-essential amino acids: Can be synthesised by the body (e.g., Glycine, Alanine, Glutamine, Serine)

3.2 Peptide Bond

Peptide Bond Formation

A peptide bond is a covalent bond formed between the carboxyl group (—COOH) of one amino acid and the amino group (—NH₂) of another amino acid with the release of one water molecule (condensation reaction).

  • A chain of amino acids linked by peptide bonds = polypeptide
  • A protein has a free N-terminus (H₂N—) and a free C-terminus (—COOH)
  • Peptide bond has partial double bond character (resonance) — planar, rigid, and resistant to rotation
  • Protein sequence is always written N → C terminus

4. Lipids

Lipids are NOT true macromolecules (most are not polymers). They are insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents (chloroform, ether, benzene). They are grouped together due to their hydrophobic nature.

4.1 Classification of Lipids

Types of Lipids

A. Fatty Acids

  • Long chain hydrocarbons with a terminal carboxyl group (—COOH)
  • Saturated fatty acids: No double bonds; maximum H atoms (solid at room temp) — e.g., Palmitic acid (C16), Stearic acid (C18)
  • Unsaturated fatty acids: One or more double bonds (C=C) — liquid at room temp (oils) — e.g., Oleic acid (C18:1), Linoleic acid (C18:2 — essential), Linolenic acid (C18:3 — essential)
  • Essential fatty acids: Linoleic acid and Linolenic acid — cannot be synthesised in body

B. Triglycerides (Fats and Oils)

  • Glycerol + 3 Fatty Acids linked by ester bonds
  • Fats = solid at room temp (saturated fatty acids) | Oils = liquid at room temp (unsaturated)
  • Function: Energy storage (9 kcal/g — highest energy density), insulation, protection

C. Phospholipids

  • Glycerol + 2 Fatty acids + Phosphate + Alcohol (choline, serine, ethanolamine)
  • Amphipathic — hydrophilic head (phosphate group) + hydrophobic tail (fatty acid chains)
  • Form bilayers — basis of all biological membranes
  • Examples: Lecithin (phosphatidylcholine), Cephalin (phosphatidylethanolamine)

D. Steroids (Sterols)

  • Based on steroid nucleus (four fused carbon rings)
  • Cholesterol — in animal cell membranes; precursor for bile acids, steroid hormones (cortisol, testosterone, oestrogen, aldosterone), Vitamin D
  • Plant sterols: Sitosterol, Stigmasterol

E. Waxes

  • Long chain fatty acid + long chain alcohol (ester bond)
  • Function: Waterproofing (cuticle of plants, beeswax, earwax)
⚡ Memory Trick — Lipids

"Lipids are SOS — Soluble in Organic Solvents" (not water)

"Phospholipids are AMP — AMPhipathic" — one end loves water, other end hates it → perfect for membranes

Energy order: Fats (9 kcal/g) > Proteins (4 kcal/g) = Carbohydrates (4 kcal/g)

5. Nucleic Acids

Nucleic acids are polymers of nucleotides (polynucleotides). They store and transmit genetic information and direct protein synthesis. Two types: DNA (Deoxyribonucleic acid) and RNA (Ribonucleic acid).

5.1 Nucleotide Structure

Components of a Nucleotide

Each nucleotide = Phosphate group + Sugar + Nitrogenous base

  • Sugar: Ribose (in RNA) or Deoxyribose (in DNA) — pentose sugar
  • Nitrogenous bases:
    • Purines (double ring): Adenine (A), Guanine (G) — present in both DNA and RNA
    • Pyrimidines (single ring): Cytosine (C), Thymine (T) — only in DNA; Uracil (U) — only in RNA (replaces Thymine)
  • Nucleoside = Sugar + Base (no phosphate)
  • Nucleotide = Sugar + Base + Phosphate
  • Nucleotides are linked by 3'→5' phosphodiester bonds

5.3 Structure of DNA — Watson and Crick Model (1953)

DNA Double Helix

Proposed by James Watson and Francis Crick (1953), based on X-ray crystallography data of Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins.

  • Two polynucleotide strands coiled around each other in a right-handed double helix
  • Strands are antiparallel — one runs 5'→3', the other 3'→5'
  • Sugar-phosphate backbone on the outside; bases on the inside
  • Chargaff's rules: A = T and G = C (A + G = C + T, i.e., purines = pyrimidines)
  • Base pairing: A–T (2 hydrogen bonds) and G–C (3 hydrogen bonds)
  • Helical parameters: Pitch = 3.4 nm per complete turn; base pairs per turn = 10; distance between adjacent base pairs = 0.34 nm
  • Diameter of helix = 2 nm
  • Major groove and minor groove visible in B-form DNA
  • Forms: B-DNA (physiological, right-handed); A-DNA (dehydrated, right-handed); Z-DNA (left-handed)

6. Enzymes — Detailed Study

Enzymes are biological catalysts — they speed up biochemical reactions without being consumed. Almost all enzymes are proteins (exceptions: Ribozymes — catalytic RNA molecules). Enzymes lower the activation energy of reactions.

Key Enzyme Terminology
  • Substrate (S): The molecule on which an enzyme acts
  • Active site: The specific region of the enzyme where the substrate binds; has two regions: binding site (for substrate) and catalytic site (where reaction occurs)
  • Enzyme-Substrate Complex (ES): Temporary complex formed when substrate binds to active site
  • Product (P): The result of the enzyme-catalysed reaction
  • Activation energy: The minimum energy required to initiate a reaction — enzymes lower this barrier
  • Cofactors: Non-protein components required by some enzymes for activity:
    • Prosthetic groups: Tightly/covalently bound cofactors (e.g., haem in peroxidase, biotin in pyruvate carboxylase)
    • Coenzymes: Loosely, transiently bound organic cofactors — usually vitamins or their derivatives (e.g., NAD⁺, FAD, CoA, ATP)
    • Metal ions: Act as cofactors (e.g., Zn²⁺ in carbonic anhydrase, Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ in cytochromes, Mg²⁺ in phosphatases)
  • Apoenzyme: Protein part of enzyme (inactive without cofactor)
  • Holoenzyme: Apoenzyme + Cofactor = Complete, active enzyme

6.1 Mechanism of Enzyme Action

A. Lock and Key Model (Emil Fischer, 1894)

  • The active site of an enzyme is rigid and pre-shaped like a lock
  • Only a specific substrate (the key) fits perfectly into the active site
  • Explains enzyme specificity but does not explain why closely related substrates can sometimes be acted upon

B. Induced Fit Model (Daniel Koshland, 1958)

  • The active site is flexible — not rigid
  • When the substrate approaches, the active site changes shape (induces fit) to accommodate the substrate — like a hand fitting into a glove
  • More widely accepted — explains broader substrate specificity and allosteric enzymes
📷 Fig. 9.7 (NCERT) Enzyme-substrate complex formation — showing (a) Lock and Key model and (b) Induced Fit model; active site, substrate, ES complex, and product formation NCERT Figure 9.7

6.2 Enzyme Reaction Steps

Step-by-step Mechanism
  1. Substrate (S) binds to active site of enzyme (E) → forms ES complex
  2. Chemical bonds in substrate are broken/formed → EP complex (enzyme-product)
  3. Product (P) is released; enzyme is regenerated unchanged

E + S ⇌ ES → EP → E + P

  • Turnover number (kcat): Number of substrate molecules converted to product per enzyme molecule per second (Catalytic efficiency)
  • Enzymes do not alter equilibrium — they only speed up reaching equilibrium from both directions

6.3 Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity

📷 Fig. 9.8 (NCERT) Graphs showing effect of (a) Temperature, (b) pH, (c) Substrate concentration on enzyme activity — showing optimum temperature/pH, Vmax, and Km NCERT Figure 9.8 — combine all three graphs

A. Effect of Temperature

  • Enzyme activity increases with temperature up to an optimum temperature (usually 37°C for human enzymes; ~60–80°C for thermophilic bacteria)
  • Beyond optimum: Denaturation — H-bonds and other non-covalent interactions break → active site loses shape → activity falls to zero
  • Q₁₀ value = activity at (T + 10°C) / activity at T°C ≈ 2 (for most enzymes)

B. Effect of pH

  • Each enzyme has an optimum pH at which activity is maximum
  • Pepsin (stomach): optimum pH ~2 (acidic) | Trypsin (intestine): optimum pH ~8 (alkaline) | Most intracellular enzymes: ~7.2–7.4
  • Extreme pH → ionisation changes in R groups → disruption of H-bonds → denaturation

C. Effect of Substrate Concentration

  • As substrate concentration increases → enzyme activity increases (more ES complexes formed)
  • At Vmax (maximum velocity): All active sites are saturated with substrate — further increase in [S] has no effect
  • Km (Michaelis constant): Substrate concentration at which enzyme velocity = ½ Vmax
  • Low Km = High affinity of enzyme for substrate | High Km = Low affinity

6.4 Enzyme Inhibition

TypeDescriptionEffect on Km & VmaxExample
Competitive Inhibition Inhibitor resembles substrate; competes for active site; reversible; overcome by high [S] Km ↑ (apparent); Vmax unchanged Malonate inhibits succinate dehydrogenase; Sulfa drugs inhibit bacterial DHPS
Non-competitive Inhibition Inhibitor binds to site other than active site (allosteric site); changes enzyme conformation; cannot be overcome by ↑[S] Km unchanged; Vmax ↓ Cyanide inhibits cytochrome oxidase; heavy metals (Pb²⁺, Hg²⁺)
Uncompetitive Inhibition Inhibitor binds only to ES complex (not free enzyme) Both Km ↓ and Vmax ↓ Rare; seen in multi-substrate reactions
Irreversible Inhibition Inhibitor permanently inactivates enzyme (covalent bond) Vmax ↓ (cannot be reversed) Organophosphates (nerve agents) inhibit acetylcholinesterase; DFP, PCMB

7. School Examination Practice Questions

📝 1 MARK

Q1. What is a coenzyme? Give one example.

Answer: A coenzyme is an organic, non-protein molecule that loosely associates with an enzyme and is essential for its catalytic activity. Coenzymes are usually derived from vitamins.

Example: NAD⁺ (Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide) — derived from Niacin (Vitamin B₃); acts as hydrogen/electron carrier in oxidoreductase reactions.

📝 3 MARKS

Q3. Describe the four levels of protein structure.

  1. Primary structure: Linear sequence of amino acids joined by peptide bonds (N→C direction). Any change here = different protein (e.g., sickle cell haemoglobin).
  2. Secondary structure: Local folding of polypeptide backbone due to hydrogen bonds → α-helix or β-pleated sheet.
  3. Tertiary structure: Overall 3D shape of a single polypeptide chain; maintained by H-bonds, disulfide bridges, hydrophobic interactions, ionic bonds.
  4. Quaternary structure: Association of two or more polypeptide subunits (protomers) — only in multi-subunit proteins. Example: Haemoglobin (2α + 2β subunits).
📝 5 MARKS

Q4. Explain the effect of temperature, pH, and substrate concentration on enzyme activity. Draw graphs to illustrate.

Temperature: Activity rises with temperature to an optimum (~37°C in humans); beyond this, enzyme denatures and activity drops. Thermophiles have optima at 60–80°C.

pH: Each enzyme has an optimum pH. Pepsin works at pH 2; Trypsin at pH 8; salivary amylase at pH 6.8. Extreme pH causes denaturation.

Substrate concentration: Activity increases with [S] until Vmax is reached (all active sites saturated). The Km is the [S] at ½Vmax — measure of enzyme-substrate affinity.
