Chemical Bonding & Molecular Structure

1. Kössel-Lewis Approach & Octet Rule

Core Concept

Lewis Octet Rule: Atoms combine either by transfer of valence electrons (gaining or losing) or by sharing valence electrons in order to attain a stable octet ($8\mathrm{e^-}$) in their outermost shell (duplet $2\mathrm{e^-}$ for Hydrogen).

Fig. 4.1 Covalent sharing and Lewis dot representations of $\mathrm{Cl_2}$, $\mathrm{H_2O}$, and $\mathrm{CCl_4}$.

1.1 Formal Charge Calculation (100% Board Exam Guaranteed)

The formal charge ($FC$) of an atom in a polyatomic ion or molecule is defined as:

Formula $$\text{Formal Charge } (FC) = V - L - \frac{1}{2}S$$

Where: $V = \text{Valence } \mathrm{e^-}$ of isolated atom, $L = \text{Lone pair (non-bonding) } \mathrm{e^-}$, $S = \text{Shared (bonding) } \mathrm{e^-}$.

✍ SCHOOL EXAM CLASSIC — Formal Charge of Ozone ($\mathrm{O_3}$)

Q. Calculate formal charge on each oxygen atom in Ozone ($\mathrm{O_3}$): $\mathrm{O_{(1)} = O_{(2)} - O_{(3)}}$.

Atom 1 (Central $\mathrm{O}$): $V = 6$, $L = 2$, $S = 6 \implies FC = 6 - 2 - \frac{1}{2}(6) = \mathbf{+1}$.
Atom 2 (Double-bonded $\mathrm{O}$): $V = 6$, $L = 4$, $S = 4 \implies FC = 6 - 4 - \frac{1}{2}(4) = \mathbf{0}$.
Atom 3 (Single-bonded $\mathrm{O}$): $V = 6$, $L = 6$, $S = 2 \implies FC = 6 - 6 - \frac{1}{2}(2) = \mathbf{-1}$.

1.2 Limitations of the Octet Rule (3-Mark Board Favorite)

Limitation Explanation Key Standard Examples
1. Incomplete Octet Central atom has fewer than 8 valence electrons. $\mathrm{LiCl}$ ($2\mathrm{e^-}$), $\mathrm{BeH_2}$ ($4\mathrm{e^-}$), $\mathrm{BF_3, BCl_3}$ ($6\mathrm{e^-}$)
2. Odd-Electron Molecules Total valence electrons is odd, so octet cannot be satisfied for all atoms. Nitric oxide ($\mathrm{NO}$), Nitrogen dioxide ($\mathrm{NO_2}$)
3. Expanded Octet Central atom (Period 3+) uses vacant $d$-orbitals, accommodating $> 8$ electrons. $\mathrm{PF_5}$ ($10\mathrm{e^-}$), $\mathrm{SF_6}$ ($12\mathrm{e^-}$), $\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$ ($12\mathrm{e^-}$)

2. Ionic Bond, Lattice Enthalpy & Fajans' Rules

Conditions Favoring Ionic Bond Formation
  1. Low Ionization Enthalpy of the metal atom (easier cation formation).
  2. High Negative Electron Gain Enthalpy of the non-metal atom (easier anion formation).
  3. High Lattice Enthalpy of the resulting ionic crystal lattice.

2.1 Lattice Enthalpy & Born-Haber Cycle

Lattice Enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{lattice}}H^\circ$): Energy required to completely separate 1 mole of a solid ionic compound into gaseous constituent ions (or energy released when 1 mole ionic solid forms from gaseous ions).

Fig. 4.2 Born-Haber Cycle for Solid Sodium Chloride ($\mathrm{NaCl}$). $\Delta_f H^\circ = \Delta_{\text{sub}}H + \frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\text{bond}}H + \Delta_i H + \Delta_{eg}H + U_L$.

2.2 Fajans' Rules (Covalent Character in Ionic Bonds)

No bond is 100% ionic. Cation distorts electron cloud of anion — a phenomenon called Polarization.

Fajans' Rules Summary
  • Covalent Character Increases When:
    • Cation is small (high polarizing power).
    • Anion is large (easily polarizable).
    • Charges on cation/anion are high.
    • Cation has pseudo-noble gas configuration ($18\mathrm{e^-}$ shell: $ns^2 np^6 nd^{10}$ like $\mathrm{Cu^+, Ag^+}$) vs noble gas configuration ($8\mathrm{e^-}$ shell like $\mathrm{Na^+}$).
✍ REASONING DRILL — Fajans' Rules

Q. Why is $\mathrm{LiI}$ more covalent than $\mathrm{LiF}$? Why is $\mathrm{CuCl}$ more covalent than $\mathrm{NaCl}$?

Ans 1: $\mathrm{I^-}$ is much larger than $\mathrm{F^-}$, making it more easily polarized by $\mathrm{Li^+}$, giving $\mathrm{LiI}$ greater covalent character.
Ans 2: $\mathrm{Cu^+}$ ($3d^{10}$) has a $18\mathrm{e^-}$ pseudo-noble shell which shields nuclear charge less effectively than $\mathrm{Na^+}$ ($2p^6$, $8\mathrm{e^-}$), exerting greater polarizing power.

3. Covalent Bond Parameters & Dipole Moment

3.1 Dipole Moment ($\mu$) & Molecular Geometry

Dipole Moment ($\mu$): $\mu = Q \times r$ (Unit: Debye, $1\text{ D} = 3.3356 \times 10^{-30}\text{ C m}$). It is a vector quantity pointing from $\delta+$ to $\delta-$.

Molecule Geometry Dipole Moment ($\mu$) Exam Reason / Explanation
$\mathrm{CO_2}$ Linear $\mu = 0$ Two equal bond dipoles ($\mathrm{C=O}$) point in opposite directions ($180^\circ$) and cancel.
$\mathrm{BF_3}$ Trigonal Planar $\mu = 0$ Resultant of two $\mathrm{B-F}$ dipoles equals and opposes the third $\mathrm{B-F}$ dipole ($120^\circ$).
$\mathrm{NH_3}$ vs $\mathrm{NF_3}$ Pyramidal $\mathrm{NH_3} (1.47\text{D}) > \mathrm{NF_3} (0.23\text{D})$ In $\mathrm{NH_3}$, orbital dipole of lone pair & $\mathrm{N-H}$ bond dipoles reinforce; in $\mathrm{NF_3}$, $\mathrm{N-F}$ dipoles oppose lone pair dipole.
✍ SCHOOL EXAM CLASSIC — Dipole Moment ($\mathrm{CCl_4}$ vs $\mathrm{CHCl_3}$)

Q. Why is dipole moment ($\mu$) of $\mathrm{CCl_4}$ zero, whereas that of $\mathrm{CHCl_3}$ is non-zero ($1.04\text{ D}$)? How is % ionic character calculated?

Ans 1: $\mathrm{CCl_4}$ is symmetrical tetrahedral; the vector sum of 4 equal $\mathrm{C-Cl}$ bond dipoles cancels out ($\mu = 0$). In $\mathrm{CHCl_3}$, replacement of one $\mathrm{Cl}$ by $\mathrm{H}$ breaks symmetry, producing a net dipole moment ($\mu = 1.04\text{ D}$).
Ans 2 (% Ionic Character Formula): $$\% \text{Ionic Character} = \left(\frac{\mu_{\text{observed}}}{\mu_{\text{theoretical ionic}}}\right) \times 100$$

3.2 Resonance & Resonance Hybrid

Core Concept

Resonance: When a single Lewis structure cannot accurately describe a molecule, two or more canonical structures with identical atomic positions but different electron distributions are drawn. The actual molecule is a resonance hybrid of all canonical forms.

Resonance Bond Order Formula: $\text{Bond Order} = \frac{\text{Total number of bonds between two atoms in all canonical forms}}{\text{Total number of canonical forms}}$.
Example ($\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}$): $\mathrm{C-O}$ Bond Order = $\frac{4}{3} = \mathbf{1.33}$.

4. VSEPR Theory (Shape of Molecules)

Postulates of VSEPR Theory
  1. The shape of a molecule depends on the total number of valence shell electron pairs (bonded and lone pairs) around the central atom.
  2. Electron pairs repel each other and stay as far apart as possible to minimize repulsion.
  3. Order of Repulsive Force: $\mathbf{\text{Lone Pair - Lone Pair (lp-lp) > Lone Pair - Bond Pair (lp-bp) > Bond Pair - Bond Pair (bp-bp)}}$.

✍ SCHOOL EXAM FAVORITE — $\mathrm{PCl_5}$ Axial vs Equatorial Bonds

Q. Why are axial $\mathrm{P-Cl}$ bonds in $\mathrm{PCl_5}$ longer and weaker than equatorial bonds?

Ans: In $\mathrm{PCl_5}$ (Trigonal Bipyramidal), 3 equatorial bonds lie in one plane ($120^\circ$ apart), while 2 axial bonds lie perpendicular ($90^\circ$).
The 2 axial bond pairs experience greater repulsion from 3 equatorial bond pairs at $90^\circ$ compared to equatorial pairs which experience repulsion from only 2 pairs at $90^\circ$. To minimize repulsion, axial bonds stretch, becoming longer and weaker.
Fig. 4.17 Trigonal bipyramidal geometry of $\mathrm{PCl_5}$ showing axial ($90^\circ$, $240\text{ pm}$) and equatorial ($120^\circ$, $202\text{ pm}$) bonds.
✍ 3-MARK BOARD REASONING — Bond Angle Trend ($\mathrm{CH_4 > NH_3 > H_2O}$)

Q. Explain why $\mathrm{CH_4, NH_3}$, and $\mathrm{H_2O}$ all have $sp^3$ central atoms, but their bond angles decrease: $\mathrm{CH_4} (109.5^\circ) > \mathrm{NH_3} (107^\circ) > \mathrm{H_2O} (104.5^\circ)$.

Ans: According to VSEPR theory, electron pair repulsion order is $\mathbf{\text{lp-lp > lp-bp > bp-bp}}$.
1. $\mathrm{CH_4}$ has 0 lone pairs: regular tetrahedral angle ($109.5^\circ$).
2. $\mathrm{NH_3}$ has 1 lone pair: $\text{lp-bp}$ repulsion pushes $\mathrm{N-H}$ bond pairs closer, reducing angle to $107^\circ$.
3. $\mathrm{H_2O}$ has 2 lone pairs: strong $\text{lp-lp}$ repulsion compresses $\mathrm{O-H}$ bond pairs even further, reducing angle to $104.5^\circ$.

6. Hybridisation

Definition & Rules

Hybridisation: The process of intermixing of atomic orbitals of slightly different energies of an atom to form a new set of equivalent orbitals of identical shape and energy called hybrid orbitals.

  • Number of hybrid orbitals formed = Number of atomic orbitals mixed.
  • Hybrid orbitals form stronger $\sigma$ bonds than pure atomic orbitals.
  • Steric Number Formula: $SN = \frac{1}{2} [ V + M - C + A ]$ (where $V=$ valence $\mathrm{e^-}$, $M=$ monovalent atoms, $C=$ cation charge, $A=$ anion charge).

Q. Find the total number of $\sigma$ and $\pi$ bonds in: (a) Ethene ($\mathrm{C_2H_4}$), (b) Ethyne ($\mathrm{C_2H_2}$), (c) Benzene ($\mathrm{C_6H_6}$), and (d) Propadiene ($\mathrm{CH_2=C=CH_2}$).

7. Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT)

Salient Features of MOT
  • Atomic orbitals of comparable energies and proper symmetry combine to form Molecular Orbitals (MOs).
  • Combination of 2 AOs gives 2 MOs: 1 Bonding MO ($\psi = \psi_A + \psi_B$, lower energy) and 1 Antibonding MO ($\psi^* = \psi_A - \psi_B$, higher energy).
  • Bond Order ($BO$): $BO = \frac{N_b - N_a}{2}$ (where $N_b =$ bonding $\mathrm{e^-}$, $N_a =$ antibonding $\mathrm{e^-}$).
  • If $BO > 0$, molecule is stable; if $BO \le 0$, molecule does not exist ($\mathrm{He_2, Be_2}$).
  • If all electrons are paired $\implies$ Diamagnetic; if unpaired electrons present $\implies$ Paramagnetic.
Feature Bonding Molecular Orbital (BMO) Antibonding Molecular Orbital (ABMO)
Wave Function Formed by constructive interference ($\psi = \psi_A + \psi_B$). Formed by destructive interference ($\psi^* = \psi_A - \psi_B$).
Energy & Stability Lower energy than parent AOs $\implies$ High stability. Higher energy than parent AOs $\implies$ Lower stability.
Electron Density High electron density concentrated between the nuclei. Zero electron density between nuclei (possesses a Nodal Plane).

7.1 Two Energy Level Sequences & $2s-2p$ Orbital Mixing

Physical Reason for Energy Sequence Shift ($2s-2p$ Mixing)

In lighter homonuclear diatomics ($Z \le 7$: $\mathrm{Li_2, Be_2, B_2, C_2, N_2}$), the energy gap between $2s$ and $2p$ atomic orbitals is small, leading to significant $2s-2p$ orbital mixing. This pushes the $\sigma 2p_z$ MO higher in energy than the degenerate $\pi 2p_x = \pi 2p_y$ pair. In heavier diatomics ($Z > 7$: $\mathrm{O_2, F_2}$), the $2s-2p$ energy gap is large $\implies$ negligible mixing $\implies \sigma 2p_z$ remains lower in energy than $\pi 2p$.

Energy Order Rule

1. For $Z \le 7$ ($14\mathrm{e^-}$ or fewer: $\mathrm{Li_2, Be_2, B_2, C_2, N_2}$):

$$\sigma 1s < \sigma^* 1s < \sigma 2s < \sigma^* 2s < (\pi 2p_x = \pi 2p_y) < \sigma 2p_z < (\pi^* 2p_x = \pi^* 2p_y) < \sigma^* 2p_z$$

2. For $Z > 7$ (More than $14\mathrm{e^-}$: $\mathrm{O_2, F_2, Ne_2}$):

$$\sigma 1s < \sigma^* 1s < \sigma 2s < \sigma^* 2s < \sigma 2p_z < (\pi 2p_x = \pi 2p_y) < (\pi^* 2p_x = \pi^* 2p_y) < \sigma^* 2p_z$$

✍ MOST FAMOUS BOARD QUESTION — Paramagnetism of Oxygen

Q. Why is $\mathrm{O_2}$ paramagnetic according to MOT while VBT predicts it to be diamagnetic?

Ans: VBT assumes all electrons in $\mathrm{O_2}$ are paired in double bonds ($\mathrm{:O=O:}$), wrongly predicting diamagnetism.
MOT gives electronic configuration: $\sigma 1s^2 \, \sigma^* 1s^2 \, \sigma 2s^2 \, \sigma^* 2s^2 \, \sigma 2p_z^2 \, (\pi 2p_x^2 = \pi 2p_y^2) \, (\pi^* 2p_x^1 = \pi^* 2p_y^1)$. Due to Hund's rule, the 2 highest energy electrons enter degenerate antibonding orbitals $\pi^* 2p_x$ and $\pi^* 2p_y$ singly, proving $\mathrm{O_2}$ has 2 unpaired electrons and is paramagnetic.
✍ HIGH-YIELD BOARD DRILL — Oxygen Series ($\mathrm{O_2^+, O_2, O_2^-, O_2^{2-}}$)

Q. Arrange $\mathrm{O_2, O_2^+, O_2^-, O_2^{2-}}$ in increasing order of bond length and stability.

Bond Orders: $\mathrm{O_2^+} (2.5) > \mathrm{O_2} (2.0) > \mathrm{O_2^-} (1.5) > \mathrm{O_2^{2-}} (1.0)$.
Stability Order ($\propto \text{Bond Order}$): $\mathbf{\mathrm{O_2^{2-}} < \mathrm{O_2^-} < \mathrm{O_2} < \mathrm{O_2^+}}$.
Bond Length Order ($\propto 1/\text{Bond Order}$): $\mathbf{\mathrm{O_2^+} < \mathrm{O_2} < \mathrm{O_2^-} < \mathrm{O_2^{2-}}}$.
✍ HIGH-YIELD BOARD DRILL — Nitrogen Series ($\mathrm{N_2, N_2^+, N_2^-}$)

Q. Calculate bond order of $\mathrm{N_2, N_2^+}$, and $\mathrm{N_2^-}$. Compare their stabilities and magnetic behaviors.

$\mathrm{N_2}$ ($14e^-$): $BO = \frac{10 - 4}{2} = \mathbf{3.0}$. Diamagnetic. Highest stability.
$\mathrm{N_2^+}$ ($13e^-$): Electron removed from bonding $\sigma 2p_z \implies BO = \frac{9 - 4}{2} = \mathbf{2.5}$. Paramagnetic.
$\mathrm{N_2^-}$ ($15e^-$): Electron added to antibonding $\pi^* 2p_x \implies BO = \frac{10 - 5}{2} = \mathbf{2.5}$. Paramagnetic.
Stability Order: $\mathbf{\mathrm{N_2} > \mathrm{N_2^+} > \mathrm{N_2^-}}$. Note: $\mathrm{N_2^+}$ is slightly more stable than $\mathrm{N_2^-}$ despite equal bond order because $\mathrm{N_2^-}$ contains 5 antibonding electrons versus 4 in $\mathrm{N_2^+}$.

8. Hydrogen Bonding

Definition & Conditions

Hydrogen Bond: Electrostatic attraction between hydrogen atom covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom ($\mathrm{F, O, N}$) and another electronegative atom with a lone pair. Energy $\approx 10 - 40\text{ kJ/mol}$.

Type Description & Mechanism Examples & Physical Effect
Intermolecular H-Bond Occurs between different molecules of same or different compounds. $\mathrm{H_2O, HF, NH_3}$, alcohols.
Effect: Raises b.p. ($\mathrm{H_2O}$ liquid while $\mathrm{H_2S}$ gas).
Intramolecular H-Bond Occurs within the same molecule forming a 5/6-membered chelate ring. $o$-nitrophenol, salicylaldehyde.
Effect: Lowers b.p., makes $o$-nitrophenol steam volatile.

✍ BOARD REASONING — $o$-Nitrophenol vs $p$-Nitrophenol

Q. Why does $o$-nitrophenol have a lower boiling point and higher steam volatility than $p$-nitrophenol?

Ans: In $o$-nitrophenol, $\mathrm{-OH}$ and $\mathrm{-NO_2}$ groups are adjacent, forming intramolecular H-bonding (chelate ring within molecule). It does not associate with neighboring molecules.
In $p$-nitrophenol, groups are far apart, forming extensive intermolecular H-bonding (chains between molecules). Thus, $p$-nitrophenol requires more energy to separate molecules $\implies$ higher b.p. and non-volatile in steam.
✍ CLASSIC SCHOOL EXAM QUESTIONS — H-Bonding Anomalies

Q1. Why is $\mathrm{H_2O}$ a liquid while $\mathrm{H_2S}$ is a gas at room temperature?
Q2. Why does ice float on water and why is water's density maximum at $4^\circ\text{C}$?

Ans 1: Oxygen has high electronegativity and small size, forming strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds in $\mathrm{H_2O}$ that associate molecules into liquid state. Sulfur is larger with lower electronegativity, so $\mathrm{H_2S}$ has no H-bonding and remains a gas.
Ans 2: Ice forms a 3D open cage-like structure held by 4 hydrogen bonds per $\mathrm{H_2O}$ molecule, trapping empty space $\implies$ lower density than liquid water (ice floats). At $4^\circ\text{C}$, cage partially collapses, giving maximum density.

9. Rapid Exam Formula & Fact Summary

Concept High-Yield Exam Formula / Key Result Exam Tip
Formal Charge $FC = V - L - \frac{1}{2}S$ Sum of FCs equals total charge on ion.
Dipole Moment $\mu = q \times r$ (Debye, D) $\mu = 0$ for symmetrical molecules ($\mathrm{CO_2, BF_3, CCCl_4}$).
VSEPR Repulsion $\text{lp-lp} > \text{lp-bp} > \text{bp-bp}$ Lone pairs squeeze bond angles ($\mathrm{CH_4} 109.5^\circ \to \mathrm{NH_3} 107^\circ \to \mathrm{H_2O} 104.5^\circ$).
Bond Order (MOT) $BO = \frac{N_b - N_a}{2}$ $BO \propto \text{Bond Enthalpy} \propto \frac{1}{\text{Bond Length}}$.

⚠️ Top 5 Common School Exam Pitfalls & Mistakes
  • Mistake 1 (Shape vs Geometry): Geometry includes all electron pairs (bonded + lone pairs), whereas Molecular Shape describes ONLY atomic positions. Example: $\mathrm{NH_3}$ geometry is Tetrahedral, but its shape is Trigonal Pyramidal.
  • Mistake 2 (MOT Energy Sequence Swap): For $Z \le 7$ ($\mathrm{B_2, C_2, N_2}$), $\pi 2p$ orbitals are LOWER in energy than $\sigma 2p_z$ due to $2s-2p$ mixing. Do NOT use the $\mathrm{O_2}$ order for $\mathrm{N_2}$!
  • Mistake 3 ($\mathrm{C_2}$ Molecule Unique Feature): In $\mathrm{C_2}$ ($12\mathrm{e^-}$), the double bond consists of two $\pi$ bonds (all 4 valence electrons occupy $\pi 2p$), unlike usual double bonds ($1\sigma + 1\pi$).
  • Mistake 4 (TBP Lone Pair Position): In Trigonal Bipyramidal arrangements ($\mathrm{SF_4, ClF_3, XeF_2}$), lone pairs ALWAYS occupy equatorial positions to minimize $90^\circ$ repulsions.
  • Mistake 5 (Boiling Point & H-bonding): Intramolecular H-bonding ($\text{within one molecule}$) lowers boiling point ($o$-nitrophenol), whereas Intermolecular H-bonding ($\text{between molecules}$) raises boiling point ($p$-nitrophenol).
