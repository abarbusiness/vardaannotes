CHEMICAL BONDING: STRUCTURES, VSEPR & HYBRIDISATION (ULTIMATE MASTER GUIDE)

To predict the exact 3D geometry of any molecule, we must understand the evolutionary sequence of chemical theories. The Lewis Dot Structure gave us connectivity but failed at 3D shapes. This necessitated Valence Bond Theory (VBT), which explained orbital overlap. However, to account for actual bond angles and polyatomic molecular shapes, VSEPR Theory and Hybridisation were introduced.

This comprehensive module is designed strictly for Boards, JEE (Main & Advanced), and NEET, covering NCERT fundamentals to J.D. Lee advanced concepts with extensive PYQ analysis.

1. Valence Bond Theory (VBT) - The Foundation

Proposed by Heitler & London (1927) and extended by Linus Pauling, VBT focuses on atomic orbitals and their overlap to form covalent bonds.

Core Postulates Essential Postulates of VBT:
  1. A covalent bond is formed by the partial overlap of two half-filled atomic orbitals possessing electrons with opposite spins.
  2. The overlapping orbitals must have electrons with opposite spins (Pauli Exclusion Principle).
  3. The greater the extent of overlap, the stronger the bond formed and the lower the energy of the system.
  4. The strength of a covalent bond depends on the extent of overlapping: $s-s < s-p < p-p$ overlap.
  5. The direction of the covalent bond coincides with the direction of maximum overlap.

1.1 Types of Orbital Overlapping (NCERT Core + JEE/NEET Critical)

Figure 1.1: Types of Orbital Overlaps (s-s, s-p, p-p axial σ and p-p lateral π)

A. Sigma (σ) Bond - The Primary Bond

  • Formation: Formed by axial (head-on/end-to-end) overlap along the internuclear axis.
  • Types of σ Overlap:
    • $s-s$ overlap: H₂ molecule
    • $s-p$ overlap: HCl molecule
    • $p-p$ overlap: F₂, Cl₂, O₂, N₂ (one σ bond)
  • Characteristics:
    • Strongest type of covalent bond due to maximum overlap
    • Cylindrically symmetrical about the bond axis
    • Free rotation possible around σ bond axis
    • Electron density is maximum along the internuclear axis

B. Pi (π) Bond - The Secondary Bond

  • Formation: Formed by lateral (sideways/parallel) overlap of perpendicular $p$ or $d$ orbitals.
  • Types of π Overlap:
    • $p-p$ overlap: Present in C₂H₄ (ethene), C₂H₂ (ethyne)
    • $d-d$ overlap: Present in some transition metal complexes
    • $p-d$ overlap: Present in compounds like SO₂, NO₂⁻
  • Characteristics:
    • Weaker than σ bond due to lesser overlap
    • Forms parallel electron clouds above and below the plane
    • No free rotation - rotation would break the π bond
    • Electron density is maximum above and below the internuclear axis
    • Always exists along with a σ bond - never alone
JEE Advanced Concept Strength Comparison (Critical for Numericals):
Bond dissociation energy follows: σ bond > π bond

Example Analysis:
• C-C single bond (1σ): 348 kJ/mol
• C=C double bond (1σ + 1π): 614 kJ/mol
• C≡C triple bond (1σ + 2π): 839 kJ/mol

Therefore: Energy of one π bond ≈ 614 - 348 = 266 kJ/mol
Energy of two π bonds ≈ 839 - 348 = 491 kJ/mol

JEE PYQ Application: This concept is tested in thermochemistry and reaction mechanism questions where you need to calculate bond energy changes.

1.2 Resonance - The Reality of Electron Delocalization

Figure 1.2: Resonance Structures and Delocalization in Benzene, Carbonate Ion, and Ozone
Resonance Principles Key Principles of Resonance:
• When a single Lewis structure cannot explain all properties of a molecule, two or more canonical structures are drawn.
• The actual molecule is a resonance hybrid of all canonical forms.
• Resonance increases stability (Resonance Energy = $E_{\text{hybrid}} - E_{\text{most stable canonical}}$).

1. Benzene (C₆H₆):
• Kekulé structures interconvert via $\pi$ electron delocalization.
• All C-C bond lengths are equal (139 pm, intermediate between single 154 pm and double 134 pm).
• Bond order = 1.5.

2. Carbonate Ion (CO₃²⁻):
• Has three equivalent resonance structures.
• All C-O bonds are identical (129 pm).
• Each C-O bond has partial double bond character (1.33 bond order).
• Bond order = $\frac{4}{3}$ for each C-O bond.

3. Ozone (O₃):
• Has two equivalent resonance structures.
• Bond angle: 117° ($sp^2$ hybridization with one lone pair).
• O-O bond length: 128 pm (intermediate between O-O single: 148 pm and O=O double: 121 pm).
• Bond order: 1.5 for each O-O bond.

1.3 Failure of Simple VBT (Why Hybridisation Was Born)

Critical Understanding The Crisis that VBT Could Not Solve:

Problem 1: Carbon's Tetravalency
• Ground state electronic configuration of C: $1s^2 2s^2 2p_x^1 2p_y^1$
• Only 2 half-filled orbitals → Should form only 2 bonds (divalent)
• But experimentally, Carbon is tetravalent!
• Even with excitation to $1s^2 2s^1 2p_x^1 2p_y^1 2p_z^1$, we have a problem...

Problem 2: Identical Bonds in CH₄
• If C uses 1 $s$-orbital and 3 $p$-orbitals for bonding:
  → One C-H bond (using $s$-orbital) should be different from other three (using $p$-orbitals)
  → Three H atoms should be at 90° (perpendicular $p$-orbitals)
• But experimentally in CH₄:
  → All 4 C-H bonds are absolutely identical (same length: 109 pm, same energy)
  → Bond angle is 109°28', NOT 90°!
  → Tetrahedral geometry, NOT planar

Problem 3: NH₃ and H₂O Bond Angles
• If N and O use pure $p$-orbitals for bonding, bond angles should be 90°
• But experimental values:
  → NH₃: 107° (not 90°)
  → H₂O: 104.5° (not 90°)

Conclusion: Pure VBT fails to explain observed molecular geometries. This led to the development of Hybridisation Theory by Linus Pauling.

2. VSEPR Theory - Predicting Molecular Geometry

Figure 2.1: Comprehensive VSEPR Geometries & Shapes Chart (Steric Number 2 to 6)

The Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) theory, developed by Sidgwick and Powell (1940) and refined by Nyholm and Gillespie (1957), predicts 3D molecular geometry based on electron pair repulsions.

Fundamental Postulates Core Principles of VSEPR Theory:
  1. The geometry of a molecule depends on the total number of electron pairs (bonding + lone pairs) in the valence shell of the central atom.
  2. Electron pairs arrange themselves to achieve maximum separation (minimum repulsion).
  3. The repulsion order is critical:
    $lp-lp > lp-bp > bp-bp$
    where $lp$ = lone pair, $bp$ = bond pair
  4. A multiple bond is treated as a single electron pair for geometry prediction.
  5. Lone pairs occupy more space than bond pairs (localized on one atom vs. shared between two).

3. Hybridisation - The Ultimate Solution (Linus Pauling's Masterpiece)

Definition Hybridisation is the process of intermixing of atomic orbitals of slightly different energies belonging to the same atom, to produce an equal number of entirely new orbitals of equivalent energy, identical shape, and maximum symmetry, called hybrid orbitals.

3.1 Fundamental Rules of Hybridisation (Memorize These!)

Golden Rules Strict Rules You MUST Know:
  1. Same Atom Rule: Only orbitals from the same atom can hybridize (never orbitals from different atoms).
  2. Conservation Rule: Number of hybrid orbitals formed = Number of pure atomic orbitals mixed.
  3. Energy Rule: All hybrid orbitals have exactly the same energy (degenerate).
  4. Sigma Bond Rule: Hybrid orbitals form ONLY σ bonds or accommodate lone pairs. They NEVER form π bonds!
  5. Pi Bond Rule: π bonds are always formed by pure, unhybridized $p$ or $d$ orbitals.
  6. Promotion NOT Essential: Excitation/promotion of electrons is NOT always necessary (e.g., NH₃, H₂O hybridize in ground state).
  7. Empty Orbital Participation: Even empty orbitals can participate in hybridization (e.g., BF₃, coordination compounds).
  8. Orbital Mixing Condition: Only orbitals with comparable energies can hybridize (e.g., 2s with 2p, NOT 1s with 2p).
  9. Geometry Prediction: Type of hybridization determines the geometry of arrangement of hybrid orbitals, but VSEPR determines actual molecular shape (accounting for lone pairs).

3.2 Characteristics of Hybrid Orbitals

Figure 3.1: Comparative Chart of $sp$, $sp^2$, and $sp^3$ Hybrid Orbitals
  • Shape: Hybrid orbitals are more directional than pure atomic orbitals → better overlap → stronger bonds
  • Orientation: Hybrid orbitals arrange themselves to minimize repulsion (same principle as VSEPR)
  • Equivalency: All hybrid orbitals of the same type are completely equivalent
  • Bond Formation: Form only σ bonds by overlapping with:
    • Half-filled orbitals of other atoms
    • Hybrid orbitals of other atoms
  • Lone Pair Accommodation: Can accommodate lone pairs of electrons

4. Types of Hybridisation - Complete Classification

4.1 sp Hybridisation (Linear)

sp Details Formation & Characteristics:

Mixing: 1 $s$ + 1 $p$ orbital (usually $p_z$)
Result: 2 equivalent $sp$ hybrid orbitals
Remaining: 2 pure $p$ orbitals ($p_x$ and $p_y$) available for π bonding
s-character: 50% (maximum among all hybridizations)
p-character: 50%
Angle between orbitals: 180°
Geometry: Linear
Bond Angle: 180°

Key Examples of sp Hybridisation:

Examples 1. BeCl₂ (Beryllium Chloride)
• Be ground state: $1s^2 2s^2$ (no unpaired electrons!)
• Excited state: $1s^2 2s^1 2p^1$ (promotion required)
• Hybridization: $sp$ (mix $2s$ + $2p_z$)
• 2 $sp$ orbitals form 2 σ bonds with Cl
• Geometry: Linear | Bond angle: 180°
• NEET Note: BeCl₂ is linear in vapor phase (covalent), but polymeric in solid state

2. C₂H₂ (Acetylene/Ethyne)
• Each C: $1s^2 2s^1 2p_x^1 2p_y^1 2p_z^1$ (excited state)
• Hybridization: $sp$ (mix $2s$ + $2p_z$)
• Each C forms:
  → 1 C-H σ bond (using one $sp$ orbital)
  → 1 C-C σ bond (using one $sp$ orbital)
  → 2 C-C π bonds (using $2p_x$ and $2p_y$)
• Triple bond: 1σ + 2π
• H-C-C angle: 180° (linear)
• JEE Numerical: C-C bond length in ethyne: 120 pm (shortest)

3. CO₂ (Carbon Dioxide)
• C: $sp$ hybridized
• Forms 2 C=O double bonds (each: 1σ + 1π)
• O=C=O angle: 180°
• Total π bonds: 2
• Why is CO₂ non-polar? Linear shape → dipole moments cancel

4. HCN (Hydrogen Cyanide)
• C: $sp$ hybridized
• N: $sp$ hybridized
• H-C≡N structure
• C-N triple bond: 1σ + 2π
• Bond angle: 180°

4.2 sp² Hybridisation (Trigonal Planar)

sp² Details Formation & Characteristics:

Mixing: 1 $s$ + 2 $p$ orbitals (usually $p_x$ and $p_y$)
Result: 3 equivalent $sp^2$ hybrid orbitals
Remaining: 1 pure $p$ orbital ($p_z$) available for π bonding
s-character: 33.33% (one-third)
p-character: 66.67% (two-thirds)
Angle between orbitals: 120°
Geometry: Trigonal Planar
Bond Angle: 120° (ideal)

Key Examples of sp² Hybridisation:

Examples 1. BF₃ (Boron Trifluoride)
• B ground state: $1s^2 2s^2 2p^1$
• Excited state: $1s^2 2s^1 2p_x^1 2p_y^1$
• Hybridization: $sp^2$
• 3 $sp^2$ orbitals form 3 σ bonds with F
• F-B-F angle: 120°
• Geometry: Trigonal planar
• Lewis Acid: B has empty $p_z$ orbital → can accept electron pair
• JEE Trick: BF₃ is planar but BF₃·NH₃ has B in $sp^3$ (tetrahedral)

2. C₂H₄ (Ethene/Ethylene)
• Each C: $sp^2$ hybridized
• Each C forms:
  → 2 C-H σ bonds (using two $sp^2$ orbitals)
  → 1 C-C σ bond (using one $sp^2$ orbital)
  → 1 C-C π bond (using $2p_z$ orbital)
• All 6 atoms lie in the same plane
• H-C-H angle: ~117° (slightly less than 120° due to C=C repulsion)
• C-C bond length: 134 pm
• No free rotation around C=C bond (π bond restricts)

3. BCl₃ (Boron Trichloride)
• Similar to BF₃
• $sp^2$ hybridization
• Trigonal planar
• Bond angle: 120°

4. Carbonate Ion (CO₃²⁻)
• C: $sp^2$ hybridized
• 3 $sp^2$ orbitals form 3 σ bonds with O
• Remaining $p_z$ forms π bond (delocalized over 3 O atoms - resonance)
• All 4 atoms in same plane
• O-C-O angle: 120°
• All C-O bonds equivalent (resonance)
• Bond order: 1.33 for each C-O bond

5. Graphite Structure
• Each C: $sp^2$ hybridized
• Forms hexagonal layers
• 3 σ bonds with neighboring C atoms
• Delocalized π electrons (one $p_z$ per C) → electrical conductivity
• NEET Favorite: Why graphite conducts electricity? Delocalized π electrons!

4.3 sp³ Hybridisation (Tetrahedral)

sp³ Details Formation & Characteristics:

Mixing: 1 $s$ + 3 $p$ orbitals ($p_x$, $p_y$, $p_z$)
Result: 4 equivalent $sp^3$ hybrid orbitals
Remaining: NO pure $p$ orbitals (all used up)
s-character: 25% (one-fourth)
p-character: 75% (three-fourths)
Angle between orbitals: 109°28' (109.5°)
Geometry: Tetrahedral
Bond Angle: 109.5° (ideal, when all are bond pairs)

Key Examples of sp³ Hybridisation:

Examples 1. CH₄ (Methane) - The Perfect Tetrahedral
• C ground state: $1s^2 2s^2 2p_x^1 2p_y^1$
• Excited state: $1s^2 2s^1 2p_x^1 2p_y^1 2p_z^1$
• Hybridization: $sp^3$
• All 4 $sp^3$ orbitals form σ bonds with H
• H-C-H angle: 109.5° (perfect tetrahedral)
• All C-H bonds: identical (109 pm length)
• Solves the VBT crisis! All bonds are equivalent
• Non-polar molecule (symmetrical)

2. NH₃ (Ammonia) - Pyramidal Shape
• N ground state: $1s^2 2s^2 2p_x^1 2p_y^1 2p_z^1$
• Hybridization: $sp^3$ (NO excitation needed!)
• 3 $sp^3$ orbitals form σ bonds with H
• 1 $sp^3$ orbital contains lone pair
• Electron Geometry: Tetrahedral (arrangement of all 4 electron pairs)
• Molecular Shape: Trigonal Pyramidal (considering only atoms)
• H-N-H angle: 107° (less than 109.5° due to $lp-bp$ repulsion)
• Polar molecule
• JEE Concept: Why pyramidal? Lone pair occupies more space → compresses H-N-H angle

3. H₂O (Water) - Bent/V-Shape
• O ground state: $1s^2 2s^2 2p_x^2 2p_y^1 2p_z^1$
• Hybridization: $sp^3$ (NO excitation needed!)
• 2 $sp^3$ orbitals form σ bonds with H
• 2 $sp^3$ orbitals contain lone pairs
• Electron Geometry: Tetrahedral
• Molecular Shape: Bent/Angular/V-shaped
• H-O-H angle: 104.5° (significantly less than 109.5°)
• Reason: 2 lone pairs → maximum $lp-lp$ repulsion → H-O-H compressed
• Highly polar molecule
• NEET Key Point: Bent shape → net dipole moment → high polarity → hydrogen bonding

4. NH₄⁺ (Ammonium Ion) - Perfect Tetrahedral
• N: $sp^3$ hybridized
• All 4 $sp^3$ orbitals form σ bonds with 4 H atoms
• NO lone pairs
• H-N-H angle: 109.5° (perfect tetrahedral)
• How 4 bonds with N having only 3 unpaired electrons?
  → One bond is coordinate/dative bond (NH₃ donates lone pair to H⁺)

5. CCl₄ (Carbon Tetrachloride)
• C: $sp^3$ hybridized
• 4 C-Cl σ bonds
• Cl-C-Cl angle: 109.5°
• Tetrahedral, non-polar (symmetrical)

6. Diamond Structure
• Each C: $sp^3$ hybridized
• Forms 4 σ bonds with 4 neighboring C atoms
• 3D tetrahedral network
• Extremely hard (all strong C-C σ bonds)
• Electrical insulator (no free electrons - all in σ bonds)
JEE/NEET Comparison Bond Angle Trend in sp³ Hybridization (Most Frequently Asked!):

Molecule Hybridization bp lp Shape Bond Angle Reasoning
CH₄ sp³ 4 0 Tetrahedral 109.5° Perfect - no lone pairs
NH₃ sp³ 3 1 Trigonal Pyramidal 107° 1 lp reduces angle by ~2.5°
H₂O sp³ 2 2 Bent 104.5° 2 lp → maximum lp-lp repulsion

Critical Observation: As number of lone pairs increases, bond angle decreases!
JEE Mantra: $CH_4 > NH_3 > H_2O$ (bond angle order)

4.4 sp³d Hybridisation (Trigonal Bipyramidal)

sp³d Details Formation & Characteristics:

Mixing: 1 $s$ + 3 $p$ + 1 $d$ orbital (specifically $d_{z^2}$)
Result: 5 equivalent $sp^3d$ hybrid orbitals
Geometry: Trigonal Bipyramidal
Two Types of Positions:
• Axial positions: 2 orbitals along vertical axis (formed by $p_z$ + $d_{z^2}$)
  → 0% s-character
  → Angle with equatorial: 90°
• Equatorial positions: 3 orbitals in horizontal plane (formed by $s$ + $p_x$ + $p_y$)
  → 33.3% s-character
  → Angle between equatorial: 120°
Critical JEE Point: Axial and equatorial positions are NOT equivalent!

Bent's Rule for sp³d (EXTREMELY IMPORTANT FOR JEE ADVANCED)

Figure 4.2: Axial vs Equatorial Positions in $sp^3d$ Hybridisation & Bent's Rule
Bent's Rule - Master This! Bent's Rule: Atomic orbitals with more s-character form bonds with less electronegative atoms. Atomic orbitals with less s-character form bonds with more electronegative atoms.

Application in Trigonal Bipyramidal Geometry:

Rule 1: More electronegative atoms/groups prefer AXIAL positions (0% s-character)
Rule 2: Lone pairs and multiple bonds prefer EQUATORIAL positions (33.3% s-character - more stable)

Why?
• Lone pairs need more space (high electron density)
• Equatorial position has only ONE 90° angle with other equatorial positions
• Axial position has THREE 90° angles with equatorial positions
• Therefore, lone pair at equatorial position experiences less repulsion

Consequence:
• If molecule has different surrounding atoms → most electronegative goes to axial first
• If molecule has lone pairs → they occupy equatorial positions first

Key Examples of sp³d Hybridisation:

Examples 1. PCl₅ (Phosphorus Pentachloride) - Perfect TBP
• P: $[Ne] 3s^2 3p_x^1 3p_y^1 3p_z^1$
• Excited state: $[Ne] 3s^1 3p_x^1 3p_y^1 3p_z^1 3d_{z^2}^1$
• Hybridization: $sp^3d$
• All 5 positions occupied by Cl
• Shape: Trigonal Bipyramidal
• Bond lengths NOT equal!
  → Axial P-Cl: 219 pm (longer, weaker)
  → Equatorial P-Cl: 204 pm (shorter, stronger)
• JEE Question: Why are axial bonds longer?
  → Greater repulsion from 3 equatorial bonds at 90°

2. PF₅ (Phosphorus Pentafluoride)
• Similar to PCl₅
• Axial P-F bonds longer than equatorial P-F bonds

3. SF₄ (Sulfur Tetrafluoride) - See-Saw Shape
• S: $sp^3d$ hybridized
• 4 bond pairs + 1 lone pair
• Lone pair position: EQUATORIAL (Bent's Rule!)
• Shape: See-Saw (distorted TBP)
• Bond angles:
  → F(equatorial)-S-F(equatorial): <120° (compressed by lp)
  → F(axial)-S-F(axial): <180° (bent due to lp repulsion)
• Polar molecule

4. ClF₃ (Chlorine Trifluoride) - T-Shaped
• Cl: $sp^3d$ hybridized
• 3 bond pairs + 2 lone pairs
• Lone pair positions: BOTH EQUATORIAL (Bent's Rule!)
• Shape: T-shaped
• Bond angles:
  → F(axial)-Cl-F(axial): <180° (~87°)
  → F(equatorial)-Cl-F(axial): <90° (~87.5°)
• NEET Favorite: Why T-shaped? 2 lp in equatorial plane push F atoms

5. XeF₂ (Xenon Difluoride) - Linear
• Xe: $sp^3d$ hybridized
• 2 bond pairs + 3 lone pairs
• Lone pair positions: ALL THREE EQUATORIAL (Bent's Rule!)
• F positions: BOTH AXIAL
• Shape: Linear
• F-Xe-F angle: 180°
• Non-polar (symmetrical)
• JEE Advanced Trap: Don't confuse with $sp$ hybridization! It's $sp^3d$ with 3 lp equatorial

6. I₃⁻ (Triiodide Ion) - Linear
• Central I: $sp^3d$ hybridized
• 2 bond pairs + 3 lone pairs
• Same as XeF₂
• Shape: Linear
• Important in Iodometry (Analytical Chemistry)
JEE PYQ Pattern Common JEE Advanced Question on PCl₃F₂:

Question: What is the structure of PCl₃F₂? Are all P-Cl bonds equivalent?

Answer:
• P: $sp^3d$ hybridized (TBP geometry)
• Total: 3 Cl + 2 F
• Applying Bent's Rule: F is more electronegative → prefers axial positions
• Structure:
  → 2 F atoms at AXIAL positions (top and bottom)
  → 3 Cl atoms at EQUATORIAL positions (in horizontal plane)
• All P-Cl bonds are equivalent? YES! (all equatorial)
• All P-F bonds are equivalent? YES! (all axial)
• But P-Cl ≠ P-F in length and strength

Similar Questions:
• PCl₂F₃: 3 F axial, wait... only 2 axial positions! → 2 F axial, 1 F equatorial, 2 Cl equatorial
• PF₃Cl₂: 2 F axial, 1 F equatorial, 2 Cl equatorial

4.5 sp³d² Hybridisation (Octahedral)

sp³d² Details Formation & Characteristics:

Mixing: 1 $s$ + 3 $p$ + 2 $d$ orbitals (specifically $d_{x^2-y^2}$ and $d_{z^2}$)
Result: 6 equivalent $sp^3d^2$ hybrid orbitals
Geometry: Octahedral (also called Square Bipyramidal)
All positions are equivalent! (unlike sp³d)
Bond angles: All 90° (adjacent) and 180° (opposite)
s-character: 16.67% (one-sixth)

Key Examples of sp³d² Hybridisation:

Examples 1. SF₆ (Sulfur Hexafluoride) - Perfect Octahedron
• S: $[Ne] 3s^2 3p^4$
• Excited state: $[Ne] 3s^1 3p^3 3d^2$ (unpair all 6 electrons)
• Hybridization: $sp^3d^2$ (using $d_{x^2-y^2}$ and $d_{z^2}$)
• 6 bond pairs, 0 lone pairs
• Shape: Octahedral
• F-S-F angle: 90° (adjacent), 180° (opposite)
• All S-F bonds equivalent
• Non-polar molecule (highly symmetrical)
• Extremely stable, inert gas

2. [CoF₆]³⁻ (Hexafluorocobaltate(III) Ion)
• Co³⁺: $sp^3d^2$ hybridized
• 6 coordinate bonds with F⁻
• Octahedral complex
• Used in coordination chemistry

3. BrF₅ (Bromine Pentafluoride) - Square Pyramidal
• Br: $sp^3d^2$ hybridized
• 5 bond pairs + 1 lone pair
• Lone pair position: Any one position (all equivalent)
• Shape: Square Pyramidal
• F(axial)-Br-F(basal) angle: <90° (~84.8°)
• F(basal)-Br-F(basal) angle: <90°

Square Planar vs Tetrahedral (Common Confusion)

Property Tetrahedral (sp³) Square Planar (sp³d²)
Hybridization sp³ sp³d²
Total electron pairs 4 6 (4 bp + 2 lp)
Bond angle 109.5° 90°, 180°
All atoms in one plane? NO (3D) YES (planar)
Examples CH₄, NH₄⁺ XeF₄, [PtCl₄]²⁻, [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻

4.6 sp³d³ Hybridisation (Pentagonal Bipyramidal)

sp³d³ Details Formation & Characteristics:

Mixing: 1 $s$ + 3 $p$ + 3 $d$ orbitals (usually $d_{xy}$, $d_{yz}$, $d_{zx}$)
Result: 7 equivalent $sp^3d^3$ hybrid orbitals
Geometry: Pentagonal Bipyramidal
• 5 orbitals in pentagonal equatorial plane
• 2 orbitals perpendicular (axial)
Bond angles: 72° (in pentagon), 90° (axial-equatorial), 180° (axial-axial)
Rare hybridization! Found mainly in xenon and iodine compounds

Key Examples:

Examples 1. IF₇ (Iodine Heptafluoride) - Perfect Pentagonal Bipyramidal
• I: $sp^3d^3$ hybridized
• 7 bond pairs, 0 lone pairs
• Shape: Pentagonal Bipyramidal
• 5 F atoms in pentagonal plane
• 2 F atoms perpendicular (axial)
• JEE Point: This is the ONLY common example you need to know

2. XeF₆ (Xenon Hexafluoride) - Distorted Octahedral
• Xe: $sp^3d^3$ hybridized (controversial - some texts say $sp^3d^3$, some say distorted $sp^3d^2$)
• 6 bond pairs + 1 lone pair
• Shape: Distorted Octahedral (capped octahedron)
• NOT perfectly octahedral due to stereochemically active lone pair
• The lone pair distorts the structure
• Advanced Note: XeF₆ is highly fluxional (rapidly changing shape)

3. XeF₅⁻ (Pentafluoroxenate Ion) - Pentagonal Planar
• Xe: $sp^3d^3$ hybridized
• 5 bond pairs + 2 lone pairs
• Both lone pairs occupy axial positions (opposite to minimize repulsion)
• 5 F atoms in pentagonal plane
• Shape: Pentagonal Planar
• Very rare structure!

5. The Master Calculation Method - Steric Number Formula

To solve MCQs in under 30 seconds without drawing Lewis structures, use the Steric Number (Z) approach.

Definition Steric Number (Z): Total number of atoms bonded to the central atom + Number of lone pairs on the central atom

$$Z = \text{Number of } \sigma \text{ bonds} + \text{Number of lone pairs on central atom}$$
Alternative Formula (Faster for ions):

$$Z = \frac{1}{2}(V + M - C + A)$$
where:
• V = Valence electrons of central atom
• M = Number of monovalent atoms attached (H, F, Cl, Br, I count as 1 each)
• C = Charge on cation (subtract)
• A = Charge on anion (add)

⚠️ CRITICAL WARNING: Do NOT count divalent atoms like O, S in M! They contribute 0 to M.

6. Advanced Concepts (Strictly JEE Advanced + NEET)

6.1 Drago's Rule (When Hybridisation Does NOT Occur)

Figure 6.1: Drago's Rule Comparison — Hybridized $\text{NH}_3$ (107°) vs Pure $p$-Orbital Bonding $\text{PH}_3$ (93.6°)
Drago's Rule When to Apply Drago's Rule:

Hybridisation does NOT occur (atoms use pure $p$ orbitals for bonding) when:

Conditions (ALL must be satisfied):
1. Central atom is from Group 15 or 16
2. Central atom is from 3rd period or below (P, As, Sb, S, Se, Te, etc.)
3. Surrounding atoms are H or other atoms with electronegativity ≤ 2.5
4. No positive charge on central atom

Result:
• Central atom uses pure, unhybridized $p$ orbitals (mutually perpendicular)
• Bond angle ≈ 90° (since $p_x$, $p_y$, $p_z$ are at 90° to each other)
• Lone pair resides in pure $s$ orbital (spherical, non-directional)

Classic Examples:

PH₃ (Phosphine):
• P: [Ne] 3s² 3p³
• Uses pure 3p orbitals for bonding with H
• Bond angle: 93.6° (close to 90°)
• NOT 107° like NH₃!
• Reason: No $sp^3$ hybridization
JEE Trap Question Why does NH₃ show hybridization but PH₃ does not?

Answer:
• N is from 2nd period → small size → high effective nuclear charge
• 2s and 2p orbitals have comparable energies → easy to hybridize
• Energy released in forming stronger hybrid orbital bonds > energy required for hybridization

• P is from 3rd period → larger size → 3s-3p energy gap is larger
• Energy required for hybridization > energy released in bond formation
• Therefore, uses pure $p$ orbitals (lower energy pathway)
• H has low electronegativity → cannot pull enough electron density to force hybridization

But PF₃ shows hybridization (bond angle ~97.8°)!
• F is highly electronegative
• Pulls electron density strongly
• Energy released in forming P-F bonds > energy required for hybridization
• Therefore, partial $sp^3$ character develops

AsH₃ (Arsine):
• Bond angle: 91.8°

SbH₃ (Stibine):
• Bond angle: 91.3°

H₂S (Hydrogen Sulfide):
• S: [Ne] 3s² 3p⁴
• Bond angle: 92.1°
• NOT 104.5° like H₂O!

H₂Se (Hydrogen Selenide):
• Bond angle: 91°

H₂Te (Hydrogen Telluride):
• Bond angle: 90°

6.2 Odd-Electron Species (Free Radicals & Paramagnetic Molecules)

Free Radicals Hybridization in Free Radicals:

The hybridization state depends on the electronegativity of surrounding atoms.

1. Methyl Radical (•CH₃):
• C has 3 H atoms + 1 unpaired electron
• Electronegativity: C (2.5) > H (2.1)
• C pulls electron density more than H
• Unpaired electron stays in pure $p$ orbital
• C is $sp^2$ hybridized
• Shape: Planar (trigonal planar with 3 H, electron in $p_z$)
• H-C-H angle: ~120°

2. Trifluoromethyl Radical (•CF₃):
• C has 3 F atoms + 1 unpaired electron
• Electronegativity: F (4.0) >>> C (2.5)
• F pulls electron density STRONGLY away from C
• This creates high electron-electron repulsion
• Unpaired electron is forced to take on $s$-character
• C is $sp^3$ hybridized
• Shape: Pyramidal (like NH₃)
• F-C-F angle: <109.5°

3. Nitrogen Dioxide (NO₂):
• N has 1 unpaired electron
• Steric number: Z = 2 bonds + 1 lone pair equivalent = 3
• N is $sp^2$ hybridized
• Unpaired electron in $sp^2$ orbital
• Shape: Bent
• O-N-O angle: ~134°
• Paramagnetic (unpaired electron)

4. Oxygen Molecule (O₂):
• Has 2 unpaired electrons (in antibonding π* orbitals)
• Paramagnetic
• Cannot be explained by simple VBT → need MOT

6.4 Back Bonding & Expanded Octet (Advanced JEE)

Back Bonding pπ-pπ and pπ-dπ Back Bonding (Ultimate JEE/NEET Masterclass):

When an atom with a vacant orbital (like B, Si, S, P) bonds with an adjacent atom having a filled orbital/lone pair (like F, O, N, Cl), electron density is donated back to form a $\pi$ bond.

Key Effects of Back Bonding:
• Creates partial double bond character (shortens bond length, increases bond energy)
• Alters hybridization and geometry (e.g. $sp^3$ pyramidal → $sp^2$ planar)
• Modifies Lewis acidity / basicity

Master Examples for JEE/NEET:

1. Lewis Acidity Trend of Boron Trihalides (BF₃ < BCl₃ < BBr₃ < BI₃):
• Expected order by electronegativity: $\text{BF}_3 > \text{BCl}_3 > \text{BBr}_3 > \text{BI}_3$
• Actual Experimental Order: $\text{BF}_3 < \text{BCl}_3 < \text{BBr}_3 < \text{BI}_3$ ($\text{BI}_3$ is the strongest Lewis acid!)
• Reason: Extent of $p\pi-p\pi$ back-bonding from halogen lone pair to empty $2p$ orbital of Boron:
  → $\text{BF}_3$: $2p(\text{F}) \to 2p(\text{B})$ overlap (maximum overlap, identical shell $n=2$)
  → $\text{BCl}_3$: $3p(\text{Cl}) \to 2p(\text{B})$ overlap (weaker)
  → $\text{BBr}_3$: $4p(\text{Br}) \to 2p(\text{B})$ overlap (very weak)
  → $\text{BI}_3$: $5p(\text{I}) \to 2p(\text{B})$ overlap (negligible)
• Strong back-bonding in $\text{BF}_3$ compensates Boron's electron deficiency, making it the weakest Lewis acid!

2. Trisilyylamine N(SiH₃)₃ vs Trimethylamine N(CH₃)₃ (JEE Advanced Classic):
• $\text{N(CH}_3)_3$ (Trimethylamine): Pyramidal shape, $sp^3$ hybridized, strong Lewis base.
  → Carbon has no vacant $d$-orbitals, lone pair stays localized on Nitrogen.
• $\text{N(SiH}_3)_3$ (Trisilyylamine): Trigonal Planar shape, $sp^2$ hybridized, non-basic!
  → Nitrogen's lone pair is donated into the empty $3d$ orbital of Silicon ($p\pi-d\pi$ back bonding).
  → Hybridization changes from $sp^3$ to $sp^2$, bond angle expands from ~107° to 120°!

3. SO₃ & SO₂ Bond Lengths:
• Actual S-O bond length in $\text{SO}_3$: 143 pm (much shorter than single S-O bond 170 pm)
• $p\pi-d\pi$ back bonding from O lone pairs to empty $3d$ orbitals of Sulfur.

4. Disiloxane (SiH₃)₂O Bond Angle:
• Si-O-Si bond angle is 144° (much wider than H₂O's 104.5°)

6.5 Coordinate/Dative Bonds (Crucial for Complexes)

Coordinate Bond Coordinate Covalent Bond: A covalent bond in which both electrons come from the same atom.

Formation:
• One atom (donor) has a lone pair
• Other atom (acceptor) has a vacant orbital
• Lone pair is shared

Representation: A → B (arrow from donor to acceptor)

Important Note: Once formed, a coordinate bond is identical to a normal covalent bond in strength and properties.

Examples of Coordinate Bonds:

Examples 1. NH₄⁺ (Ammonium Ion):
• NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺
• NH₃ donates lone pair to H⁺
• Results in 4 equivalent N-H bonds (all $sp^3$ hybrid orbital bonds)
• Cannot distinguish which bond is coordinate after formation

2. H₃O⁺ (Hydronium Ion):
• H₂O + H⁺ → H₃O⁺
• One O-H bond is coordinate
• $sp^3$ hybridized, pyramidal

3. BF₃·NH₃ (Adduct):
• BF₃ (Lewis acid, $sp^2$, planar) + NH₃ (Lewis base, $sp^3$, pyramidal)
• NH₃ donates lone pair to empty $p_z$ of B
• After bonding:
  → B becomes $sp^3$ hybridized
  → Tetrahedral around B
• F-B-F angle changes from 120° to ~109.5°

4. CO (Carbon Monoxide):
• :C≡O: has one coordinate bond
• C donates lone pair, creating triple bond
• Structure: $C \equiv O$ (1σ + 2π normal bonds + 1 coordinate bond contribution)

5. [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ (Ferrocyanide Ion):
• 6 CN⁻ ligands donate lone pairs to Fe²⁺
• All Fe-C bonds are coordinate bonds
• Octahedral complex

6. Ozone (O₃):
• Central O is $sp^2$ hybridized
• One O-O bond is coordinate (central O donates lone pair)
• Both O-O bonds equivalent due to resonance

6.6 Berry Pseudorotation & Fluxional Molecules

Berry Pseudorotation Intramolecular Rearrangement in Trigonal Bipyramidal Molecules:

In molecules with Trigonal Bipyramidal (TBP) geometry such as $\text{PF}_5$ and $\text{Fe(CO)}_5$:
• Statistically, axial bonds are longer and weaker than equatorial bonds (axial = 219 pm, equatorial = 204 pm for PCl₅).
• However, in $^{19}\text{F-NMR}$ spectroscopy at room temperature, all 5 Fluorine atoms in $\text{PF}_5$ appear completely IDENTICAL!

The Mechanism (Berry Pseudorotation):
• The 2 axial ligands and 2 of the equatorial ligands simultaneously bend towards each other.
• The molecule transitions through a Square Pyramidal ($C_{4v}$) transition state.
• The axial ligands become equatorial, and the equatorial ligands become axial!
• This exchange happens extremely rapidly ($>10^5$ times per second), making all 5 F atoms equivalent on the NMR timescale.
• Molecules showing such rapid structural rearrangements are called fluxional molecules (e.g. $\text{PF}_5, \text{Fe(CO)}_5, \text{XeF}_6$).

6.7 Organic Molecular Geometry & Cumulenes

Allenes & Cumulenes Hybridization & Spatial Orientation in Polyalkenes:

1. Allenes (CH₂=C=CH₂):
• Central Carbon: $sp$ hybridized (forms 2 $\sigma$ bonds + 2 $\pi$ bonds at 180°).
• Terminal Carbons: $sp^2$ hybridized.
• Geometry & Plane Orientation: The two $\pi$ bonds are formed by mutually perpendicular $p_y$ and $p_z$ orbitals.
  → Therefore, the two terminal $-\text{CH}_2$ groups lie in MUTUALLY PERPENDICULAR PLANES!
  → JEE Advanced Consequence: Substituted allenes of the type $\text{abC}=\text{C}=\text{Cab}$ have no plane of symmetry and are OPTICALLY ACTIVE (CHIRAL) without any asymmetric carbon atom!

2. Cumulenes with Odd vs Even Double Bonds:
• Even number of C=C bonds (Allenes, $\text{C}=\text{C}=\text{C}$): Terminal groups are perpendicular $\implies$ Optically active (enantiomerism).
• Odd number of C=C bonds (Butatriene, $\text{C}=\text{C}=\text{C}=\text{C}$): Terminal groups are COPLANAR $\implies$ Show Geometrical Isomerism (*cis/trans*)!

7. Step-by-Step Problem Solving Strategy

Figure 7.1: Master Flowchart for Determining Hybridization, Geometry, and Bond Angles
Master Strategy To Find Hybridization, Shape, and Bond Angle of ANY Molecule:

STEP 1: Identify the central atom
STEP 2: Calculate Steric Number (Z)
  • Method 1: Count σ bonds + lone pairs
  • Method 2: Use formula $Z = \frac{1}{2}(V + M - C + A)$
STEP 3: Determine hybridization from Z
  • Z=2 → sp
  • Z=3 → sp²
  • Z=4 → sp³
  • Z=5 → sp³d
  • Z=6 → sp³d²
  • Z=7 → sp³d³
STEP 4: Find number of lone pairs
  • lp = Z - (number of atoms bonded)
STEP 5: Use VSEPR to determine molecular shape
  • For sp³d: Apply Bent's Rule for lone pair position
STEP 6: Determine bond angle based on shape and lone pair repulsions
STEP 7: Check for special cases (Drago's rule, back bonding, etc.)

8. Solved Examples (JEE/NEET PYQ Pattern)

Example 1 (JEE Main 2023):

Question: What is the hybridization and shape of ClO₃⁻ (chlorate ion)?

Solution:

Step 1: Central atom = Cl

Step 2: Calculate Z using formula
• V = 7 (Cl has 7 valence electrons)
• M = 0 (O is divalent, doesn't count!)
• C = 0
• A = 1 (chlorate is an anion)
• $Z = \frac{1}{2}(7 + 0 - 0 + 1) = \frac{8}{2} = 4$

Step 3: Z = 4 → sp³ hybridization

Step 4: Cl forms bonds with 3 O atoms
• Number of bonds = 3
• Lone pairs = Z - bonds = 4 - 3 = 1

Step 5: sp³ with 3 bp + 1 lp → Trigonal Pyramidal shape

Step 6: Bond angle: ~107° (less than 109.5° due to lone pair repulsion)

Answer: sp³ hybridization, Trigonal Pyramidal, angle ~107°

Example 3 (JEE Advanced 2021):

Question: In the molecule PCl₂F₃, how many P-Cl bonds are in the equatorial plane and how many in axial positions?

Solution:

Analysis:
• P: $sp^3d$ hybridized (5 bonds, TBP geometry)
• 2 Cl atoms + 3 F atoms
• Applying Bent's Rule:
  → More electronegative atoms (F) prefer axial positions (0% s-character)
  → Less electronegative atoms (Cl) prefer equatorial positions (33.3% s-character)

Arrangement:
• Axial positions (2): 2 F atoms
• Equatorial positions (3): 2 Cl + 1 F

Answer:
• P-Cl bonds in equatorial plane: 2
• P-Cl bonds in axial positions: 0
• All P-Cl bonds are equivalent (both equatorial)

Example 4 (NEET 2023):

Question: The correct order of bond angles in H₂O, NH₃, CH₄, and C₂H₂ is:
(A) H₂O < NH₃ < CH₄ < C₂H₂
(B) C₂H₂ < CH₄ < NH₃ < H₂O
(C) CH₄ < NH₃ < H₂O < C₂H₂
(D) H₂O < NH₃ < C₂H₂ < CH₄

Solution:

Step 1: Determine hybridization & lone pairs for each molecule:
• H₂O: $sp^3$ (2 bp + 2 lp) $\to$ Bond angle: 104.5°
• NH₃: $sp^3$ (3 bp + 1 lp) $\to$ Bond angle: 107°
• CH₄: $sp^3$ (4 bp + 0 lp) $\to$ Bond angle: 109.5°
• C₂H₂: $sp$ (2 bp + 0 lp) $\to$ Bond angle: 180°

Step 2: Compare bond angles:
104.5° (H₂O) < 107° (NH₃) < 109.5° (CH₄) < 180° (C₂H₂)

Answer: (A) H₂O < NH₃ < CH₄ < C₂H₂

Example 5 (JEE Main 2024):

Question: Total number of lone pairs on the central atom in XeF₄, SF₄, and ClF₃ are respectively:

Solution:

• XeF₄: Xe (8 valence $e^-$) - 4 bp = 4 non-bonding $e^-$ = 2 lone pairs
• SF₄: S (6 valence $e^-$) - 4 bp = 2 non-bonding $e^-$ = 1 lone pair
• ClF₃: Cl (7 valence $e^-$) - 3 bp = 4 non-bonding $e^-$ = 2 lone pairs

Answer: 2, 1, 2 respectively

11. Common Mistakes to Avoid (JEE/NEET Traps)

⚠️ Avoid These Errors 1. Confusing Shape with Hybridization
✗ "XeF₂ is linear, so it must be sp hybridized"
✓ XeF₂ is linear due to sp³d hybridization with 3 equatorial lone pairs

2. Counting Divalent Atoms in M
✗ For ClO₃⁻: Using M = 3 (counting 3 oxygens)
✓ For ClO₃⁻: M = 0 (oxygen is divalent, ignore it!)

3. Assuming Hybridization Always Occurs
✗ "PH₃ is sp³ hybridized like NH₃"
✓ PH₃ follows Drago's rule → NO hybridization → uses pure p orbitals

4. Forgetting Bent's Rule for sp³d
✗ In ClF₃, placing lone pairs randomly
✓ In ClF₃, both lone pairs MUST be equatorial (Bent's Rule)

5. Wrong Lone Pair Count
✗ Forgetting to count lone pairs on central atom
✓ Always calculate: lp = Z - (number of bonds)

6. Assuming π Bonds Use Hybrid Orbitals
✗ "In C₂H₄, all bonds use sp² orbitals"
✓ C=C has 1 σ bond (sp²-sp²) + 1 π bond (p-p pure overlap)

7. Not Considering Back Bonding
✗ "SO₃ has normal S-O single bonds"
✓ SO₃ has shortened S-O bonds due to pπ-dπ back bonding

8. Solid State Structure Ignorance
✗ "PCl₅ is always sp³d"
✓ In solid state, PCl₅ is [PCl₄]⁺ (sp³) + [PCl₆]⁻ (sp³d²)

9. Electronegativity Effect on Angle
✗ Thinking NF₃ has larger angle than NH₃ (F is larger atom)
✓ NF₃ (102°) < NH₃ (107°) because F pulls bp away, reducing bp-bp repulsion

12. Formula Sheet (Print & Keep)

Quick Formulas 1. Steric Number:
$$Z = \frac{1}{2}(V + M - C + A)$$

2. Lone Pairs:
$$\text{Lone Pairs} = Z - \text{Number of bonds}$$

3. Bond Order:
$$\text{Bond Order} = \frac{\text{Total number of bonds}}{\text{Total number of bond groups}}$$

4. Formal Charge:
$$\text{FC} = V - N_b - \frac{B}{2}$$
where V = valence electrons, N_b = non-bonding electrons, B = bonding electrons

5. Resonance Bond Length:
$$\text{Actual length} = \frac{(\text{Single bond length}) + (\text{Double bond length})}{2}$$ (for equal resonance)

• sp: 50%
• sp²: 33.33%
• sp³: 25%
• sp³d: 20%
