CLASSIFICATION OF ELEMENTS AND PERIODICITY IN PROPERTIES

Exam Weightage & Importance This chapter is a foundational pillar for CBSE Boards, NEET, JEE Main/Advanced, WBJEE, COMEDK, and VITEEE. Master the periodic laws, electronic configurations, $s, p, d, f$ blocks, and periodic exceptions (Ionization Enthalpy anomalies, Electron Gain Enthalpy halogen trends, Isoelectronic radii, Oxide natures) to guarantee top marks!

1. The Journey of Classification: Old Era to Modern Era

Early scientists struggled to find a pattern among elements. The earliest attempts focused on Atomic Weight, while the modern table unlocked the true secret: Atomic Number ($Z$).

The Old Era (Based on Atomic Mass) All these early models eventually failed because they relied on Atomic Mass rather than Atomic Number.

1.1 Dobereiner's Triads (1829)

He grouped elements in threes. The atomic weight of the middle element was roughly the average of the first and third.
Examples: $\text{Li} (7) + \text{K} (39) \rightarrow \text{Na} (23)$; $\text{Ca} (40) + \text{Ba} (137) \rightarrow \text{Sr} (88.5)$

1.2 Newlands' Law of Octaves (1865)

Arranged elements like musical notes! Every 8th element repeats properties of the 1st. But it only worked up to Calcium ($Z=20$).

1.3 Lothar Meyer's Curve (1869)

Plotted Atomic Volume vs. Atomic Mass. He noticed periodic peaks where highly reactive alkali metals ($\text{Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs}$) sat right at the top.

1.4 Mendeleev's Masterpiece (1869)

Mendeleev's Periodic Law: "Properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic weights."

Mendeleev was a genius because he left empty gaps for undiscovered elements and accurately predicted their properties!
Key Predictions (High-Yield):

  • Eka-Aluminium $\rightarrow$ Gallium ($\text{Ga}$) ($E_2O_3 \rightarrow \text{Ga}_2\text{O}_3, ECl_3 \rightarrow \text{GaCl}_3$)
  • Eka-Silicon $\rightarrow$ Germanium ($\text{Ge}$) ($EO_2 \rightarrow \text{GeO}_2, ECl_4 \rightarrow \text{GeCl}_4$)
  • Eka-Boron $\rightarrow$ Scandium ($\text{Sc}$)

Anomalous Pairs: He had to put some heavier elements before lighter ones to match properties: $\text{Te} (127.6)$ before $\text{I} (126.9)$; $\text{Ar} (39.9)$ before $\text{K} (39.1)$.

The Modern Era (Based on Atomic Number) The revolutionary shift from Atomic Mass to Atomic Number that gave us the table we use today.

1.5 The Modern Periodic Law (Henry Moseley, 1913)

Moseley studied X-ray frequency ($\nu$) of metals and established the linear relation:

$$\sqrt{\nu} = a(Z - b)$$

He proved that Atomic Number ($Z$), not atomic mass, is the fundamental property governing periodic trends.

Modern Periodic Law "The physical and chemical properties of the elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers ($Z$)."

1.6 Structure of Long Form Periodic Table

  • 7 Periods (Horizontal Rows): Period number = highest principal quantum number ($n$).
    • Period 1 ($n=1$): 2 elements ($1s$) • Period 2 ($n=2$): 8 elements ($2s, 2p$)
    • Period 3 ($n=3$): 8 elements ($3s, 3p$) • Period 4 ($n=4$): 18 elements ($4s, 3d, 4p$)
    • Period 5 ($n=5$): 18 elements ($5s, 4d, 5p$) • Period 6 ($n=6$): 32 elements ($6s, 4f, 5d, 6p$)
    • Period 7 ($n=7$): 32 elements ($7s, 5f, 6d, 7p$)
  • 18 Groups (Vertical Columns): Numbered 1 to 18 (IUPAC). Elements in a group share identical outer electronic configuration and similar chemical behavior.
Practice Set 1 — Laws & History Q1 (Board & NEET): What was predicted as Eka-Aluminium and Eka-Silicon by Mendeleev?
Solution: Eka-Aluminium is Gallium ($\text{Ga}$); Eka-Silicon is Germanium ($\text{Ge}$).
Q2 (JEE Main): How many elements are present in the 6th period of the periodic table? Justify using quantum numbers.
Solution: For $n=6$, orbitals filled are $6s (1), 4f (7), 5d (5), 6p (3) \Rightarrow 16 \text{ orbitals}$. Max capacity $= 16 \times 2 = \mathbf{32 \text{ elements}}$.

4. Block Classification ($s, p, d, f$) & General Configurations

The division of elements into $s, p, d,$ and $f$ blocks is based on the Aufbau principle and the type of atomic orbital being filled with electrons. This classification naturally emerges from Niels Bohr's construction of the Long Form Periodic Table.

Table 3.1: Block-wise Characteristics & Configurations
Block General Outer Configuration Groups Key Characteristics & Exam Exceptions
s-Block $ns^{1-2}$ 1, 2 Reactive metals, low IE, form $+1, +2$ cations. Predominantly ionic. $\text{H} (1s^1)$ placed separately. $\text{He} (1s^2)$ placed in p-block (Group 18).
p-Block $ns^2 np^{1-6}$ 13 to 18 Includes metals, non-metals, metalloids ($\text{Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te}$). Representative elements ($s+p$). Halogens (Group 17), Chalcogens (Group 16), Noble gases (Group 18).
d-Block $(n-1)d^{1-10} ns^{0-2}$ 3 to 12 Transition metals. Variable oxidation states, colored ions, paramagnetic, catalysts. Exceptions: Palladium $\text{Pd} (4d^{10} 5s^0)$. $\mathbf{\text{Zn, Cd, Hg}}$ ($(n-1)d^{10} ns^2$) are not transition elements (fully filled $d$-subshell).
f-Block $(n-2)f^{1-14} (n-1)d^{0-1} ns^2$ Group 3 Inner transition elements. Lanthanoids ($4f, Z=58-71, \text{Ce}$ to $\text{Lu}$) and Actinoids ($5f, Z=90-103, \text{Th}$ to $\text{Lr}$). Transuranium elements ($Z > 92$) are synthetic.
Positional Anomalies Helium ($\text{He}$): Strictly belongs to the s-block ($1s^2$), but is placed in Group 18 p-block because its completely filled valence shell exhibits noble gas properties.
Hydrogen ($\text{H}$): Has $1s^1$ configuration. Can lose an electron to form $\text{H}^+$ (like alkali metals) or gain an electron to form $\text{H}^-$ (like halogens). Kept separately at the top of the periodic table.
Practice Set 3 — Blocks & Position Q1 (NEET / COMEDK): Why are $\text{Zn, Cd, Hg}$ not considered typical transition elements?
Solution: Because they have completely filled $(n-1)d^{10}$ subshells in both ground state and common oxidation states ($+2$).

5. Periodic Trends in Physical Properties (High-Yield Exam Focus)

1. Effective Nuclear Charge ($Z_{eff}$) & Slater's Rules

$$Z_{eff} = Z - \sigma$$

Inner electrons shield valence electrons from nuclear pull. Screening power: $s > p > d > f$. Across a period, $Z_{eff}$ increases, pulling electrons closer.

2. Atomic Radius ($r_{vdw} > r_{met} > r_{cov}$)

Because an atom's electron cloud has no sharp boundary, atomic size is estimated in the combined state:

  • Covalent Radius: Half the distance between two identical atoms bonded by a single covalent bond (e.g., $\text{Cl}_2$ bond is $198\text{ pm} \Rightarrow r_{cov} = 99\text{ pm}$).
  • Metallic Radius: Half the internuclear distance between two adjacent metal cores in a metallic crystal (e.g., $\text{Cu}$ distance is $256\text{ pm} \Rightarrow r_{met} = 128\text{ pm}$).
  • Van der Waals Radius: Half the distance between two non-bonded adjacent atoms in solid state (used for Noble Gases).
  • Across Period: Atomic radius decreases (increasing $Z_{eff}$).
  • Down Group: Atomic radius increases (addition of principal shells $n$).
  • Noble Gases: Have non-bonded van der Waals radii, which are larger than covalent radii of preceding halogens!

3. Ionic Radius & Isoelectronic Series (100% Exam Favorite)

Cation < Parent Atom ($\text{Na}^+ 95\text{ pm} < \text{Na } 186\text{ pm}$). Anion > Parent Atom ($\text{F}^- 136\text{ pm} > \text{F } 64\text{ pm}$).

Isoelectronic Species Radius Rule For species with same number of electrons ($\text{N}^{3-}, \text{O}^{2-}, \text{F}^-, \text{Na}^+, \text{Mg}^{2+}, \text{Al}^{3+}$):
Higher positive nuclear charge ($Z$) $\Rightarrow$ Smaller radius. $$\text{N}^{3-} > \text{O}^{2-} > \text{F}^- > \text{Na}^+ > \text{Mg}^{2+} > \text{Al}^{3+}$$
JEE / NEET Exception — Lanthanoid Contraction Poor shielding by $14$ inner $4f$ electrons causes size of 5d series elements to be almost identical to 4d series elements: $$\text{Zr} (4d) \approx \text{Hf} (5d) \quad (160\text{ pm} \approx 159\text{ pm})$$ $$\text{Nb} \approx \text{Ta}, \quad \text{Mo} \approx \text{W}$$

4. Ionization Enthalpy ($\Delta_i H$) & Crucial Exceptions

Energy required to remove outermost electron from isolated gaseous ground-state atom. Always endothermic ($\Delta_i H > 0$). $\Delta_i H_1 < \Delta_i H_2 < \Delta_i H_3$.

TOP EXAM EXCEPTIONS — Ionization Enthalpy 1. $\text{Be} > \text{B}$ ($\Delta_i H_1$): $\text{Be} (1s^2 2s^2)$ has higher $\Delta_i H_1$ ($899\text{ kJ/mol}$) than $\text{B} (1s^2 2s^2 2p^1, 801\text{ kJ/mol}$). Removing penetrating $2s$ electron from stable $2s^2$ is harder.
2. $\text{N} > \text{O}$ ($\Delta_i H_1$): $\text{N} (2p^3)$ has extra stable half-filled $2p^3$ subshell ($1402\text{ kJ/mol}$). $\text{O} (2p^4)$ has paired electron repulsion ($1314\text{ kJ/mol}$).
3. Period 2 IE Order (JEE/NEET): $$\text{Li} < \text{B} < \text{Be} < \text{C} < \text{O} < \text{N} < \text{F} < \text{Ne}$$ 4. Group 13 IE Anomaly: $$\text{B} > \text{Tl} > \text{Ga} > \text{Al} > \text{In}$$ (Due to transition contraction in $\text{Ga}$ and lanthanoid contraction in $\text{Tl}$).
5. 2nd Ionization Enthalpy: $\Delta_i H_2(\text{Na}) \gg \Delta_i H_2(\text{Mg})$ because removing 2nd electron from $\text{Na}^+$ breaks noble gas core ($2p^6$).
6. Identifying Valency from IE Jumps (JEE/NEET): A sudden huge jump between $IE_n$ and $IE_{n+1}$ indicates the atom has $n$ valence electrons (i.e. belongs to Group $n$ or $n+10$). E.g., if $IE_1 = 520, IE_2 = 7300$, the massive jump means 1 valence $e^-$ (Group 1).

5. Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg} H$)

Enthalpy change when neutral gaseous atom gains an electron: $X_{(g)} + e^- \rightarrow X^-_{(g)}$. Negative for non-metals; Positive for noble gases.

TOP EXAM EXCEPTIONS — Electron Gain Enthalpy 1. Halogen Trend ($\text{Cl} > \text{F}$): Chlorine has the most negative $\Delta_{eg} H$ in periodic table! $$\text{Cl} (-349\text{ kJ/mol}) > \text{F} (-328) > \text{Br} (-325) > \text{I} (-295)$$ Reason: Fluorine is extremely small; incoming electron suffers high inter-electronic repulsion in compact $2p$ subshell.

2. Group 16 Trend ($\text{S} > \text{O}$): Sulfur ($-200\text{ kJ/mol}$) is more negative than Oxygen ($-141\text{ kJ/mol}$). Oxygen is least negative in Group 16.

6. Electronegativity (EN)

Tendency of a bonded atom to attract shared pair of electrons. Fluorine is assigned max value $4.0$ on Pauling scale.

Scales of Electronegativity:
• Pauling Scale: $\text{F} (4.0) > \text{O} (3.5) > \text{N} (3.0) \approx \text{Cl} (3.0) > \text{Br} (2.8) > \text{I} (2.5) > \text{H} (2.1)$
• Mulliken Scale: $\text{EN} = \frac{\text{IE} + \text{EA}}{2} \quad (\text{in eV})$ • Hybridization Effect: $\text{EN} \propto \% s\text{-character} \Rightarrow sp (50\%) > sp^2 (33\%) > sp^3 (25\%)$
Practice Set 4 — Trends & Exceptions (JEE / NEET / VITEEE) Q1 (JEE Main): Arrange in decreasing order of ionic radius: $\text{Mg}^{2+}, \text{Na}^+, \text{F}^-, \text{O}^{2-}, \text{N}^{3-}$.
Solution: Isoelectronic 10e series. Higher positive charge = smaller size.
Order: $\text{N}^{3-} > \text{O}^{2-} > \text{F}^- > \text{Na}^+ > \text{Mg}^{2+}$.
Q2 (NEET / WBJEE): Which element has the highest first ionization enthalpy among $\text{B, C, N, O}$?
Solution: Nitrogen ($\text{N}$) ($1402\text{ kJ/mol}$) due to extra stable half-filled $2p^3$ configuration ($\text{N} > \text{O}$).
Q3 (COMEDK / VITEEE): Which element has the most negative electron gain enthalpy in the periodic table?
Solution: Chlorine ($\text{Cl}$) ($-349\text{ kJ/mol}$).

6. Periodic Trends in Chemical Properties

1. Valence & Oxidation States

Valence = Number of outer electrons (Group 1, 2, 13, 14) or $(8 - \text{outer electrons})$ (Group 15 to 18).

Example ($OF_2$ vs $Na_2O$): Fluorine is more electronegative than Oxygen $\Rightarrow$ In $\text{OF}_2$, $\text{F} = -1$ and $\text{O} = +2$. In $\text{Na}_2\text{O}$, $\text{O} = -2$ and $\text{Na} = +1$.

2. Anomalous Properties of 2nd Period Elements ($\text{Li, Be, B, C, N, O, F}$)

  1. Small size & high charge/radius ratio.
  2. High electronegativity & high IE.
  3. Absence of vacant $d$-orbitals: Max covalency is 4 (e.g. Boron forms $[\text{BF}_4]^-$, but Aluminium forms $[\text{AlF}_6]^{3-}$).
  4. Form $p\pi-p\pi$ multiple bonds ($\text{C=C, C}\equiv\text{C, N}\equiv\text{N, C=O}$).

3. Diagonal Relationship

Similarities between diagonal elements ($\text{Li}-\text{Mg}, \text{Be}-\text{Al}, \text{B}-\text{Si}$) due to nearly equal polarizing power ($\text{Ionic Charge} / \text{Radius}^2$).

4. Oxides Nature Classification (Direct Exam MCQs)

  • Basic Oxides: $\text{Na}_2\text{O}, \text{K}_2\text{O}, \text{CaO}, \text{BaO}$ ($\text{Na}_2\text{O} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 2\text{NaOH}$).
  • Acidic Oxides: $\text{Cl}_2\text{O}_7, \text{SO}_3, \text{N}_2\text{O}_5, \text{CO}_2, \text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}$ ($\text{Cl}_2\text{O}_7 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 2\text{HClO}_4$).
  • Amphoteric Oxides (High-Yield): $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3, \text{Ga}_2\text{O}_3, \text{ZnO}, \text{BeO}, \text{PbO}, \text{SnO}, \text{As}_2\text{O}_3$.
  • Neutral Oxides (High-Yield): $\text{CO}, \text{NO}, \text{N}_2\text{O}, \text{H}_2\text{O}$.

5. Metallic Character, Oxidizing & Reducing Power

  • Metallic Character (Reducing Power): Increases down a group (due to ease of losing electrons) and decreases across a period.
  • Non-Metallic Character (Oxidizing Power): Increases across a period from left to right, and decreases down a group.
    Order of Oxidizing Power (NCERT Ex 3.40): $\text{F} > \text{O} > \text{Cl} > \text{N}$

7. Master Exam Practice Target Set (Board, JEE, NEET, WBJEE, COMEDK)

Section A: Multiple Choice Questions (PYQs) Q1 (JEE Advanced): The first ionization enthalpies of $\text{Na, Mg, Al,}$ and $\text{Si}$ are in the order:
(A) $\text{Na} < \text{Mg} < \text{Al} < \text{Si}$   (B) $\text{Na} < \text{Al} < \text{Mg} < \text{Si}$
(C) $\text{Al} < \text{Na} < \text{Mg} < \text{Si}$   (D) $\text{Na} < \text{Al} < \text{Si} < \text{Mg}$
Solution: (B) $\text{Mg} (3s^2)$ has a fully filled stable subshell, so its 1st IE is greater than $\text{Al} (3s^2 3p^1)$.
Q2 (NEET PYQ): The correct order of atomic radii in group 13 elements is:
(A) $\text{B} < \text{Al} < \text{Ga} < \text{In} < \text{Tl}$   (B) $\text{B} < \text{Ga} < \text{Al} < \text{In} < \text{Tl}$
Solution: (B) Due to poor screening by $10$ $3d$-electrons in $\text{Ga}$, $Z_{eff}$ increases, making $\text{Ga}$ ($135\text{ pm}$) smaller than $\text{Al}$ ($143\text{ pm}$).
Q3 (WBJEE / COMEDK): Which of the following is an amphoteric oxide?
(A) $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$   (B) $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_3$   (C) $\text{BeO}$   (D) All of the above
Solution: (D) $\text{BeO}, \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3, \text{ZnO}, \text{V}_2\text{O}_5,$ and $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_3$ all exhibit amphoteric behavior (reacting with both acids and bases).

(B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
(C) A is true, R is false.
(D) A is false, R is true.

Section C: Integer / Calculation Type (JEE Main & Board HOTS) Q6: An element has successive ionization enthalpies (in $\text{kJ/mol}$) of $IE_1=899, IE_2=1757, IE_3=14850, IE_4=21005$. To which group of the periodic table does it belong?
Solution: Group 2. There is a massive jump between $IE_2$ and $IE_3$, indicating the atom has 2 valence electrons and breaking into the core requires huge energy.
Q7: What is the atomic number of the element located in the 6th period and 4th group of the periodic table?
Solution: The noble gas before 6th period is Xenon ($Z=54$).
6th period starts with 6s. $6s^2 \rightarrow 56$ (Ba).
Then 4f fills ($57-71$).
Then 5d series starts. Group 4 means $d^2$. So $54 (\text{Xe}) + 2 (6s) + 14 (4f) + 2 (5d) = \mathbf{72}$ (Hafnium, Hf).
