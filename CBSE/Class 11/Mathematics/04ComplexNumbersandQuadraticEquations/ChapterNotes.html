Topic 1: Need for Complex Numbers — The Imaginary Unit $i$

Historical Motivation

In the real number system $\mathbb{R}$, we cannot find the square root of a negative number. For example, $\sqrt{-1}$ does not exist in $\mathbb{R}$ because the square of any real number is always non-negative ($x^2 \geq 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$).

The equation $x^2 + 1 = 0 \implies x^2 = -1$ has no real solution. To solve equations of this type, mathematicians invented the concept of an imaginary unit denoted by $i$ (or $j$ in engineering).

Historical Note: The term "imaginary" was coined by the mathematician René Descartes. Swiss mathematician Euler introduced the symbol $i$ for $\sqrt{-1}$ in 1777.

Definition of $i$ (Iota): $$i = \sqrt{-1} \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad i^2 = -1$$ With this definition: $\sqrt{-a} = i\sqrt{a}$ for any real number $a > 0$.

Examples: $\sqrt{-4} = 2i$,   $\sqrt{-9} = 3i$,   $\sqrt{-7} = i\sqrt{7}$,   $\sqrt{-\frac{1}{4}} = \frac{i}{2}$
Critical Caution (NCERT Note — Very Frequently Tested)
The property $\sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}$ holds ONLY when both $a$ and $b$ are non-negative real numbers. When $a < 0$ or $b < 0$, you MUST first convert to the $i$ form before multiplying.

WRONG: $\sqrt{-2} \times \sqrt{-3} = \sqrt{(-2)(-3)} = \sqrt{6}$   ❌
CORRECT: $\sqrt{-2} \times \sqrt{-3} = (i\sqrt{2}) \times (i\sqrt{3}) = i^2\sqrt{6} = (-1)\sqrt{6} = -\sqrt{6}$   ✔

Topic 2: Definition & Standard Form of a Complex Number

Core Definition (NCERT)

A number of the form $z = a + ib$, where $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ and $i = \sqrt{-1}$, is called a Complex Number.

  • Real part: $\text{Re}(z) = a$
  • Imaginary part: $\text{Im}(z) = b$  —  The imaginary part is the REAL number $b$, NOT $ib$

The set of all complex numbers is denoted $\mathbb{C}$. The number system hierarchy: $\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R} \subset \mathbb{C}$

Equality of Complex Numbers

Two complex numbers $z_1 = a + ib$ and $z_2 = c + id$ (where $a,b,c,d \in \mathbb{R}$) are equal if and only if:

$$a = c \quad \text{AND} \quad b = d$$

i.e., their real parts are equal AND their imaginary parts are equal (separately).

Important: Complex numbers CANNOT be ordered (compared using < or >). So "$z_1 > z_2$" has no meaning unless both are real.

Topic 3: Algebra of Complex Numbers

3.1 Addition of Complex Numbers

Addition Rule

$(a+ib) + (c+id) = (a+c) + i(b+d)$

Add real parts together and imaginary parts together.

Properties of Addition: Closure, Commutative, Associative, Additive Identity (0+0i), Additive Inverse $(-a-ib)$.

3.2 Subtraction

Subtraction Rule $(a+ib) - (c+id) = (a-c) + i(b-d)$

3.3 Multiplication of Complex Numbers

Multiplication Rule

$(a+ib)(c+id) = ac + iad + ibc + i^2bd = (ac-bd) + i(ad+bc)$

Remember: treat as polynomial multiplication, then replace $i^2 = -1$.

Properties: Closure, Commutative, Associative, Distributive over addition, Multiplicative Identity $(1+0i)$.

3.4 Division of Complex Numbers

Division — Rationalise the Denominator

To compute $\dfrac{a+ib}{c+id}$, multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate of denominator $(c-id)$:

$$\frac{a+ib}{c+id} = \frac{(a+ib)(c-id)}{(c+id)(c-id)} = \frac{(ac+bd)+i(bc-ad)}{c^2+d^2}$$ $$= \frac{ac+bd}{c^2+d^2} + i\frac{bc-ad}{c^2+d^2}$$

The denominator $(c+id)(c-id) = c^2+d^2$ is always a positive real number (when $c+id \neq 0$).

Worked Example: Complete Arithmetic

Let $z_1 = 3+4i$ and $z_2 = 1-2i$. Find all four operations.

Addition: $z_1+z_2 = (3+1)+(4-2)i = \mathbf{4+2i}$

Subtraction: $z_1-z_2 = (3-1)+(4+2)i = \mathbf{2+6i}$

Multiplication: $(3+4i)(1-2i) = 3-6i+4i-8i^2 = 3-2i+8 = \mathbf{11-2i}$

Division: $\dfrac{3+4i}{1-2i} = \dfrac{(3+4i)(1+2i)}{(1-2i)(1+2i)} = \dfrac{3+6i+4i+8i^2}{1+4} = \dfrac{3-8+10i}{5} = \mathbf{-1+2i}$

General Rule for $i^n$: Divide $n$ by 4 and look at remainder $r = n \bmod 4$:

$r=0 \Rightarrow i^n = 1$;   $r=1 \Rightarrow i^n = i$;   $r=2 \Rightarrow i^n = -1$;   $r=3 \Rightarrow i^n = -i$

Negative powers shortcut: $i^{-n} = \overline{i^n}$ (take conjugate). So $i^{-1} = \overline{i} = -i$, $i^{-2} = \overline{-1} = -1$, etc.

Key fact: $i^{4k} = 1$ for any integer $k$. And $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{3} i^n = 0$.
Memory Trick
Cycle: i → -1 → -i → 1 → i → -1 → ... (repeats every 4). Also note: $\dfrac{1}{i} = \dfrac{i}{i^2} = \dfrac{i}{-1} = -i$, so $i^{-1} = -i$.

Topic 5: Modulus and Conjugate of a Complex Number

Modulus (Absolute Value)

For $z = a+ib$, the modulus of $z$ is:

$$|z| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2} \geq 0$$

Geometrically: $|z|$ is the distance of point $(a,b)$ from the origin O in the Argand plane.

Key Properties of Modulus:

  • $|z| = 0 \iff z = 0$    (modulus is zero iff the number itself is zero)
  • $|z| = |\bar{z}|$    (modulus equals modulus of conjugate)
  • $z\bar{z} = |z|^2$    (Most important identity for proofs!)
  • $|z_1 z_2| = |z_1|\cdot|z_2|$    (multiplicative)
  • $\left|\dfrac{z_1}{z_2}\right| = \dfrac{|z_1|}{|z_2|}$, $z_2\neq 0$
  • $|z_1+z_2| \leq |z_1|+|z_2|$    (Triangle Inequality)
  • $\big||z_1|-|z_2|\big| \leq |z_1-z_2|$    (Reverse Triangle Inequality)
  • $|\text{Re}(z)| \leq |z|$  and  $|\text{Im}(z)| \leq |z|$
Conjugate of a Complex Number

For $z = a+ib$, the conjugate $\bar{z}$ is obtained by changing the sign of the imaginary part:

$$\bar{z} = a-ib$$

Geometrically: $\bar{z}$ is the reflection of $z$ across the real (X) axis.

Key Properties of Conjugate:

  • $\overline{(\bar{z})} = z$
  • $z+\bar{z} = 2\,\text{Re}(z) = 2a$   (purely real)
  • $z-\bar{z} = 2i\,\text{Im}(z) = 2ib$   (purely imaginary)
  • $z\bar{z} = a^2+b^2 = |z|^2$
  • $\overline{z_1+z_2} = \bar{z}_1+\bar{z}_2$   and   $\overline{z_1z_2} = \bar{z}_1\cdot\bar{z}_2$
  • $\overline{\left(\dfrac{z_1}{z_2}\right)} = \dfrac{\bar{z}_1}{\bar{z}_2}$
  • $z$ is real $\iff z = \bar{z}$   (imaginary part is zero)
  • $z$ is purely imaginary $\iff z+\bar{z} = 0$ (i.e., $\bar{z} = -z$)
Multiplicative Inverse using Conjugate: $$z^{-1} = \frac{1}{z} = \frac{\bar{z}}{z\bar{z}} = \frac{\bar{z}}{|z|^2}$$

Problem 5.3 (Prove $z$ is purely imaginary) NCERT EX 5.1 Q14

Show that $\dfrac{z-i}{z+i}$ is a purely imaginary number if $|z| = 1$ and $z \neq \pm i$.

Proof:
Let $w = \dfrac{z-i}{z+i}$. Compute $w + \bar{w}$:
$\bar{w} = \dfrac{\bar{z}-(-i)}{\bar{z}+(-i)} = \dfrac{\bar{z}+i}{\bar{z}-i}$   (since conjugate of $i$ is $-i$)
Wait: $\overline{\left(\frac{z-i}{z+i}\right)} = \frac{\bar{z}-\bar{i}}{\bar{z}+\bar{i}} = \frac{\bar{z}+i}{\bar{z}-i}$
$w+\bar{w} = \dfrac{(z-i)(\bar{z}-i)+(z+i)(\bar{z}+i)}{(z+i)(\bar{z}-i)}$
Num: $(z\bar{z}-iz-i\bar{z}-1)+(z\bar{z}+iz+i\bar{z}-1) = 2|z|^2-2 = 2(1)-2 = 0$ (using $|z|=1$)
Since $w+\bar{w}=0$, $w$ is purely imaginary. ■

$(z_1+z_2)\overline{(z_1+z_2)} = (z_1-z_2)\overline{(z_1-z_2)}$

$2(z_1\bar{z}_2+\bar{z}_1 z_2) = 0 \Rightarrow z_1\bar{z}_2+\overline{z_1\bar{z}_2} = 0 \Rightarrow 2\,\text{Re}(z_1\bar{z}_2) = 0$
So $\text{Re}(z_1\bar{z}_2)=0$, meaning $z_1\bar{z}_2$ is purely imaginary $\Rightarrow \arg(z_1\bar{z}_2) = \pm\dfrac{\pi}{2}$
$\arg(z_1)-\arg(z_2) = \arg\!\left(\dfrac{z_1}{z_2}\right) = \arg(z_1\bar{z}_2) = \pm\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ ■

Topic 6: Argand Plane & Geometric Representation

Argand Plane / Complex Plane
  • Every complex number $z = a+ib$ corresponds uniquely to the ordered pair $(a,b)$ and thus to a unique point in the Cartesian plane.
  • The $X$-axis is called the Real Axis (all purely real numbers lie here).
  • The $Y$-axis is called the Imaginary Axis (all purely imaginary numbers lie here).
  • This plane is called the Argand Plane or the Complex Plane, named after mathematician Jean-Robert Argand (1806).
Geometric Interpretations
  • Addition (Parallelogram Law): $z_1+z_2$ corresponds to the diagonal of the parallelogram formed by position vectors of $z_1$ and $z_2$ from origin.
  • Subtraction: $z_1-z_2$ corresponds to the vector from $P_2$ to $P_1$ (or the sum $z_1+(-z_2)$).
  • Modulus $|z|$: The length of position vector $\overrightarrow{OP}$.
  • $|z_1-z_2|$: The distance between the points $P_1$ and $P_2$ representing $z_1$ and $z_2$.
  • Conjugate $\bar{z}$: Reflection of point $z$ in the Real (X) Axis.
  • $-z$: Reflection of point $z$ through the Origin.
  • $iz$: Rotation of $z$ by $90°$ anticlockwise about the origin.

Topic 7: Polar Form (Modulus-Argument Form)

Polar Representation (NCERT)

For $z = a+ib \neq 0$, let $r = |z| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ and let $\theta$ be the angle the vector $\overrightarrow{OP}$ makes with the positive Real axis. Then:

$$\boxed{z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)}$$

This is the Polar Form or Modulus-Argument Form of $z$, where:

  • $r = |z|$ is the modulus
  • $\theta = \arg(z)$ is the argument (angle in radians)
  • Relations: $a = r\cos\theta$ and $b = r\sin\theta$
Principal Argument

The unique value of $\theta$ in the range $(-\pi, \pi]$ is called the Principal Argument, denoted $\text{Arg}(z)$.

The general argument: $\arg(z) = \text{Arg}(z) + 2k\pi$ for any integer $k$.

Let $\alpha = \tan^{-1}\!\left|\dfrac{b}{a}\right|$ (reference angle, always $\geq 0$):

Quadrant$a$$b$$\theta = \text{Arg}(z)$
I$+$$+$$\theta = \alpha$
II$-$$+$$\theta = \pi - \alpha$
III$-$$-$$\theta = -(\pi-\alpha) = \alpha-\pi$
IV$+$$-$$\theta = -\alpha$

Special cases: $\arg(1)=0$, $\arg(-1)=\pi$, $\arg(i)=\dfrac{\pi}{2}$, $\arg(-i)=-\dfrac{\pi}{2}$

Fig 5.3: Polar Form Representation $z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$, with $r = |z|$ (Modulus) and $\theta = \arg(z)$ (Principal Argument)
Properties of Argument: $$\arg(z_1 z_2) = \arg(z_1)+\arg(z_2) \qquad \arg\!\left(\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right) = \arg(z_1)-\arg(z_2)$$ $$\arg(\bar{z}) = -\arg(z) \qquad \arg(z^n) = n\cdot\arg(z)$$ (All equalities hold modulo $2\pi$, i.e., up to a multiple of $2\pi$.)

Topic 8: Square Roots of Negative Numbers & Complex Numbers

Square Root of a Negative Real Number

For any real $a > 0$: $\sqrt{-a} = i\sqrt{a}$ (two square roots: $+i\sqrt{a}$ and $-i\sqrt{a}$).

Square Root of a Complex Number $a+ib$

Let $\sqrt{a+ib} = x+iy$ (where $x, y \in \mathbb{R}$). Squaring both sides:

$(x+iy)^2 = a+ib \Rightarrow x^2-y^2 = a$ …(i)   and   $2xy = b$ …(ii)

Also, taking moduli: $|x+iy|^2 = |a+ib| \Rightarrow x^2+y^2 = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ …(iii)

From (i) and (iii):

$$x^2 = \frac{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}+a}{2}, \qquad y^2 = \frac{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}-a}{2}$$

Take positive square roots, then choose sign of $y$ same as sign of $b$ (since $2xy = b$). Both $(x+iy)$ and $-(x+iy) = (-x-iy)$ are square roots.

Worked Example: Square Root of Complex Number

Find $\sqrt{-5+12i}$.

Step 1: Let $\sqrt{-5+12i} = x+iy$. Equations: $x^2-y^2=-5$ …(i) and $2xy=12$ …(ii).

Step 2: $x^2+y^2 = \sqrt{(-5)^2+12^2} = \sqrt{169} = 13$ …(iii).

Step 3: (i)+(iii): $2x^2=8 \Rightarrow x^2=4 \Rightarrow x=\pm2$. (iii)-(i): $2y^2=18 \Rightarrow y^2=9 \Rightarrow y=\pm3$.

Step 4: Since $2xy=12>0$, $x$ and $y$ have the same sign.

Answer: $\sqrt{-5+12i} = \mathbf{2+3i}$ or $\mathbf{-2-3i}$.

Topic 9: Quadratic Equations with Complex Roots

Discriminant & Nature of Roots

For $ax^2+bx+c=0$ with real coefficients $a,b,c$ ($a\neq 0$), the discriminant is $D = b^2-4ac$:

  • $D > 0$: two distinct real roots $\dfrac{-b\pm\sqrt{D}}{2a}$
  • $D = 0$: two equal real roots $\dfrac{-b}{2a}$ (repeated root)
  • $D < 0$: two distinct complex conjugate roots (no real roots)
Quadratic Formula (extended to complex case): $$x = \frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$$ When $D = b^2-4ac < 0$: $\sqrt{D} = \sqrt{-|D|} = i\sqrt{|D|}$, giving: $$x = \frac{-b\pm i\sqrt{|D|}}{2a}$$ The two roots are complex conjugates of each other (for real $a,b,c$).
Quadratic with Complex Coefficients

If the quadratic $az^2+bz+c=0$ has complex coefficients ($a,b,c \in \mathbb{C}$), the formula still applies, but:

  • The roots need NOT be complex conjugates of each other.
  • Compute $\sqrt{b^2-4ac}$ using the method from Topic 8.
Vieta's Formulas (Sum & Product of Roots)

If $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are roots of $az^2+bz+c=0$, then:

$$\alpha+\beta = -\frac{b}{a} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \alpha\beta = \frac{c}{a}$$

For complex conjugate roots $\alpha = p+qi$ and $\beta = p-qi$ (real coefficients):

$$\alpha+\beta = 2p \text{ (real)}, \qquad \alpha\beta = p^2+q^2 \text{ (real, positive)}$$
Worked Examples

Solve $x^2+x+1=0$: $D=1-4=-3$; $x=\dfrac{-1\pm i\sqrt{3}}{2}$   (these are $\omega$ and $\omega^2$, cube roots of unity!)

Solve $x^2+4x+8=0$: $D=16-32=-16$; $x=\dfrac{-4\pm 4i}{2} = -2\pm 2i$

Form eq. with roots $2+3i, 2-3i$: Sum $=4$, Product $=4+9=13$. Equation: $x^2-4x+13=0$.

Topic 10: Important Identities, Cube Roots of Unity & Mixed Results

10.1 Key Algebraic Identities

Important Results:
  • $z^2+1 = (z+i)(z-i)$
  • $x^2+y^2 = (x+iy)(x-iy) = z\bar{z}$  where $z=x+iy$
  • $\text{Re}(z) = \dfrac{z+\bar{z}}{2}$   and   $\text{Im}(z) = \dfrac{z-\bar{z}}{2i}$
  • $z^{-1} = \dfrac{\bar{z}}{|z|^2}$   (multiplicative inverse)
  • If $a+ib = 0$ where $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$, then $a=0$ AND $b=0$
  • $\text{Re}(iz) = -\text{Im}(z)$   and   $\text{Im}(iz) = \text{Re}(z)$

10.2 Cube Roots of Unity ($\omega$)

Cube Roots of Unity — The Magic of $\omega$

The three cube roots of unity (solutions of $x^3=1$, i.e., $x^3-1=0$) are:

$$1, \quad \omega = \frac{-1+i\sqrt{3}}{2}, \quad \omega^2 = \frac{-1-i\sqrt{3}}{2}$$

Fundamental Properties (Must Memorize):

  • $\omega^3 = 1$     $1+\omega+\omega^2 = 0$     $\omega \cdot \omega^2 = 1$
  • $\omega^2 = \bar{\omega}$ (they are complex conjugates)
  • $|\omega| = |\omega^2| = 1$
  • $\omega$ is a root of $x^2+x+1=0$ (since $x^3-1=(x-1)(x^2+x+1)$)
  • $1+\omega^n+\omega^{2n} = 3$ if $3|n$, else $= 0$
  • $x^3+y^3+z^3-3xyz = (x+y+z)(x+\omega y+\omega^2 z)(x+\omega^2 y+\omega z)$

10.3 De Moivre's Theorem (Reference)

De Moivre's Theorem (Reference for Class 11)

For any integer $n$:

$$(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n = \cos n\theta+i\sin n\theta$$

This is used to find powers and roots of complex numbers efficiently and to prove $\alpha^n+\beta^n$ identities.

Assertion (A): $z = \cos60^\circ+i\sin60^\circ$ satisfies $z^6=1$.

Options: (a) Both A & R true, R explains A   (b) A true, R false   (c) A false, R true   (d) Both false

Answer:
$z^6=(\cos60^\circ+i\sin60^\circ)^6=\cos360^\circ+i\sin360^\circ=1+0=1$ ✔. A is true.
R (De Moivre's theorem) is also true and is the reason A works.

Mixed 5 (5 marks) CBSE 2022 / HOTS

If $z = x+iy$ and $w = \dfrac{1-iz}{z-i}$, show that $|w|=1$ implies $z$ is real.

Proof:
$|w|=1 \Rightarrow |w|^2=1 \Rightarrow w\bar{w}=1$
$w=\dfrac{1-iz}{z-i}$. $|w|^2=\dfrac{|1-iz|^2}{|z-i|^2}=1 \Rightarrow |1-iz|^2=|z-i|^2$
