1. Scalars and Vectors

Motion in a plane (2-D motion) needs two coordinates to describe position — so the ± sign trick of 1-D no longer works. We need the full machinery of vectors. Examples of 2-D motion: projectile motion, circular motion, a boat crossing a river, rain falling on a moving man.

Definitions

Scalar: A physical quantity with magnitude only. It follows ordinary algebra. Examples: distance, speed, mass, time, temperature, work, energy, power, electric charge.

Vector: A physical quantity with magnitude and direction which obeys the laws of vector addition (triangle/parallelogram law). Examples: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum, weight, electric field.

⭐ CONCEPTUAL TRAP — "Direction alone is not enough"

Electric current has direction but is a scalar — because currents add by ordinary algebra, not by the triangle law. Similarly, pressure and surface tension are scalars. A quantity is a vector only if it obeys vector addition. This exact point is asked in Boards, NEET and NDA.

1.1 Types of Vectors (Full List)

Type Meaning
Equal vectors Same magnitude AND same direction (position doesn't matter)
Negative vector Same magnitude, opposite direction: $-\vec{A}$
Zero (null) vector Magnitude 0, arbitrary direction. E.g., displacement of a round trip; $\vec{A} + (-\vec{A}) = \vec{0}$
Unit vector Magnitude 1; gives only direction: $\hat{A} = \dfrac{\vec{A}}{|\vec{A}|}$. Along axes: $\hat{i}, \hat{j}, \hat{k}$
Collinear / Parallel vectors Along the same line / same direction (θ = 0°); anti-parallel: θ = 180°
Coplanar vectors All lying in the same plane
Position vector $\vec{r} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j}$ — locates a particle w.r.t. origin
Axial vector Represents rotational effect, along the axis via right-hand rule (e.g., angular velocity, torque)
Key Points
  • Multiplying a vector by a positive scalar changes only magnitude; by a negative scalar reverses direction too.
  • A vector does not change if it is shifted parallel to itself (free vector).
  • Vectors of different physical quantities (e.g., force and velocity) can never be added.

2. Addition and Subtraction of Vectors

2.1 Triangle Law of Vector Addition

If two vectors are represented (in magnitude and direction) by the two sides of a triangle taken in order, their resultant is represented by the third side taken in the opposite order: $\vec{R} = \vec{A} + \vec{B}$ — draw $\vec{B}$ from the head of $\vec{A}$; $\vec{R}$ runs from the tail of $\vec{A}$ to the head of $\vec{B}$.

2.2 Parallelogram Law — Magnitude & Direction of the Resultant (Full Derivation)

Let $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$ act at a point with angle θ between them. Complete the parallelogram; the diagonal from the common tail is $\vec{R}$. Drop a perpendicular from the head of $\vec{R}$'s far corner to the extended line of $\vec{A}$ (foot N, corner C).

Derivation. In the right triangle, base $= A + B\cos\theta$ and height $= B\sin\theta$. By Pythagoras:

$$R^{2} = (A + B\cos\theta)^{2} + (B\sin\theta)^{2}$$ $$R^{2} = A^{2} + 2AB\cos\theta + B^{2}\cos^{2}\theta + B^{2}\sin^{2}\theta$$
Resultant — Magnitude & Direction $$\boxed{\,R = \sqrt{A^{2} + B^{2} + 2AB\cos\theta}\,}$$ $$\tan\beta = \frac{B\sin\theta}{A + B\cos\theta}$$

where β is the angle of $\vec{R}$ with $\vec{A}$.

2.3 Special Cases (Must Memorise)

Angle θ Resultant R Remark
0° (same direction) $R = A + B$ Maximum possible resultant
90° $R = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2}$ $\tan\beta = B/A$
180° (opposite) $R = |A - B|$ Minimum possible resultant
Equal vectors, angle θ $R = 2A\cos(\theta/2)$ β = θ/2 (resultant bisects the angle)
Equal vectors, θ = 120° $R = A$ Classic MCQ: resultant equals each vector!
Range of Resultant

$$|A - B| \;\le\; R \;\le\; A + B$$ The resultant of two vectors can never exceed A + B and can never be smaller than |A − B|. Quick check: forces 4 N and 3 N can give a resultant of 2 N (yes, since 1 ≤ R ≤ 7) but never 8 N. Three equal vectors at 120° to each other sum to zero.

The red resultant lives on the bisector and shrinks from 2A toward 0 as θ opens toward 180° — catch the instant it equals A: that is θ = 120°.

2.4 Polygon Law, Properties & Vector Subtraction

  • Polygon law: many vectors drawn head-to-tail; resultant = closing side (tail of first to head of last). If the polygon closes, resultant = 0.
  • Commutative: $\vec{A}+\vec{B} = \vec{B}+\vec{A}$; Associative: $(\vec{A}+\vec{B})+\vec{C} = \vec{A}+(\vec{B}+\vec{C})$.
  • Subtraction: $\vec{A}-\vec{B} = \vec{A}+(-\vec{B})$, with magnitude $$|\vec{A}-\vec{B}| = \sqrt{A^{2}+B^{2}-2AB\cos\theta}$$
  • For equal magnitudes at angle θ: $|\vec{A}-\vec{B}| = 2A\sin(\theta/2)$.
  • $|\vec{A}+\vec{B}| = |\vec{A}-\vec{B}| \Rightarrow \theta = 90°$ (a favourite JEE/NEET one-liner).

✍ IN-TEXT PRACTICE 2.1 — Boards / NEET

Q. Two forces of 5 N and 12 N act at a point. Find the resultant when the angle between them is (a) 0°, (b) 90°, (c) 180°.

(a) θ = 0°: $R = 5 + 12 = \mathbf{17 \text{ N}}$ (maximum)
(b) θ = 90°: $R = \sqrt{5^2 + 12^2} = \sqrt{169} = \mathbf{13 \text{ N}}$, at $\tan\beta = 12/5$ with the 5 N force
(c) θ = 180°: $R = |12 - 5| = \mathbf{7 \text{ N}}$ (minimum)
✍ IN-TEXT PRACTICE 2.2 — JEE Main pattern

Q. The resultant of two equal forces F each is also F. Find the angle between the two forces.

Use: $R = 2F\cos(\theta/2)$ for equal vectors.
$F = 2F\cos(\theta/2) \Rightarrow \cos(\theta/2) = \tfrac12 \Rightarrow \theta/2 = 60°$
Answer: $\theta = \mathbf{120°}$

3. Resolution of Vectors & Rectangular Components

Resolution is the reverse of addition: splitting one vector into two (or three) components whose sum is the original vector. The most useful split is along perpendicular (rectangular) axes.

Rectangular Components

If $\vec{A}$ makes angle θ with the x-axis:

$$A_x = A\cos\theta, \qquad A_y = A\sin\theta$$ $$\vec{A} = A_x\hat{i} + A_y\hat{j}$$ $$A = \sqrt{A_x^{2} + A_y^{2}}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{A_y}{A_x}$$
  • Rule of thumb: the component "touching" the angle gets cos θ; the one "away" from the angle gets sin θ.
  • A component of a vector can never exceed its magnitude ($\cos\theta, \sin\theta \le 1$).
  • In 3-D: $\vec{A} = A_x\hat{i} + A_y\hat{j} + A_z\hat{k}$, $A = \sqrt{A_x^2+A_y^2+A_z^2}$, with direction cosines $\cos\alpha = A_x/A$ etc., and $\cos^2\alpha+\cos^2\beta+\cos^2\gamma = 1$.
  • Component method of addition (the practical method): $\vec{R} = \vec{A}+\vec{B} \Rightarrow R_x = A_x + B_x,\ R_y = A_y + B_y$. Add components, then recombine.

✍ IN-TEXT PRACTICE 3.1 — Boards / NDA

Q. A force of 50 N acts at 37° above the horizontal (sin 37° = 0.6, cos 37° = 0.8). Find its horizontal and vertical components.

Horizontal: $F_x = 50\cos 37° = 50 \times 0.8 = \mathbf{40 \text{ N}}$
Vertical: $F_y = 50\sin 37° = 50 \times 0.6 = \mathbf{30 \text{ N}}$
Check: $\sqrt{40^2+30^2} = 50$ N ✓
✍ IN-TEXT PRACTICE 3.2 — JEE Main (Component method)

Q. $\vec{A} = 3\hat{i} + 4\hat{j}$ and $\vec{B} = \hat{i} - 2\hat{j}$. Find $|\vec{A} + \vec{B}|$ and the unit vector along $\vec{A} + \vec{B}$.

Sum: $\vec{A}+\vec{B} = (3+1)\hat{i} + (4-2)\hat{j} = 4\hat{i} + 2\hat{j}$
Magnitude: $\sqrt{16+4} = \sqrt{20} = \mathbf{2\sqrt{5}}$
Unit vector: $\hat{n} = \dfrac{4\hat{i}+2\hat{j}}{2\sqrt5} = \dfrac{2\hat{i}+\hat{j}}{\sqrt5}$

4. Multiplication of Vectors (Dot & Cross Product)

4.1 Scalar (Dot) Product

Dot Product $$\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B} = AB\cos\theta = A_xB_x + A_yB_y + A_zB_z$$
  • Result is a scalar. Commutative and distributive.
  • $\hat{i}\cdot\hat{i} = \hat{j}\cdot\hat{j} = \hat{k}\cdot\hat{k} = 1$; $\hat{i}\cdot\hat{j} = \hat{j}\cdot\hat{k} = \hat{k}\cdot\hat{i} = 0$.
  • $\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B} = 0$ (non-zero vectors) ⟹ they are perpendicular — the standard test.
  • $\vec{A}\cdot\vec{A} = A^2$; angle between vectors: $\cos\theta = \dfrac{\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}}{AB}$.
  • Physics use: Work $W = \vec{F}\cdot\vec{s}$, Power $P = \vec{F}\cdot\vec{v}$.

4.2 Vector (Cross) Product

Cross Product $$|\vec{A}\times\vec{B}| = AB\sin\theta$$

Direction: perpendicular to the plane of $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$ by the right-hand rule (curl fingers from A to B; thumb gives $\vec{A}\times\vec{B}$).

  • Not commutative: $\vec{A}\times\vec{B} = -\vec{B}\times\vec{A}$.
  • $\hat{i}\times\hat{j} = \hat{k},\ \hat{j}\times\hat{k} = \hat{i},\ \hat{k}\times\hat{i} = \hat{j}$; $\vec{A}\times\vec{A} = \vec{0}$.
  • $\vec{A}\times\vec{B} = \vec{0}$ (non-zero vectors) ⟹ they are parallel or anti-parallel.
  • $|\vec{A}\times\vec{B}|$ = area of the parallelogram formed by the two vectors.
  • Physics use: torque $\vec{\tau} = \vec{r}\times\vec{F}$, angular momentum, $\vec{v} = \vec{\omega}\times\vec{r}$.
✍ IN-TEXT PRACTICE 4.1 — NEET / JEE one-liner

Q. Find the value of m so that $\vec{A} = 2\hat{i} + 3\hat{j}$ is perpendicular to $\vec{B} = 3\hat{i} + m\hat{j}$.

Perpendicular ⟹ dot product zero: $\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B} = 6 + 3m = 0$
Answer: $m = \mathbf{-2}$

5. Motion in a Plane — Position, Velocity & Acceleration Vectors

2-D Kinematics Definitions $$\vec{r} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j}, \qquad \Delta\vec{r} = \vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1$$ $$\vec{v} = \frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} = v_x\hat{i} + v_y\hat{j}, \qquad v = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}$$ $$\vec{a} = \frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} = a_x\hat{i} + a_y\hat{j}$$
  • The instantaneous velocity is always tangent to the path.
  • In 2-D, acceleration can change the direction of velocity even at constant speed (uniform circular motion) — impossible in 1-D.
  • The angle between $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{a}$ decides the motion: 0° → speeding up along a straight line; 180° → slowing; 90° → circular; any other fixed angle with constant $\vec{a}$ → parabolic (projectile).
Figure 2b

Velocity is tangent to the path; the v–a angle decides the shape of motion

The four cases of the bullet list above, drawn: green is v, red is a.

5.1 Motion with Constant Acceleration in a Plane

The Master Principle

(NCERT's most important statement in this chapter): Motion in a plane is two independent 1-D motions along perpendicular axes. Each axis obeys its own set of kinematic equations:

$$\vec{v} = \vec{u} + \vec{a}t \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \begin{cases} v_x = u_x + a_x t \\ v_y = u_y + a_y t \end{cases}$$ $$\vec{r} = \vec{u}t + \tfrac12 \vec{a}t^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \begin{cases} x = u_x t + \tfrac12 a_x t^2 \\ y = u_y t + \tfrac12 a_y t^2 \end{cases}$$

Time t is the only quantity common to both axes. This single idea solves all of projectile motion.

✍ IN-TEXT PRACTICE 5.1 — NCERT / JEE (Calculus in 2-D)

Q. The position of a particle is $\vec{r} = 3t\hat{i} + 2t^{2}\hat{j}$ (metres, seconds). Find velocity and acceleration at t = 2 s, and the speed at that instant.

Velocity: $\vec{v} = \dfrac{d\vec{r}}{dt} = 3\hat{i} + 4t\hat{j}$; at t = 2 s: $\vec{v} = 3\hat{i} + 8\hat{j}$ m/s
Acceleration: $\vec{a} = \dfrac{d\vec{v}}{dt} = 4\hat{j}$ m/s² (constant, along y)
Speed: $v = \sqrt{3^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{73} \approx \mathbf{8.54 \text{ m/s}}$

6. Relative Velocity in Two Dimensions

Relative Velocity (2-D) $$\vec{v}_{AB} = \vec{v}_A - \vec{v}_B$$

Same rule as 1-D, but now the subtraction is a vector subtraction: $|\vec{v}_{AB}| = \sqrt{v_A^2 + v_B^2 - 2v_Av_B\cos\theta}$.

6.1 River–Boat Problems (Complete Theory)

Let river speed = $v_r$ (along the bank), boat speed in still water = $v_b$, river width = d.

Goal How to aim Results
Shortest TIME Point straight across (⊥ to bank) $t_{min} = \dfrac{d}{v_b}$; drift $x = v_r t_{min} = \dfrac{v_r d}{v_b}$; resultant speed $\sqrt{v_b^2+v_r^2}$
Shortest PATH (zero drift) Aim upstream at angle θ with the ⊥ so that $v_b\sin\theta = v_r$ $\sin\theta = \dfrac{v_r}{v_b}$ (possible only if $v_b > v_r$); crossing speed = $\sqrt{v_b^2 - v_r^2}$; $t = \dfrac{d}{\sqrt{v_b^2 - v_r^2}}$
⭐ EXAM TRAP

If $v_r > v_b$, zero drift is impossible — the boat can only minimise drift, not eliminate it. Also note: crossing time depends only on the perpendicular component of boat velocity, never on the river speed.

6.2 Rain–Man Problems

Method

Velocity of rain relative to man: $\vec{v}_{rm} = \vec{v}_r - \vec{v}_m$. The man must hold his umbrella along $\vec{v}_{rm}$.

Standard case: rain falls vertically at $v_r$; man walks horizontally at $v_m$. Then $\vec{v}_{rm}$ makes angle θ with the vertical where $$\tan\theta = \frac{v_m}{v_r}$$ — tilt the umbrella forward (in the direction of walking) by θ. Apparent speed of rain $= \sqrt{v_r^2 + v_m^2}$.

✍ IN-TEXT PRACTICE 6.1 — NEET / JEE (River–Boat)

Q. A boat can move at 5 m/s in still water. It crosses a 100 m wide river flowing at 3 m/s. Find (a) the minimum crossing time and the drift in that case, (b) the crossing time for the shortest path.

(a) Minimum time (aim ⊥): $t = d/v_b = 100/5 = \mathbf{20 \text{ s}}$; drift $= v_r t = 3\times20 = \mathbf{60 \text{ m}}$
(b) Shortest path (zero drift): effective speed $= \sqrt{5^2-3^2} = 4$ m/s → $t = 100/4 = \mathbf{25 \text{ s}}$ (aim upstream at $\sin\theta = 3/5 \Rightarrow \theta = 37°$ from the perpendicular)
✍ IN-TEXT PRACTICE 6.2 — Boards / NDA (Rain–Man)

Q. Rain falls vertically at 4 m/s. A man walks at 3 m/s on a straight road. At what angle from the vertical must he hold his umbrella, and what is the apparent speed of the rain?

Angle: $\tan\theta = v_m/v_r = 3/4 \Rightarrow \theta = \mathbf{37°}$ from the vertical, tilted forward
Apparent speed: $\sqrt{4^2+3^2} = \mathbf{5 \text{ m/s}}$

7. Projectile Motion (Ground-to-Ground) — Complete Theory

A projectile is a body thrown with some initial velocity that then moves under gravity alone (air resistance neglected). Its path is a parabola. Throughout the flight: $a_x = 0$ and $a_y = -g$ (taking up as +).

Setup

Projectile fired from the ground with speed u at angle θ with the horizontal. Components: $u_x = u\cos\theta$ (never changes) and $u_y = u\sin\theta$ (changes due to gravity). The horizontal motion is uniform; the vertical motion is exactly a "ball thrown up" from the 1-D chapter. The two are linked only by time.

7.1 Equation of Trajectory (Derivation)

Derivation. Horizontal: $x = (u\cos\theta)t \Rightarrow t = \dfrac{x}{u\cos\theta}$. Substitute in vertical: $y = (u\sin\theta)t - \tfrac12 gt^2$:

$$y = x\tan\theta - \frac{gx^{2}}{2u^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}$$

This is of the form $y = ax - bx^2$ — a parabola. Alternative exam-favourite form: $y = x\tan\theta\left(1 - \dfrac{x}{R}\right)$ where R is the range.

7.2 Time of Flight, Maximum Height & Range (Derivations)

Time of flight T: vertical displacement = 0 at landing: $0 = (u\sin\theta)T - \tfrac12 gT^2$

Maximum height H: at the top $v_y = 0$: use $v_y^2 = u_y^2 - 2gH$

Range R: $R = u_x \times T = u\cos\theta \cdot \dfrac{2u\sin\theta}{g}$, then use $2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \sin 2\theta$.

The Big Three (Must Memorise) $$T = \frac{2u\sin\theta}{g} \qquad H = \frac{u^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{2g} \qquad R = \frac{u^{2}\sin 2\theta}{g}$$

7.3 Key Results & Relations (Complete List)

  • Maximum range: at θ = 45°, $R_{max} = \dfrac{u^2}{g}$, and then $H = \dfrac{R_{max}}{4}$.
  • Complementary angles: θ and (90° − θ) give the same range (since $\sin 2\theta = \sin(180° - 2\theta)$), but different H and T. If $H_1, H_2$ are their heights: $R = 4\sqrt{H_1H_2}$ and $T_1T_2 = \dfrac{2R}{g}$.
  • Relation between R and H: $\dfrac{R}{H} = \dfrac{4}{\tan\theta}$ ⟹ $\tan\theta = \dfrac{4H}{R}$. If R = 4H exactly, θ = 45°; if H = R, tan θ = 4.
  • Velocity at time t: $v_x = u\cos\theta$, $v_y = u\sin\theta - gt$; speed $= \sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}$; direction $\tan\alpha = v_y/v_x$.
  • At the top: velocity $= u\cos\theta$ (horizontal, minimum speed of flight), acceleration is still g downward, and $\vec{v} \perp \vec{a}$ there.
  • Speed symmetry: speed (and angle magnitude) at the same height is equal going up and coming down; landing speed = launch speed u at angle −θ.
  • Change in velocity over the whole flight: $\Delta\vec{v} = \vec{v}_{land} - \vec{v}_{launch} = -gT\hat{j}$ (magnitude $2u\sin\theta$, vertically downward). Change in momentum $= 2mu\sin\theta$ downward.
  • Projectile motion is a motion with constant acceleration but continuously changing velocity (direction changes every instant).
  • Kinetic energy at the top $= \tfrac12 m(u\cos\theta)^2 = KE_{launch}\cos^2\theta$ (a NEET favourite).

✍ IN-TEXT PRACTICE 7.1 — Boards / NEET (Direct formulas)

Q. A ball is projected at 40 m/s at 30° above the horizontal (g = 10 m/s²). Find T, H and R.

T: $\dfrac{2\times40\times\sin30°}{10} = \dfrac{2\times40\times0.5}{10} = \mathbf{4 \text{ s}}$
H: $\dfrac{40^2\times(0.5)^2}{2\times10} = \dfrac{1600\times0.25}{20} = \mathbf{20 \text{ m}}$
R: $\dfrac{40^2\times\sin60°}{10} = 160\times\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = 80\sqrt3 \approx \mathbf{138.6 \text{ m}}$
✍ IN-TEXT PRACTICE 7.2 — JEE Main (R–H relation)

Q. For a projectile, the maximum height equals the horizontal range. Find the angle of projection.

Set H = R: $\dfrac{u^2\sin^2\theta}{2g} = \dfrac{u^2\sin2\theta}{g} = \dfrac{2u^2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{g}$
$\sin\theta = 4\cos\theta \Rightarrow \tan\theta = 4$
Answer: $\theta = \tan^{-1}(4) \approx \mathbf{76°}$
✍ IN-TEXT PRACTICE 7.3 — NEET (Velocity ⊥ to initial velocity)

Q. A projectile is fired with speed u at angle θ. After how much time is its velocity perpendicular to the initial velocity?

Condition: $\vec{v}\cdot\vec{u} = 0$ with $\vec{u} = u\cos\theta\,\hat{i} + u\sin\theta\,\hat{j}$ and $\vec{v} = u\cos\theta\,\hat{i} + (u\sin\theta - gt)\hat{j}$
$u^2\cos^2\theta + u\sin\theta(u\sin\theta - gt) = 0 \Rightarrow u^2 = ugt\sin\theta$
Answer: $t = \dfrac{u}{g\sin\theta}$ (possible only if this ≤ T, i.e. θ ≥ 45°... check in each problem — a JEE-level subtlety)

8. Projectile from a Height (Horizontal & Angular Projection)

8.1 Horizontal Projectile from Height h

Body thrown horizontally with speed u from height h (e.g., a ball rolled off a table, a packet dropped from a plane... careful: a dropped packet shares the plane's horizontal velocity!).

Horizontal Projectile Results $$\text{Time of fall: } t = \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}} \quad (\text{independent of } u!)$$ $$\text{Horizontal range: } R = u\sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}$$ $$\text{Landing speed: } v = \sqrt{u^{2} + 2gh}, \qquad \tan\alpha = \frac{gt}{u}$$ $$\text{Trajectory: } y = \frac{gx^{2}}{2u^{2}} \ (\text{measuring y downward})$$
⭐ THE MONKEY-AND-BULLET IDEA (HCV)

A ball dropped from rest and a ball thrown horizontally from the same height hit the ground at the same time — vertical motion is completely independent of horizontal velocity. This is the single most repeated conceptual MCQ from this topic in NEET and NDA.

8.2 Projection at an Angle from a Tower

Thrown from height h with speed u at angle θ above horizontal. Take up = +, origin at the top. Landing when vertical displacement = −h:

$$-h = (u\sin\theta)t - \tfrac12 gt^{2}$$

Solve the quadratic for t; then range $= (u\cos\theta)t$. Thrown at θ below the horizontal → just replace $u\sin\theta$ with $-u\sin\theta$. Landing speed always comes fastest from energy form: $v = \sqrt{u^2 + 2gh}$ — independent of the angle of projection (JEE favourite).

✍ IN-TEXT PRACTICE 8.1 — Boards / NDA (Horizontal projectile)

Q. A ball is thrown horizontally at 15 m/s from the top of a 20 m tower (g = 10 m/s²). Find the time of flight, the horizontal distance covered, and the landing speed.

Time: $t = \sqrt{2h/g} = \sqrt{4} = \mathbf{2 \text{ s}}$ (independent of 15 m/s!)
Range: $R = ut = 15\times2 = \mathbf{30 \text{ m}}$
Landing speed: $v = \sqrt{u^2+2gh} = \sqrt{225+400} = \sqrt{625} = \mathbf{25 \text{ m/s}}$
✍ IN-TEXT PRACTICE 8.2 — JEE Main (Packet from an aeroplane)

Q. An aeroplane flying horizontally at 100 m/s at a height of 500 m drops a packet. How far ahead of the target point (horizontally) must it be released, and where is the plane when the packet lands? (g = 10 m/s²)

Key idea: the packet starts with the plane's horizontal velocity (u = 100 m/s), so it is a horizontal projectile.
Time: $t = \sqrt{2\times500/10} = \mathbf{10 \text{ s}}$; release distance $= 100\times10 = \mathbf{1000 \text{ m}}$ before the target.
Plane's position at landing: the plane is directly above the packet (both kept the same horizontal velocity) — classic conceptual finish.

9. Uniform Circular Motion (UCM)

A particle moving on a circle of radius r with constant speed is in uniform circular motion. Speed is constant, but the direction of velocity changes continuously — so there IS acceleration, directed towards the centre.

9.1 Angular Variables

Quantity Definition & Formula SI Unit
Angular displacement θ Angle swept at the centre; $\theta = \dfrac{\text{arc}}{\text{radius}} = \dfrac{s}{r}$ radian (rad)
Angular velocity ω $\omega = \dfrac{d\theta}{dt} = \dfrac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f$ rad/s
Angular acceleration α $\alpha = \dfrac{d\omega}{dt}$ (zero for UCM) rad/s²
Time period T Time for one revolution; $T = \dfrac{2\pi r}{v} = \dfrac{1}{f}$ second
Frequency f Revolutions per second; $f = 1/T$ hertz (Hz) / rps
Linear–Angular Link $$v = \omega r \qquad (\text{vector form: } \vec{v} = \vec{\omega}\times\vec{r})$$

9.2 Centripetal Acceleration (Derivation)

Geometric derivation (NCERT). In a small time Δt the particle turns by Δθ. Velocity vectors at the two instants have equal magnitude v but differ in direction by Δθ. The velocity triangle is similar to the position triangle, so

$$\frac{|\Delta\vec{v}|}{v} = \frac{|\Delta\vec{r}|}{r} \;\Rightarrow\; |\Delta\vec{v}| = \frac{v}{r}|\Delta\vec{r}|$$

Divide by Δt and take the limit ($|\Delta\vec{r}|/\Delta t \to v$):

$$a = \frac{v}{r}\cdot v = \frac{v^{2}}{r}$$

As Δt → 0, Δ$\vec{v}$ becomes perpendicular to $\vec{v}$ and points to the centre — hence the name centripetal ("centre-seeking").

Centripetal Acceleration $$\boxed{\,a_c = \frac{v^{2}}{r} = \omega^{2}r = \frac{4\pi^{2}r}{T^{2}} = 4\pi^{2}f^{2}r\,}$$
  • In UCM: $\vec{a} \perp \vec{v}$ always; $\vec{a}$ constantly changes direction — so UCM is accelerated motion with constant magnitude of acceleration but not constant acceleration vector.
  • Work done by the centripetal force in UCM is zero (force ⊥ displacement) — speed and KE stay constant.
  • Non-uniform circular motion: speed also changes → add a tangential acceleration $a_t = \dfrac{dv}{dt}$; net acceleration $a = \sqrt{a_c^{2} + a_t^{2}}$, at angle $\tan\phi = a_t/a_c$ with the radius.
  • Angular kinematics mirror linear ones (constant α): $\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t$, $\theta = \omega_0 t + \tfrac12\alpha t^2$, $\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\theta$.

✍ IN-TEXT PRACTICE 9.1 — NEET / Boards (Direct UCM)

Q. A particle completes 7 revolutions in 44 s on a circle of radius 10 cm. Find its angular speed, linear speed and centripetal acceleration. (Take π = 22/7)

Time period: $T = 44/7$ s → $\omega = \dfrac{2\pi}{T} = \dfrac{2\times(22/7)}{44/7} = \mathbf{1 \text{ rad/s}}$
Linear speed: $v = \omega r = 1 \times 0.10 = \mathbf{0.1 \text{ m/s}}$
Centripetal acceleration: $a_c = \omega^2 r = 1 \times 0.10 = \mathbf{0.1 \text{ m/s}^2}$ toward the centre
✍ IN-TEXT PRACTICE 9.2 — NCERT classic (Cyclist on a circular track)

Q. A cyclist rides at 27 km/h on a circular track of radius 80 m and applies brakes, reducing speed at 0.5 m/s². Find the magnitude and direction of his net acceleration at that instant.

Convert: $v = 27 \times \tfrac{5}{18} = 7.5$ m/s.
Centripetal: $a_c = v^2/r = 56.25/80 \approx 0.70$ m/s² (toward centre). Tangential: $a_t = 0.5$ m/s² (opposite to velocity, braking).
Net: $a = \sqrt{0.70^2 + 0.5^2} \approx \mathbf{0.86 \text{ m/s}^2}$, at $\tan\phi = a_t/a_c = 0.5/0.70 \Rightarrow \phi \approx 35.5°$ from the radius (toward the backward tangent).

10. Solved Examples — Every Question Type in the Chapter

One worked model of each distinct question type asked from this chapter, tagged by exam. Attempt each yourself first, then check.

✍ TYPE 1 — Boards · State & Prove Laws

Q. State the parallelogram law of vector addition and derive expressions for the magnitude and direction of the resultant (3–5 marks). / Derive a_c = v²/r (3 marks). / Derive T, H, R of a projectile (5 marks).

Where to answer from: Section 2.2 (parallelogram derivation with Fig. 1), Section 9.2 (centripetal derivation with Fig. 6), Sections 7.1–7.2 (projectile derivations with Fig. 4). Reproduce the constructions exactly as in the figures.
✍ TYPE 2 — NEET / NDA · Resultant & Angle Between Vectors

Q. Two vectors of equal magnitude A have a resultant of magnitude A√3. Find the angle between them, and the magnitude of their difference.

Resultant of equal vectors: $R = 2A\cos(\theta/2) = A\sqrt3 \Rightarrow \cos(\theta/2) = \dfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \Rightarrow \theta = \mathbf{60°}$
Difference: $|\vec{A}-\vec{B}| = 2A\sin(\theta/2) = 2A\sin30° = \mathbf{A}$
✍ TYPE 3 — JEE Main · Perpendicularity / Dot-Product Conditions

Q. If $|\vec{A}+\vec{B}| = |\vec{A}-\vec{B}|$, prove the vectors are perpendicular. Also: when is $|\vec{A}+\vec{B}| = |\vec{A}| = |\vec{B}|$?

Square both sides: $A^2+B^2+2AB\cos\theta = A^2+B^2-2AB\cos\theta \Rightarrow \cos\theta = 0 \Rightarrow \theta = \mathbf{90°}$ ✓
Second part: equal vectors with resultant equal to each: $R = 2A\cos(\theta/2) = A \Rightarrow \theta = \mathbf{120°}$ (Section 2.3 special case).
✍ TYPE 4 — NEET · Complementary Angles of Projection

Q. Two projectiles are fired with the same speed at 30° and 60°. Compare their ranges, heights and times of flight.

Ranges: equal (complementary angles): $R_1 = R_2$ ✓
Heights: $\dfrac{H_1}{H_2} = \dfrac{\sin^2 30°}{\sin^2 60°} = \dfrac{1/4}{3/4} = \mathbf{1:3}$
Times: $\dfrac{T_1}{T_2} = \dfrac{\sin30°}{\sin60°} = \mathbf{1:\sqrt3}$; and check $R = 4\sqrt{H_1H_2}$, $T_1T_2 = 2R/g$.
✍ TYPE 5 — JEE Main · Trajectory Equation Given

Q. The trajectory of a projectile is $y = 12x - \dfrac{3}{4}x^{2}$ (metres). Find the angle of projection, range and maximum height. (g = 10 m/s²)

Compare with $y = x\tan\theta\left(1 - \dfrac{x}{R}\right)$: at x → 0, slope = tan θ = 12 → $\theta = \tan^{-1}12$.
Range: y = 0 at $x = R$: $12 = \tfrac34 R \Rightarrow R = \mathbf{16 \text{ m}}$
Max height at $x = R/2 = 8$: $H = 12(8) - \tfrac34(64) = 96 - 48 = \mathbf{48 \text{ m}}$ (or use $H = \tfrac{R\tan\theta}{4}$).
✍ TYPE 6 — NEET / NDA · Speed & KE at the Top

Q. A projectile fired with kinetic energy E at 45°. What is its KE at the highest point?

At the top only vₓ survives: $KE_{top} = E\cos^2\theta = E\cos^2 45° = \mathbf{E/2}$
General: KE_top = E cos²θ; momentum change over full flight = 2mu sin θ downward.
✍ TYPE 7 — JEE · Two Projectiles / Same Range Times

Q. A projectile has the same range R for two angles of projection with times of flight t₁ and t₂. Prove that $R = \tfrac12 g\,t_1 t_2$.

Complementary angles θ and 90°−θ: $t_1 = \dfrac{2u\sin\theta}{g}$, $t_2 = \dfrac{2u\cos\theta}{g}$
$t_1 t_2 = \dfrac{4u^2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{g^2} = \dfrac{2u^2\sin2\theta}{g^2} = \dfrac{2R}{g}$
∴ $R = \tfrac12 g\,t_1t_2$ ✓ (a very frequent proof question)
✍ TYPE 8 — JEE Main · River Crossing at Minimum Drift ($v_r > v_b$ case)

Q. A swimmer can swim at 3 m/s in still water; the river flows at 5 m/s. At what angle should he swim to minimise drift? (Note $v_r > v_b$ — zero drift impossible.)

Result (derived via calculus/geometry): aim upstream at angle θ with the perpendicular where $\sin\theta = \dfrac{v_b}{v_r} = \dfrac{3}{5} \Rightarrow \theta = 37°$ — i.e., swim at 90° + 37° = 127° from the river-flow direction.
Contrast with Section 6.1: when $v_b > v_r$ the zero-drift condition is $\sin\theta = v_r/v_b$; when $v_b < v_r$ the minimum-drift condition flips to $\sin\theta = v_b/v_r$. Both are asked in JEE.
✍ TYPE 9 — NEET · Ratio of Centripetal Accelerations

Q. Two particles move on circles of radii in ratio 1 : 2. Compare their centripetal accelerations when (a) their speeds are equal, (b) their time periods are equal.

(a) Same v: $a_c = v^2/r \propto 1/r \Rightarrow a_1 : a_2 = \mathbf{2 : 1}$
(b) Same T: $a_c = 4\pi^2 r/T^2 \propto r \Rightarrow a_1 : a_2 = \mathbf{1 : 2}$
Moral: pick the formula whose "same" quantity is given — the most common UCM MCQ pattern.
✍ TYPE 10 — HC Verma Level · Projectile Hitting an Incline / Wall (Coordinate Attack)

Q. A ball is thrown at 20 m/s at 53° from the foot of a vertical wall 12 m away (sin 53° = 0.8, cos 53° = 0.6, g = 10 m/s²). At what height does it hit the wall?

Components: $u_x = 20\times0.6 = 12$ m/s, $u_y = 20\times0.8 = 16$ m/s.
Time to reach wall: $t = x/u_x = 12/12 = 1$ s.
Height: $y = u_yt - \tfrac12 gt^2 = 16 - 5 = \mathbf{11 \text{ m}}$
Method moral: any "hits a wall / clears a fence / lands on a roof" question = find t from the horizontal equation, substitute in the vertical equation.

11. Common Mistakes & Misconceptions — Final Checklist

Night Before Exam

Read this the night before the exam:

  1. A quantity with direction is not automatically a vector — it must obey the triangle law (current is a scalar!).
  2. Resultant range: $|A-B| \le R \le A+B$. Instantly eliminate impossible MCQ options.
  3. Component "touching" the angle → cos θ; away from the angle → sin θ. Don't swap them.
  4. In projectile motion, $u\cos\theta$ never changes; only $v_y$ evolves. Time links the two axes.
  5. At the top of a projectile: v ≠ 0 (it equals u cos θ); only $v_y = 0$. Acceleration is still g.
  6. Complementary angles → same range but different H and T. Same range does NOT mean same trajectory.
  7. Time of fall of a horizontal projectile is $\sqrt{2h/g}$ — independent of horizontal speed.
  8. A body dropped from a moving carrier (plane/balloon/train) inherits the carrier's velocity.
  9. River crossing time depends only on the velocity component perpendicular to the bank.
  10. Zero drift needs $v_b > v_r$; otherwise only minimum drift is possible (and the aiming formula flips).
  11. UCM has constant speed but is still accelerated motion; $\vec{a}\perp\vec{v}$ and W = 0 by the centripetal force.
  12. Use $a_c = v^2/r$ when v is common, $a_c = \omega^2 r$ or $4\pi^2r/T^2$ when ω or T is common.
  13. Dot product zero → perpendicular; cross product zero → parallel. Don't interchange the tests.
  14. Landing speed from a height is $\sqrt{u^2+2gh}$ regardless of launch angle (energy) — but landing TIME does depend on the angle.
