ICSE Class 10 Physics — Chapter 8: Current Electricity

(A) Concept of Charge, Current, Potential, Potential Difference, and Resistance; Ohm's Law

8.1 Concept of Electric Charge

Electric Charge ($q$ or $Q$): An intrinsic property of elementary particles that gives rise to electric forces between them. When two bodies (such as glass and silk, or ebonite and fur) are rubbed together, electrons are transferred from one body to another.

  • The body which gains electrons becomes negatively charged (excess of electrons).
  • The body which loses electrons becomes positively charged (deficit of electrons).
  • Fundamental Law: Like charges repel each other, while unlike charges attract each other.
  • Quantization of Charge: The total charge on a body is always an integral multiple of the elementary charge $e$.
QUANTIZATION FORMULA $$ q = \pm n e $$

Where $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ (number of electrons in excess or deficit) and $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$ (charge of one electron).

Units of Charge:

  • SI Unit of charge: Coulomb (symbol C).
  • Smaller units: $1 \text{ mC (milli-coulomb)} = 10^{-3} \text{ C}$, $1 \text{ }\mu\text{C (micro-coulomb)} = 10^{-6} \text{ C}$, $1 \text{ nC (nano-coulomb)} = 10^{-9} \text{ C}$.
  • Number of electrons in 1 Coulomb charge: Since $q = ne \implies n = \frac{q}{e} = \frac{1 \text{ C}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}} = \mathbf{6.25 \times 10^{18} \text{ electrons}}$.
8.2 Concept of Electric Current

Electric Current ($I$): Charges in motion constitute electric current. Current is defined as the rate of flow of charge through any cross-section of a conductor.

FORMULA: ELECTRIC CURRENT $$ I = \frac{Q}{t} = \frac{n e}{t} $$

Where $Q$ = net charge flowing (Coulombs), $t$ = time (seconds), $I$ = electric current (Amperes).

  • SI Unit: Ampere (symbol A) — named after the French physicist André-Marie Ampère.
  • Definition of 1 Ampere: The current through a conductor is said to be 1 Ampere if 1 Coulomb of charge flows across any cross-section of the conductor in 1 second ($1 \text{ A} = 1 \text{ C/s} = 1 \text{ C s}^{-1}$).
  • Smaller units: $1 \text{ mA (milli-ampere)} = 10^{-3} \text{ A}$, $1 \text{ }\mu\text{A (micro-ampere)} = 10^{-6} \text{ A}$.
  • Scalar Quantity: Electric current has a specified direction, but it is a scalar quantity because it follows algebraic addition rules rather than vector addition laws.

Charge Carriers in Different Media

  • In Metallic Conductors: Free electrons are the charge carriers. Positive metal ions remain fixed in the crystal lattice.
  • In Electrolytes: Both positive ions (cations) and negative ions (anions) move to carry current.
  • In Ionized Gases: Positive ions and free electrons act as charge carriers.

Direction of Electric Current

  • Conventional Current: Direction of flow of positive charge — from the positive (+ terminal) to negative (- terminal) of the battery externally.
  • Electron Flow: Negative electrons move from the negative (- terminal) to positive (+ terminal) of the battery externally (opposite to conventional current).

Ammeter: An instrument used to measure electric current. It is always connected in series in the circuit and possesses very low (ideally zero) resistance so as not to change the current flowing in the circuit.

8.3 Concept of Potential and Potential Difference

Electric Potential ($V$): The electric potential at a point in an electric field is defined as the amount of work done in bringing a unit positive test charge from infinity to that point.

FORMULA: ELECTRIC POTENTIAL $$ V = \frac{W}{Q} $$

Where $W$ = Work done (in Joules), $Q$ = charge (in Coulombs), $V$ = potential (in Volts).

Electric Potential Difference ($V_A - V_B$ or $V$): The potential difference between two points A and B in an electric circuit is equal to the work done in moving a unit positive charge from point B to point A.

FORMULA: POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE $$ V_A - V_B = \frac{W}{Q} $$
  • SI Unit: Volt (symbol V) — named after Alessandro Volta.
  • Definition of 1 Volt: The potential difference between two points is said to be 1 Volt if 1 Joule of work is done in moving 1 Coulomb of charge from one point to the other ($1 \text{ V} = 1 \text{ J/C} = 1 \text{ J C}^{-1}$).
  • Scalar Quantity: Work and charge are both scalars, hence potential difference is a scalar quantity.
  • Sign of Potential: The potential near a positive charge is positive (work is done against repulsive force), while near a negative charge it is negative (work is done by attractive force).

Voltmeter: An instrument used to measure potential difference across two points. It is always connected in parallel across the two points in a circuit and has a very high (ideally infinite) resistance so that it draws negligible current from the main circuit.

8.4 Concept of Electrical Resistance

Electrical Resistance ($R$): When current flows through a conductor, the wire offers an obstruction to the flow of electrons. This obstruction offered by the conductor to the flow of electric current is called its resistance.

Atomic Cause of Resistance

A metal conductor contains a large number of free electrons and fixed positive metal ions. In the absence of an electric field, free electrons move randomly in all directions. When a potential difference is applied across the ends of the conductor:

  1. An electric field is set up inside the conductor, causing free electrons to accelerate towards the positive terminal.
  2. As the electrons drift, they collide continuously with the fixed positive ions of the metal lattice.
  3. In each collision, electrons transfer kinetic energy to the positive ions (causing them to vibrate more vigorously, generating heat) and lose speed.
  4. After collision, electrons accelerate again, collide again, and drift with a constant average velocity called drift speed.
  5. These frequent collisions cause obstruction to electron flow, which manifests as resistance of the conductor.

8.5 Ohm's Law

Ohm's Law: Discovered by German physicist Georg Simon Ohm in 1826.

OHM'S LAW STATEMENT

"According to Ohm's law, the electric current flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference applied across its ends, provided physical conditions (such as temperature) remain constant."

$$ V \propto I \implies \frac{V}{I} = \text{Constant} = R $$ $$ \mathbf{V = I R} $$
  • Resistance ($R = V/I$): The ratio of potential difference across a conductor to the current flowing through it is constant for a given conductor at a constant temperature.
  • SI Unit of Resistance: Ohm (symbol $\Omega$, Omega).
  • Definition of 1 Ohm ($1 \ \Omega$): The resistance of a conductor is said to be 1 Ohm if a potential difference of 1 Volt across its ends causes a current of 1 Ampere to flow through it ($1 \ \Omega = \frac{1 \text{ V}}{1 \text{ A}} = 1 \text{ V A}^{-1}$).
  • Bigger units: $1 \text{ k}\Omega \text{ (kilo-ohm)} = 10^3 \ \Omega$, $1 \text{ M}\Omega \text{ (mega-ohm)} = 10^6 \ \Omega$.
  • Electrical Conductance ($G$): The reciprocal of resistance is called conductance ($G = \frac{1}{R}$). Its SI unit is $\Omega^{-1}$ or Siemens (S) or mho.

8.7 Ohmic and Non-Ohmic Resistors

Definitions & Characteristics

1. Ohmic Resistors (Linear Resistances):

  • Conductors which obey Ohm's law (i.e. the ratio $\frac{V}{I}$ remains constant for all values of $V$ and $I$).
  • The $V-I$ graph is a straight line passing through the origin.
  • The slope of the $V-I$ graph is constant at all temperatures.
  • Examples: All metallic conductors (e.g., silver, copper, aluminium, iron), nichrome wire, copper sulphate solution with copper electrodes, dilute sulphuric acid with platinum electrodes.

2. Non-Ohmic Resistors (Non-Linear Resistances):

  • Conductors which do not obey Ohm's law (i.e. the ratio $\frac{V}{I}$ is not constant and varies with voltage/current).
  • The $V-I$ graph is a curve (non-linear).
  • Dynamic Resistance ($r_d$): For a non-ohmic conductor, the resistance at any point is given by the slope of the tangent to the $V-I$ curve at that point: $r_d = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta I}$.
  • Examples: Light Emitting Diode (LED), Junction Diode, Transistor, Solar Cell, Filament lamp (bulb), Vacuum tubes.
Figure 8.5: $V-I$ graphs comparing Ohmic conductors (straight line) and Non-ohmic conductors (curved graphs).
Distinction Parameter Ohmic Resistor Non-Ohmic Resistor
Ohm's Law Compliance Obeys Ohm's law at all voltages ($\frac{V}{I} = \text{constant}$). Does not obey Ohm's law ($\frac{V}{I}$ varies with $V$ or $I$).

8.8 Factors Affecting Resistance of a Conductor

Four Key Factors

The electrical resistance $R$ of a conductor depends on four physical factors:

  1. Material of Conductor (Nature of Material):

    Different substances possess different concentrations of free electrons. Metals (like Silver, Copper, Aluminium) have very high free electron concentration ($\approx 10^{28} \text{ m}^{-3}$), offering very low resistance (good conductors). Insulators (like glass, rubber, wood) have negligible free electrons, offering extremely high resistance.

  2. Length of Conductor ($l$):

    Resistance is directly proportional to the length of the conductor. In a longer wire, electrons undergo a greater number of collisions with positive lattice ions while traveling from one end to the other.

    $$ R \propto l $$

    Example: If length of wire is doubled, its resistance gets doubled.

  3. Thickness / Area of Cross-Section of Conductor ($a$):

    Resistance is inversely proportional to the area of cross-section of the conductor ($a = \pi r^2$). A thick wire offers a larger area for electrons to flow, leading to fewer collisions per unit volume compared to a thin wire.

    $$ R \propto \frac{1}{a} \propto \frac{1}{r^2} $$

    Example: If radius $r$ of a wire is doubled, its cross-sectional area becomes 4 times, so its resistance becomes one-fourth ($\frac{1}{4}\text{th}$).

  4. Temperature of Conductor:
    • For Metallic Conductors: Resistance increases with an increase in temperature. As temperature rises, positive lattice ions vibrate more violently, increasing the frequency of collisions with drifting electrons.
    • For Semiconductors (Silicon, Germanium, Carbon): Resistance decreases with increase in temperature because more covalent bonds break, freeing additional charge carriers.
    • For Alloys (Manganin, Constantan): Resistance increases very slightly with temperature (negligible temperature coefficient).

8.9 Specific Resistance (or Resistivity)

Resistivity Formula & Definition

Combining the factors affecting resistance:

$$ R \propto l \quad \text{and} \quad R \propto \frac{1}{a} \implies R \propto \frac{l}{a} $$
SPECIFIC RESISTANCE FORMULA $$ R = \rho \frac{l}{a} \implies \mathbf{\rho = \frac{R a}{l} = \frac{R \pi r^2}{l}} $$

Where $\rho$ (Greek letter rho) is a constant of proportionality called Specific Resistance or Resistivity of the material of the wire.

Definition of Specific Resistance ($\rho$): If $l = 1 \text{ m}$ and $a = 1 \text{ m}^2$, then $\rho = R$. Thus, Specific Resistance of a material is defined as the resistance of a wire of that material having unit length ($1 \text{ m}$) and unit area of cross-section ($1 \text{ m}^2$).

  • SI Unit of Specific Resistance: Ohm-metre (symbol $\Omega \cdot \text{m}$ or $\Omega \text{ m}$). $$\text{Unit of } \rho = \frac{\text{Unit of } R \times \text{Unit of } a}{\text{Unit of } l} = \frac{\Omega \times \text{m}^2}{\text{m}} = \Omega \cdot \text{m}$$
  • CHARACTERISTIC PROPERTY: Specific resistance is a characteristic property of the material of a conductor. It depends ONLY on the material and temperature of the conductor. It is independent of the shape and dimensions (length and area) of the wire.
⚡ EXAM TRAP: STRETCHING OR DOUBLING A WIRE

Case 1: Wire is Stretched to $n$ times its length

When a wire of length $l$, area $a$, and resistance $R$ is stretched to $n$ times ($l' = n l$), its volume remains constant ($V = a l = a' l'$). Therefore, cross-sectional area becomes $a' = \frac{a}{n}$.

New Resistance: $R' = \rho \frac{l'}{a'} = \rho \frac{n l}{a/n} = n^2 \left(\rho \frac{l}{a}\right) = \mathbf{n^2 R}$.

Example: If length is doubled ($n=2$), resistance becomes $2^2 R = \mathbf{4 R}$. Resistivity $\rho$ remains UNCHANGED.

Case 2: Wire is Doubled on Itself

When a wire is folded in half (doubled on itself), its length is halved ($l' = l/2$) and area of cross-section is doubled ($a' = 2a$).

New Resistance: $R' = \rho \frac{l/2}{2a} = \frac{1}{4} \left(\rho \frac{l}{a}\right) = \mathbf{\frac{R}{4}}$.

Electrical Conductivity ($\sigma$): The reciprocal of specific resistance is called conductivity ($\sigma = \frac{1}{\rho} = \frac{l}{R a}$). Its SI unit is $\Omega^{-1} \text{m}^{-1}$ or Siemens metre$^{-1}$ ($\text{S m}^{-1}$).

8.11 Superconductors

Zero Resistance State

Superconductor: A substance whose resistance decreases tremendously with decrease in temperature and becomes exactly zero at a specific low temperature (near absolute zero). This state is called superconductivity.

  • At zero resistance, electric current once set up in a closed superconducting loop continues to flow indefinitely without any potential difference!
  • Examples & Transition Temperatures ($T_c$):
    • Mercury ($\text{Hg}$) below $4.2 \text{ K}$ (discovered by Heike Kamerlingh Onnes in 1911).
    • Lead ($\text{Pb}$) below $7.25 \text{ K}$.
    • Niobium ($\text{Nb}$) below $9.2 \text{ K}$.

(B) Electro-Motive Force (E.M.F.), Terminal Voltage and Internal Resistance of a Cell; Combination of Resistors

8.12 Electro-Motive Force (E.M.F.) of a Cell

Cell Action & E.M.F.

Electric Cell: A electrochemical device that maintains a continuous potential difference between two conductors (electrodes) by converting chemical energy into electrical energy.

Chemical Action in a Simple Voltaic Cell:

When two electrodes (e.g. Copper and Zinc) are immersed in dilute Sulphuric Acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$), acid molecules dissociate into $\text{H}^+$ and $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ ions. Chemical reactions cause Zinc to acquire a negative potential (Cathode) and Copper to acquire a positive potential (Anode). This creates a potential difference between the terminals.

DEFINITION: E.M.F. ($\mathcal{E}$)

"When no current is drawn from a cell (i.e. in an open circuit), the potential difference between the two terminals of the cell is called its Electro-Motive Force (e.m.f.)."

$$ \mathcal{E} = \frac{W}{q} $$

Alternatively: E.M.F. is defined as the total work done per unit positive charge in carrying it around the complete circuit (both outside the cell in the external circuit and inside the cell through the electrolyte).

  • SI Unit of E.M.F.: Volt (V) ($1 \text{ V} = 1 \text{ J C}^{-1}$).
  • Factors Affecting E.M.F. of a Cell:
    1. Nature of electrodes.
    2. Nature and concentration of electrolyte used.
  • INDEPENDENT FACTORS: E.M.F. does NOT depend on: (a) shape or size of electrodes, (b) distance between electrodes, or (c) quantity of electrolyte in the vessel.
  • Examples of Cell E.M.F.: Simple Voltaic Cell $= 1.08 \text{ V}$, Daniel Cell $= 1.08 \text{ V}$, Leclanché Cell / Dry Cell $= 1.5 \text{ V}$, Lead accumulator $= 2.0 \text{ V}$.

8.13 Terminal Voltage ($V$) and Voltage Drop ($v$)

Closed Circuit Dynamics

When the cell is connected in a closed circuit containing an external load resistor $R$, current $I$ flows from positive electrode to negative electrode in the external circuit.

Terminal Voltage ($V$): The potential difference between the two electrodes of a cell when current is being drawn from it (i.e. in a closed circuit).

$$ V = \frac{W'}{q} $$

Where $W'$ is the work done in carrying a test charge $q$ through the external circuit only.

Voltage Drop inside Cell ($v$): The work done per unit charge in carrying the charge through the electrolyte inside the cell against its internal resistance $r$.

$$ v = \frac{w}{q} = I r $$
CONSERVATION OF ENERGY RELATION

Total Work Done = External Work + Internal Work

$$ W = W' + w \implies \frac{W}{q} = \frac{W'}{q} + \frac{w}{q} $$ $$ \mathbf{\mathcal{E} = V + v \implies V = \mathcal{E} - v = \mathcal{E} - I r} $$

Conclusion: When current is drawn from a cell, terminal voltage $V$ is ALWAYS LESS than its e.m.f. $\mathcal{E}$ by an amount equal to the voltage drop $v = Ir$ inside the cell.

Figure 8.7: Cell in Open Circuit ($V = \mathcal{E}$) vs Closed Circuit ($V = \mathcal{E} - Ir$).
Distinction Parameter Electro-Motive Force (E.M.F., $\mathcal{E}$) Terminal Voltage ($V$)
Circuit Condition Measured when no current is drawn from cell (Open Circuit). Measured when current is drawn from cell (Closed Circuit).
Work Done Scope Work done in moving unit charge in the complete circuit (inside + outside). Work done in moving unit charge in the external circuit only.
Magnitude Relationship $\mathcal{E}$ is constant for a given cell. $\mathcal{E} > V$ when cell is discharging. $V$ depends on current drawn. $V < \mathcal{E}$ during discharge ($V = \mathcal{E} - Ir$).

8.14 Internal Resistance of a Cell ($r$)

Definition & Factors

Internal Resistance ($r$): The obstruction offered by the electrolyte inside the cell to the flow of electric current (ions) is called the internal resistance of the cell.

Factors Affecting Internal Resistance ($r$)

  1. Surface Area of Electrodes ($a$): Internal resistance is inversely proportional to the surface area of electrodes submerged in electrolyte ($r \propto \frac{1}{a}$). Larger electrodes $\implies$ lower $r$.
  2. Distance between Electrodes ($d$): Internal resistance is directly proportional to the distance between electrodes ($r \propto d$). Greater separation $\implies$ higher $r$.
  3. Concentration of Electrolyte ($c$): Internal resistance increases with higher concentration of electrolyte.
  4. Temperature of Electrolyte ($T$): Internal resistance decreases with an increase in temperature of the electrolyte (ionic mobility increases at higher temperature).
FORMULAS FOR CIRCUIT WITH INTERNAL RESISTANCE

Consider a cell of e.m.f. $\mathcal{E}$ and internal resistance $r$ connected across an external resistor $R$:

  1. Total Resistance of Circuit = $R + r$
  2. Current drawn from cell: $$\mathbf{I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R + r}}$$
  3. Terminal Voltage across Load $R$: $$\mathbf{V = I R = \left(\frac{\mathcal{E}}{R + r}\right) R}$$
  4. Voltage Drop inside Cell: $$\mathbf{v = I r = \mathcal{E} - V}$$
  5. Formula to calculate Internal Resistance ($r$): $$\frac{\mathcal{E}}{V} = \frac{I(R+r)}{IR} = \frac{R+r}{R} = 1 + \frac{r}{R}$$ $$\mathbf{r = \left(\frac{\mathcal{E}}{V} - 1\right) R = \frac{\mathcal{E} - V}{I}}$$

8.15 Combination of Resistors

In electronic circuits, resistors are combined in two fundamental ways to obtain desired effective resistances:

1. Series Combination of Resistors

Series Rules & Derivation

Resistors are said to be connected in series if they are joined end-to-end such that the same electric current flows through each resistor sequentially.

Figure 8.9: Three resistors $R_1, R_2, R_3$ connected in series combination.

Key Properties of Series Combination:

  1. The current $I$ is SAME through every resistor.
  2. The total potential difference $V$ across the combination is equal to the sum of potential drops across individual resistors: $$ V = V_1 + V_2 + V_3 $$
  3. By Ohm's law, $V_1 = I R_1$, $V_2 = I R_2$, $V_3 = I R_3$.
  4. If $R_s$ is the equivalent resistance of the series combination ($V = I R_s$): $$ I R_s = I R_1 + I R_2 + I R_3 \implies \mathbf{R_s = R_1 + R_2 + R_3} $$
SERIES EQUIVALENT FORMULAS $$ \mathbf{R_s = R_1 + R_2 + R_3 + \dots + R_n} $$

For $n$ equal resistors of resistance $R$ connected in series: $\mathbf{R_s = n R}$.

Voltage Divider Rule: For two resistors in series across total voltage $V$: $$ V_1 = V \left(\frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2}\right), \quad V_2 = V \left(\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\right) $$

2. Parallel Combination of Resistors

Parallel Rules & Derivation

Resistors are said to be connected in parallel if one end of each resistor is connected to one common point and the other ends are connected to another common point, such that the potential difference across each resistor is the same.

Figure 8.10: Three resistors $R_1, R_2, R_3$ connected in parallel combination.

Key Properties of Parallel Combination:

  1. The Potential Difference $V$ is SAME across each resistor.
  2. The main current $I$ divides into branch currents $I_1, I_2, I_3$: $$ I = I_1 + I_2 + I_3 $$
  3. By Ohm's law, $I_1 = \frac{V}{R_1}$, $I_2 = \frac{V}{R_2}$, $I_3 = \frac{V}{R_3}$.
  4. If $R_p$ is the equivalent resistance of the parallel combination ($I = \frac{V}{R_p}$): $$ \frac{V}{R_p} = \frac{V}{R_1} + \frac{V}{R_2} + \frac{V}{R_3} \implies \mathbf{\frac{1}{R_p} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3}} $$
PARALLEL EQUIVALENT FORMULAS $$ \mathbf{\frac{1}{R_p} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{R_n}} $$

For TWO Resistors in Parallel: $$ \mathbf{R_p = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}} $$

For $n$ equal resistors of resistance $R$ in parallel: $\mathbf{R_p = \frac{R}{n}}$.

Current Divider Rule: For two resistors in parallel carrying main current $I$: $$ I_1 = I \left(\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\right), \quad I_2 = I \left(\frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2}\right) $$

Q3. A cell of e.m.f. 1.5 V and internal resistance $1 \ \Omega$ is connected across two resistors of $4 \ \Omega$ and $20 \ \Omega$ connected in series. Calculate: (i) total resistance of circuit, (ii) current in circuit, (iii) terminal voltage of cell, and (iv) voltage drop inside cell.

Given: $\mathcal{E} = 1.5 \text{ V}$, $r = 1 \ \Omega$, $R_1 = 4 \ \Omega$, $R_2 = 20 \ \Omega$.
(i) External Resistance ($R$): $R = R_1 + R_2 = 4 + 20 = 24 \ \Omega$.
Total Circuit Resistance: $R_{\text{total}} = R + r = 24 + 1 = \mathbf{25 \ \Omega}$.
(ii) Circuit Current ($I$): $I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R_{\text{total}}} = \frac{1.5}{25} = \mathbf{0.06 \text{ A}}$.
(iii) Terminal Voltage ($V$): $V = I R = 0.06 \times 24 = \mathbf{1.44 \text{ V}}$.
(iv) Voltage Drop ($v$): $v = I r = 0.06 \times 1 = \mathbf{0.06 \text{ V}}$ (or $v = \mathcal{E} - V = 1.5 - 1.44 = 0.06 \text{ V}$).
✍ IN-TEXT NUMERICAL 8.4

Q4. Three resistors of $2 \ \Omega, 3 \ \Omega$, and $6 \ \Omega$ are connected in parallel. This combination is connected to a 6 V battery of negligible internal resistance. Calculate: (a) equivalent resistance of combination, (b) total current drawn from battery, and (c) current through each resistor.

Given: $R_1 = 2 \ \Omega, R_2 = 3 \ \Omega, R_3 = 6 \ \Omega$, $V = 6 \text{ V}$.
(a) Equivalent Resistance ($R_p$): $$\frac{1}{R_p} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{3 + 2 + 1}{6} = \frac{6}{6} = 1 \implies \mathbf{R_p = 1 \ \Omega}.$$
(b) Total Current ($I$): $I = \frac{V}{R_p} = \frac{6 \text{ V}}{1 \ \Omega} = \mathbf{6.0 \text{ A}}$.
(c) Branch Currents:
  • $I_1 = \frac{V}{R_1} = \frac{6}{2} = \mathbf{3.0 \text{ A}}$
  • $I_2 = \frac{V}{R_2} = \frac{6}{3} = \mathbf{2.0 \text{ A}}$
  • $I_3 = \frac{V}{R_3} = \frac{6}{6} = \mathbf{1.0 \text{ A}}$
Check: Total $I = 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 \text{ A}$. Verified!

(C) Electrical Energy and Power

8.16 Electrical Energy ($W$)

Concept & Energy Transformations

Electrical Energy: The work done by a source of electricity (such as a cell or generator) in maintaining an electric current in a circuit for a given time is called electrical energy.

Energy Transformations in Everyday Appliances

  • Electric Heater / Geyser / Iron: Electrical Energy $\longrightarrow$ Heat Energy.
  • Electric Bulb / Fluorescent Tube: Electrical Energy $\longrightarrow$ Light Energy + Heat Energy.
  • Electric Motor / Fan: Electrical Energy $\longrightarrow$ Mechanical Energy.
  • Loudspeaker: Electrical Energy $\longrightarrow$ Sound Energy.
  • Electrolysis (Battery Charging): Electrical Energy $\longrightarrow$ Chemical Energy.
  • Electromagnet: Electrical Energy $\longrightarrow$ Magnetic Energy.

8.17 Measurement of Electrical Energy

FORMULAS FOR ELECTRICAL ENERGY

If a current $I$ flows through a conductor of resistance $R$ under a potential difference $V$ for time $t$ seconds, the work done (electrical energy supplied) $W$ is given by:

$$ W = Q \times V $$

Since $Q = I \times t$:

$$ \mathbf{W = V I t} $$

Using Ohm's law ($V = IR$ or $I = V/R$):

$$ \mathbf{W = I^2 R t = \frac{V^2}{R} t} $$

SI Unit of Electrical Energy: Joule (symbol J).

8.18 Electrical Power ($P$)

Power Definition & Expressions

Electrical Power ($P$): The rate at which electrical energy is consumed or dissipated in an electrical circuit is called electrical power.

FORMULAS FOR ELECTRICAL POWER $$ P = \frac{\text{Electrical Energy } W}{\text{Time } t} $$ $$ \mathbf{P = V I = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R}} $$
  • SI Unit of Power: Watt (symbol W) — named after James Watt.
  • Definition of 1 Watt: The electric power of a circuit is 1 Watt if 1 Joule of electrical energy is consumed per second ($1 \text{ W} = 1 \text{ J s}^{-1} = 1 \text{ V A}$).
  • Bigger Units of Power:
    • $1 \text{ kW (kilowatt)} = 10^3 \text{ W} = 1000 \text{ W}$
    • $1 \text{ MW (megawatt)} = 10^6 \text{ W}$
    • $1 \text{ GW (gigawatt)} = 10^9 \text{ W}$

8.19 Commercial Unit of Electrical Energy

Commercial Unit: kWh

The SI unit Joule is a very small quantity of energy, making it inconvenient for commercial electricity billing. Therefore, electricity boards use a bigger unit called Kilowatt-hour (kWh), also known as the Board of Trade Unit (B.O.T.u).

DEFINITION: 1 KILOWATT-HOUR

"One kilowatt-hour (1 kWh) is the electrical energy consumed by an appliance of power 1 kilowatt (1000 W) when it is used for 1 hour."

$$ 1 \text{ kWh} = 1 \text{ kW} \times 1 \text{ h} $$

Derivation of Relation between kWh and Joule

$$ 1 \text{ kWh} = 1 \text{ kilowatt} \times 1 \text{ hour} $$ $$ 1 \text{ kWh} = 1000 \text{ Watts} \times 3600 \text{ seconds} $$ $$ 1 \text{ kWh} = 1000 \text{ J s}^{-1} \times 3600 \text{ s} $$
$$ \mathbf{1 \text{ kWh} = 3.6 \times 10^6 \text{ Joules (J)} = 3.6 \text{ MJ}} $$

Watt-hour ($1 \text{ Wh}$): $1 \text{ Wh} = 1 \text{ W} \times 3600 \text{ s} = \mathbf{3600 \text{ J}}$. Thus $1 \text{ kWh} = 1000 \text{ Wh}$.

8.20 Power Rating of Common Electrical Appliances

Appliance Ratings & Calculations

Every electrical appliance bears a nameplate mentioning its power and voltage rating. For example, an electric bulb is rated $100 \text{ W} - 220 \text{ V}$.

Meaning of "$100 \text{ W} - 220 \text{ V}$": It means that if the bulb is connected across a $220 \text{ V}$ supply line, it will consume $100 \text{ Joules}$ of electrical energy per second (converted into light and heat energy).

Two Critical Quantities Calculated from Appliance Rating:

  1. Resistance of Appliance Filament ($R$):
    $$ \mathbf{R = \frac{V_{\text{rated}}^2}{P_{\text{rated}}}} $$

    Note: The resistance of an appliance depends ONLY on its rating and is independent of the operating voltage in the circuit!

  2. Safe Current Limit ($I_{\text{safe}}$):
    $$ \mathbf{I_{\text{safe}} = \frac{P_{\text{rated}}}{V_{\text{rated}}}} $$

    If current exceeding $I_{\text{safe}}$ flows through the appliance, it will overheat and burn out/fuse.

8.21 Household Consumption of Electrical Energy & Cost Calculation

Billing Formula

Every house has an electric meter provided by the electricity board to record total electrical energy consumed in kWh (units).

ENERGY CONSUMPTION FORMULA $$ \text{Electrical Energy (in kWh)} = \frac{\text{Power (in Watts)} \times \text{Time (in hours)}}{1000} $$ $$ \mathbf{\text{Energy (in kWh)} = \frac{V (\text{volts}) \times I (\text{amperes}) \times t (\text{hours})}{1000}} $$
ELECTRICITY COST FORMULA $$ \mathbf{\text{Total Cost of Electricity} = \text{Total Energy (in kWh)} \times \text{Rate per kWh (in ₹)}} $$

8.22 Heating Effect of Current (Joule's Law of Heating)

Joule's Law

When an electric current passes through a high resistance wire (like Nichrome), the electrical energy is converted into heat energy. This is called the heating effect of electric current.

JOULE'S LAW OF HEATING

The heat $H$ produced in a conductor is directly proportional to:

  1. The square of current passing through it ($H \propto I^2$).
  2. The resistance of the conductor ($H \propto R$).
  3. The time for which current flows ($H \propto t$).
$$ \mathbf{H = I^2 R t \quad \text{(in Joules)}} $$

In calories ($1 \text{ cal} = 4.18 \text{ J} \approx 4.2 \text{ J}$): $$ \mathbf{H = \frac{I^2 R t}{4.18} \approx 0.24 \ I^2 R t \quad \text{(in calories)}} $$

Q5. An electric bulb is rated '100 W, 220 V'. Calculate: (i) the resistance of its filament when glowing, and (ii) the safe current that can be passed through it. What power will it consume if connected to a 110 V line?

Given: Rating $P = 100 \text{ W}$, $V = 220 \text{ V}$.
(i) Resistance ($R$): $R = \frac{V^2}{P} = \frac{220^2}{100} = \frac{48400}{100} = \mathbf{484 \ \Omega}$.
(ii) Safe Current ($I_{\text{safe}}$): $I = \frac{P}{V} = \frac{100}{220} = \mathbf{0.454 \text{ A} \approx 0.45 \text{ A}}$.
Power consumed at 110 V ($V' = 110 \text{ V}$): $$P' = \frac{V'^2}{R} = \frac{110^2}{484} = \frac{12100}{484} = \mathbf{25 \text{ W}}.$$ Note: Power consumed becomes $1/4\text{th}$ when voltage is halved!

Q6. In a house, 4 bulbs of 100 W each are used for 6 hours daily and an electric iron of 750 W is used for 2 hours daily. Calculate: (i) total electrical energy consumed in a month of 30 days in kWh, and (ii) the cost of electricity if rate is ₹ 4.50 per unit.

Step 1 — Daily Energy for Bulbs: $E_1 = 4 \times 100 \text{ W} \times 6 \text{ h} = 2400 \text{ Wh} = 2.4 \text{ kWh}$.
Step 2 — Daily Energy for Iron: $E_2 = 1 \times 750 \text{ W} \times 2 \text{ h} = 1500 \text{ Wh} = 1.5 \text{ kWh}$.
Total Daily Energy: $E_{\text{daily}} = 2.4 + 1.5 = 3.9 \text{ kWh}$.
(i) Total Monthly Energy (30 days): $E_{\text{monthly}} = 3.9 \text{ kWh/day} \times 30 \text{ days} = \mathbf{117 \text{ kWh (units)}}$.
(ii) Total Electricity Bill: $\text{Cost} = 117 \text{ units} \times \text{₹ } 4.50 = \mathbf{\text{₹ } 526.50}$.
