Chapter 1: The First War of Independence (1857)

Chapter Overview

The Revolt of 1857 was a monumental watershed in Indian history. Known as the First War of Independence, it was the culmination of a century of discontent against the oppressive political, economic, socio-religious, and military policies of the British East India Company. It ended Company rule and brought India directly under the British Crown.

1. Causes of the Revolt of 1857

A. Political Causes

Political Grievances
1. Policy of Doctrine of Lapse

Introduced by Lord Dalhousie. If an Indian ruler died without a natural male heir, his kingdom was annexed to the British Empire; adopted sons were refused succession rights. Principalities annexed included Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur, Sambalpur, and Jaitpur. Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi was refused permission to adopt a son after King Gangadhar Rao died.

2. Annexation of Awadh (1856)

Lord Dalhousie annexed Awadh on the false pretext of "alleged misgovernance" by Nawab Wajid Ali Shah. This created anger across Awadh, especially among the 75,000 sepoys in the Bengal Army who belonged to Awadh and lost their privileges.

3. Disrespect to Mughal Emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar

In 1849, Dalhousie announced that Bahadur Shah's successors would have to vacate the historic Red Fort and live near Qutub Minar. Lord Canning announced in 1856 that after Bahadur Shah's death, his successors would lose the title of 'King' (Emperor) and be called mere 'Princes'.

4. Treatment of Nana Saheb

Nana Saheb, adopted son of the deposed Peshwa Baji Rao II, was refused the annual pension of 8 lakh rupees after Baji Rao II's death. He was forced to live at Bithoor near Kanpur.

5. Absentee Sovereignty

Unlike previous foreign rulers who settled in India, India was ruled from distant London. Revenue collected from India was drained to Britain without benefit to local citizens.

B. Socio-Religious Causes

Social Discontent
  • Interference with Social Customs: Legislation such as the Abolition of Sati Act (1829), Female Infanticide Act, and Hindu Widow Remarriage Act (1856), though progressive, were viewed by orthodox Indians as unwanted British interference in Hindu religious laws.
  • Activities of Christian Missionaries: Missionaries openly criticized Hindu and Muslim scriptures in schools, marketplaces, and prisons. Converts to Christianity received government jobs and promotions.
  • Religious Disabilities Act (1850): Amended Hindu inheritance law so that converting to another religion did not disqualify a person from inheriting ancestral property.
  • Taxation of Temple and Mosque Lands: Exemption of tax on lands held by temples and mosques was revoked, hurting priests and religious institutions.
  • Fear of Westernization: Introduction of Western education, railways, and telegraphs created widespread panic that the British planned to convert Indians wholesale to Christianity.

C. Economic Causes

Economic Exploitation
  • Ruin of Cottage Industries & Handicrafts: Unfree British commercial policy turned India into a supplier of raw materials (cotton, silk) and a market for British manufactured goods. High import tariffs in Britain destroyed Indian handloom exports.
  • Impoverishment of Peasants: Ruthless land revenue systems (Permanent Settlement, Ryotwari, Mahalwari) subjected peasants to exorbitant taxes. Failure of crops led to land confiscation by moneylenders.
  • Annexation of Inam Lands: Lord Dalhousie set up the Inam Commission (1852) in Bombay, which confiscated 20,000 estates whose owners failed to produce land title deeds.
  • Drain of Wealth: Huge sums of money were drained to England through home charges, pensions of British officers, and trade profits.

D. Military Causes

Grievances of Indian Sepoys
  • Pay Discrimination An Indian sepoy was paid far less than a British soldier of equal rank (£7 per month vs high British allowances). No Indian sepoy could rise above the rank of Subedar.
  • General Service Enlistment Act (1856) Passed by Lord Canning. Made it compulsory for new recruits to serve anywhere, including overseas. Crossing the sea (Kalapani) meant loss of caste for high-caste Hindu sepoys.
  • Withdrawal of Foreign Batta Sepoys were denied extra allowance (Batta) when serving in newly annexed territories like Sindh and Punjab.
  • Numerical Disparity Indian sepoys far outnumbered British troops (over 200,000 Indians to 40,000 British), giving sepoys confidence in revolting.

E. Immediate Cause: The Greased Cartridges Controversy

Trigger Point

In 1856, the British introduced the new Enfield Rifle. The paper cartridge used in this rifle had to be bitten off before loading. Rumors spread that the grease used on cartridges was made from cow and pig fat. This outraged both Hindu sepoys (to whom cow is sacred) and Muslim sepoys (to whom pig is haram).

On 29 March 1857 at Barrackpore, Mangal Pandey revolted and fired at his British officer. He was executed. On 10 May 1857, sepoys at Meerut mutinied, released imprisoned comrades, killed British officers, and marched to Delhi, proclaiming Bahadur Shah Zafar as the Emperor of India.

3. Consequences and Results of the Revolt

Major Administrative & Political Changes
1. End of East India Company Rule & Act of 1858

The Government of India Act 1858 transferred administrative authority from the East India Company to the British Crown. The Board of Control and Court of Directors were abolished. A new cabinet post — Secretary of State for India (assisted by a 15-member Council) — was established in London. The Governor-General was given the additional title of Viceroy (Lord Canning became the first Viceroy).

2. Queen Victoria's Proclamation (1 November 1858)

Read by Lord Canning at a grand Durbar in Allahabad. Promised:

  • No further territorial expansion in India.
  • Non-interference in Indian religious beliefs and ancient customs.
  • Equal treatment for all subjects, irrespective of race or creed, in government employment.
  • General pardon to all rebels except those guilty of murdering British subjects.

3. Reorganization of the Army (Peel Commission Report)

To prevent future mutinies:

  • The proportion of European troops was increased (1:2 ratio in Bengal, 1:5 in Madras/Bombay).
  • Artillery, armaments, and crucial military hardware were kept strictly in European hands.
  • Troops were recruited based on 'Divide and Rule' policy, categorizing groups into 'Martial Races' (Sikhs, Gurkhas, Pathans who stayed loyal) and 'Non-Martial Races'.

4. Policy towards Princely States

The Doctrine of Lapse was abolished. Indian princes were granted the right to adopt heirs. In return, princes acknowledged Crown Paramountcy and acted as loyal breakwaters against future mass rebellions.

5. Growth of Indian Nationalism

Though suppressed, the heroic struggle of Rani Lakshmibai, Kunwar Singh, and Nana Saheb became a permanent source of inspiration for future freedom fighters. It laid the foundation for modern Indian nationalism.
